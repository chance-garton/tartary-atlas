Birch-bark tents and reindeer in the Siberian forests

Editorial contextThe note on the Kyrgyz lands of the upper Yenisei describes a forest people whose poor lived in tents of birch bark, loaded their goods on white deer (reindeer), drank deer milk, gathered pine nuts and roots, and hunted in winter on 'wooden horses' (skis). Nearby Qianzhou had a few thousand households of Mongols and Uighurs, and workshops of Chinese craftsmen moved there early in the dynasty.

The poor, who have no fixed property, all make their tents of birch bark and load their baggage on white deer. They take deer's milk, gather pine nuts and dig up the roots of mountain lilies and peonies for food. In winter they also ride 'wooden horses' (skis) to hunt... [In Qianzhou] the inhabitants, several thousand households, are all Mongols and Huihe (Uighurs); there are several workshops of craftsmen, who are Han Chinese moved there at the start of the dynasty.

A cliff of unknown writing on the Yenisei

Editorial contextBefore the great rapids of the Yenisei, Spafarii was told, a stone cliff carries carved writing in an unknown script, with crosses among the letters and carved men holding maces, and 'many other such works'. Nobody knew what it said or who made it. The upper Yenisei is famous today for its rock art and early Turkic runic inscriptions, but Spafarii could only report the mystery.

And before one reaches the great rapids there is a place, a stone cliff along the Yenisei. On that cliff there is carved in the stone some unknown writing, and among the writing crosses are carved too, and men are carved as well, and in their hands they hold maces, and many other such works. … And no one knows what is written or by whom.

Original wording

BuildingsSouthern Siberia (Yenisei, Irtysh and Tobol steppes)1711 to 1721

The 'pyramids' of Tartar kings on old maps

Editorial contextStrahlenberg describes the burial mounds of southern Siberia: some are earth heaps as high as houses, lying so thick that they look like a ridge of hills; others are ringed with rough or squared stones, and some are built entirely of stone. He explains that the 'pyramids' drawn on old European maps of Great Tartary, labelled 'Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings', must mean these tombs, which are not really pyramids.

Some are only raised up of Earth, as high as Houses, … that, at a Distance, they appear like a Ridge of Hills. … In some Places these Tombs are entirely built of Stone. Hence we find in the ancient Maps of Tartary the Greater, a Number of Pyramids, with these Words, in Latin; viz. The Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings,

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

'Om-om': the sound of bells beyond the Pesida

Editorial contextTungus sent east by the Russians met strangers who, by signs, said that thunder and a great noise of people were often heard across a river they called Pesida, repeating 'om-om'. The Muscovites decided this must be the sound of bells, and Massa guessed it was the edge of Cathay. A lovely example of how rumour pushed the map of 'Tartary' eastward.

only by signs and gestures, they made out so much that thunder was often heard on the other side by their saying: om-om; and also a dreadful noise and rumour of human beings; they pointed to the river, saying: Pesida … From the words om-om, the Muscovites afterwards came to the conclusion, that it must be the sound of bells

Isaac Massa's description of Siberia, as printed by Hessel Gerritsz in 1612 (English translation by F. J. Millard in the 1878 facsimile edition)

Alak-tsin, a town where every pot is silver

Editorial contextIn his chapter on the Tatar people, the Khivan khan and historian Abu'l-Ghazi describes a great town and many villages on a 'Bitter Sea' fed by the Uighur river (Klaproth identifies it as the Yenisei). The people shift their dwellings often, own only dapple-grey horses, mine silver nearby and cook in silver pots. Klaproth gives his own new translation because the old French version garbled the passage.

On the shores of this sea stands a great town and many villages. The inhabitants often change their dwellings; they own numerous herds and a considerable number of horses. … All are dapple-grey … The name of the town is Alak-tsin; near it there are silver mines. Among this people all the cooking pots and bowls are of silver.

Original wording

Chez ce peuple toutes les marmites et les jattes sont d'argent.

Abu'l-Ghazi Bahadur Khan (Genealogy of the Turks, 1660s), translated by Julius Klaproth

Mammoths that burrow underground and die in the light

Editorial contextThe Ides section reports mammoth tusks and bones thawing out of riverbanks, and a member of the embassy who found a head with half-rotten flesh. The Yakuts, Tungus and Ostyaks say the mammoth lives underground, making the earth rise and sink as it moves, and dies as soon as it sees daylight. The Russians of Siberia think it was an elephant-like beast buried by Noah's Flood.

The Yakuts, Tungus and Ostyaks claim that they never come out of the bosom of the earth, under which they go this way and that. They even say that one often sees the earth rise and sink when they are moving … They assure us that they die as soon as they see the light

A town of pied horses and silver plate on the Yenisei

Editorial contextAnother branch of the Tatars, Abu'l Ghazi says, settled on the river Ikar (glossed in the margin as the Yenisei). Near its mouth stood a great town called Alachzin ('pied'), ruling smaller ones, whose people rode only piebald horses and, thanks to local silver mines, used nothing but silver plate. He adds that the Uzbeks had a tradition of such a nation but could not say where it was.

Near its Mouth there is a great Town, which commands several small ones thereabouts, call'd Alachzin, that is to say, pied; because the Inhabitants of that Town have none but pied Horses … There are rich silver Mines in the Neighbourhood of that Town; and 'tis for that reason that nothing is seen there but silver Plate.

Abu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

BuildingsMinusinsk, Krasnoyarsk Krai1829

Kurgan graves of 'a race now extinct'

Editorial contextAt Krasnoyarsk Erman studied a collection of bronze objects dug from the kurgans (burial mounds) of the Minusinsk district, including round mirrors decorated with animals. The local Tatars told him the mounds belonged to 'a race now extinct, and totally different from theirs'. Erman noted that similar mirrors were still used in Buryat Buddhist rites, and wondered whether the builders were really so different from living Siberians.

The most important consideration, however, is, that these mirrors are found in graves which, as the present Tatar inhabitants of the circle maintain, belong to a race now extinct, and totally different from theirs.

Adolph Erman (translated by W. D. Cooley, 1848)
See the original page (p. 139)

About Tatars of the Minusinsk basin (Khakas); unknown ancient builders

Keep in mind

Erman saw the objects in a private collection, not the graves; the 'extinct race' is local Tatar belief. Modern archaeology links most Minusinsk bronzes to Bronze and Iron Age cultures (such as Tagar) long before the Turkic-speaking Khakas.

A Kachin shaman in ribbons and printed cotton

Editorial contextPallas saw a young Kachin shaman perform at the Karysh mine. The man was said to have been mad, or to have played mad, for years before taking up the craft, and still sometimes 'witched' all night until he collapsed. As a beginner he had no drum, only a paddle-shaped rattle stick; a shaman earned the right to a drum by sacrificing a horse and stretching its hide over it.

I saw the costume of a young Kachin sorcerer, who for many years before taking up the trade had been mad or had pretended to be mad, at Karysh… He performed his tricks without a cap and wore only brightly embroidered leather stockings and on his body a tight, very dirty smock of brightly printed cotton cloth (kitaika).

A Yuan official teaches pottery, metalwork and boats

Editorial contextThe same note says that before the Yuan official Liu Haoli arrived, the local peoples made cups from willow wood and hollowed out logs as troughs to cross rivers, and did not know how to cast farm tools. Liu reported this to the court, which sent craftsmen to teach pottery, metal-casting and boat-building. It is a Chinese state view of 'civilising' the Siberian frontier.

Before this, the custom of these several tribes was to make their cups and dishes of willow and to hollow out logs as troughs for crossing water; they did not know how to cast farm tools. Haoli reported this to the court, which then sent craftsmen to teach them pottery, metal-casting and boats, and the local people found it convenient.

Why 'Tungus' may be a Tatar word for pig-keepers

Editorial contextIn a footnote Bichurin says the word Tungus does not exist in Manchu and no ancient Tungusic ruling house used it. He guesses the Russians took it from the Tatars of the Yenisei, since the Chinese histories say the Tungus peoples bred pigs, still used in shamanic sacrifice, and the Tatar word for pig is 'donguz'. It shows how names for Siberian peoples passed through Tatar speech into Russian.

The word Tungus does not exist in the Manchu language, and none of the ancient Tungusic ruling houses was known by this name. Probably the Russians, when they first reached the Yenisei, borrowed this name from the Yenisei Tatars. … and in the Tatar language a pig is called donguz.

Working translation

Original wording

Слова: Тунгусъ, нѣтъ въ маньчжурскомъ языкѣ: да и ни одинъ изъ древнихъ тунгузскихъ владѣтельныхъ Домовъ не былъ извѣстенъ подъ симъ названіемъ. Вѣроятно, что Русскіе, первоначально достигнувъ Енисея, заимствовали сіе названіе отъ енисейскихъ Татаръ. … а свинья на татарскомъ языкѣ называется Донгусъ.

Iakinf (N. Ya. Bichurin), footnote, 1851

Editorial contextAt Krasnoyarsk the governor showed Holman his collection of 'Tartar' dresses, weapons and armour, along with objects dug up around the province: metal vases that Holman guessed once held the ashes of great chiefs, copper and stone hatchets, knives and arrowheads. These are the kind of finds that came from the kurgans (burial mounds) of the Yenisei and Minusinsk region.

The governor, on the Tuesday, most obligingly submitted to my inspection his cabinet of curiosities, comprising a large collection of Tartar dresses, weapons, and armour, as also many curious remains that had been dug up in different parts of his government; amongst the rest, metal vases, which had probably been the depositaries of the ashes of the great and powerful of their day

James Holman (1825)

Editorial contextIn the Krasnoyarsk district, Spafarii says, the Russian villages and their thousand or so servicemen were always in danger from the Kirghiz: only about a thousand men, but very warlike, 'and their language and faith are Tatar'. Elsewhere he lists the Kirghiz among the 'Tatars' living at the head of the Ket. In Siberian Russian usage 'Tatar' reached well beyond Kuchum's old realm.

only it is always dangerous from the Kirghiz: there are about 1000 of them, but they are very warlike, and their language and faith are Tatar.

The nameYenisei (Khakassia)1895 (about 6th to 16th century)

'Abakan Tatars': the Kirghiz' descendants in 1895

Editorial contextRadloff notes that the Yenisei inscriptions were left mostly by the Kirghiz, and that their descendants still form a large part of the Turkic population there, 'the Abakan Tatars and Soyots'. In the nineteenth century Russian usage called many Siberian Turkic groups 'Tatars'. The same people who carved runic stones in the early Middle Ages were called Tatars by the time Radloff wrote.

When the Russians came to know them in the sixteenth century in the southern part of the Yenisei province, the Kirghiz were complete Turks, and even now descendants of the Kirghiz form a large part of the Turkic population of that area, the Abakan Tatars and Soyots.

Working translation

Original wording

Als die Russen sie im XVI. Jahrhundert im südlichen Theile des Jenisseischen Gouvernements kennen lernten, waren die Kirgisen vollkommene Türken und noch jetzt bilden Nachkommen der Kirgisen einen grossen Theil der türkischen Bevölkerung desselben Gebietes, der Abakan-Tataren und Sojonen.

Wilhelm Radloff, Die alttuerkischen Inschriften der Mongolei (1895)
See the original page (p. 301)

Daily lifeKhakassia (Yenisei-Abakan steppe)1772

Kachin weddings and the bride who must lie flat

Editorial contextPallas reports that the Kachin Tatars, rich herders who had not taken up farming or Christianity, keep one wife, court for years, and hold two weddings. The groom's father must set up a new yurt for the couple, while the bride's father supplies the household goods. After the betrothal the father-in-law never sees his son's wife again, and if they meet she must throw herself flat on the ground until he has passed.

The first wedding is held at the bride's father's, the second at the groom's, where the groom's father must set up a new yurt for the young couple… Except at the first wooing the father-in-law never sees his son's wife again, never goes into the son's yurt, and if he does meet his daughter-in-law, custom requires that she throw herself flat on the ground.

Original wording

Die erste Hochzeit wird beym Brautvater, die andre beym Bräutigam gefeyert, wo des Bräutigams-Vater dem jungen Paar eine neue Jurte aufstellen muß… Ausser bey der ersten Freywerberey sieht der Schwiegervater seines Sohns Frau niemals wieder, geht nie in die Jurte des Sohns und geschieht es ja, daß er seiner Schwiegertochter begegnet, so will die Gewohnheit, daß sich diese platt auf die Erde niederwirft

Birch-bark summer yurts and earth-covered winter huts

Editorial contextEast of Tomsk, Pallas describes the dwellings of baptised Tatars: summer yurts of poles or bent birch rods shaped like a Kalmyk tent but covered with boiled and stitched white birch bark, and winter huts of thin birch logs shaped like a box, heaped with earth for warmth, with a clay-and-brushwood fireplace and a square smoke hole.

The baptised Tatars here live in summer in wretched yurts made partly of poles leaned together to a point, partly of birch rods fixed and bent from the ground up around some large hoops, which take the shape of a Kalmyk tent and are covered with strongly boiled, sewn-together white birch bark.
