Daily lifeGartok (western Tibet)1715

A Tartar prince's widow commands the frontier

Editorial contextAt Gartok on the western frontier Desideri found a permanent force of Tartar and Tibetan soldiers guarding the fortress of Tashigang and watching everyone who entered Tibet. Its commander, a Tartar prince, had died, and his widow had taken over the command until a new governor arrived. Desideri and his companion travelled to Lhasa under her protection; later in the book he says this 'Tartar princess' retired to a nunnery near Shigatse.

in a large plain called Cartoà a strong, permanent force of Tartar and Tibetan soldiers was stationed not only for the defence of the frontier-fortress of Treéscy-Khang and the surrounding settlements, but more especially to watch all those who from that side entered the country. The commander, a Tartar prince, had died and his widow had taken over the command

C. Wessels (1924), closely paraphrasing Ippolito Desideri's manuscript

Keep in mind

Daily lifeSpitic. 1818 to 1821

'I'll make you a real Tartar': travelling on sheep

Editorial contextWhen Gerard could not find porters for his grain on the empty high plateau, his friend Putee Ram brought a flock of sheep. The trick was to load the grain on the sheep, eat the grain first, and then eat the sheep. It is a neat picture of how people actually crossed the high Tibetan uplands.

I'll make you a real Tartar: you have grain for ten or twelve days, and now you have no use for porters; load the sheep with the grain, finish it first, and then kill and eat the sheep : this is the way we travel on the uninhabitable tracts ; we never think of grain as long as we have plenty of sheep.

Putee Ram of Soongnum, quoted by Alexander Gerard (footnote)

BuildingsTashigang, western Tibetc. 1680s

The defeated Mongols build a moated fort

Editorial contextAfter the Kashmiri army routed the Tibetans near Basgo, the Mongol army fled to Tashigang, where, the chronicle says, they built a fort, surrounded it with a wall and ringed it with water, and stayed inside, secure against assault. The builders in this passage are the Mongols themselves.

the Mongol army in its flight [eventually] reached Bkra-sis-sgan. There they built a fort, shut it in with a wall, and surrounded it with water. Inside they made it secure against an assault of armies, and there they abode.

Chronicles of Ladakh (La dvags rgyal rabs), translated by A. H. Francke (1926)

The nameTsaparang (Guge, western Tibet)1626

Tibet plus Mongolia make 'great Tartary'

Editorial contextWriting from Tsaparang in 1626, the Portuguese Jesuit Antonio de Andrade lists the kingdoms of Tibet and says that, together with the empire of the 'Sopo' (Mongols), they make up 'great Tartary'. He also demotes fabled Cathay to a single large town ruled by the Mongol king. It is a neat example of how elastic the European label 'Tartary' was: here it swallows Tibet as well as Mongolia.

Together with the great empire of the Sopò [Mongolia], which borders on China on the one side and on Moscovia on the other, they form great Tartary". … "The famous Cathayo is not a separate kingdom but a large town which is called Katay, the capital of a province situated much nearer to China and which is said to be ruled by the great king of the Sopôs"

Antonio de Andrade, letter of 15 August 1626, in C. Wessels's English translation (1924); bracketed word is Wessels's

Daily lifeSpitic. 1818 to 1821

'Very far removed from the ferocity' of the Tartar legend

Editorial contextGerard spent days travelling with boys from Tibetan villages as guides and describes them with real affection. He directly rejects the reputation for ferocity that the word Tartar carried in Europe. When he fell ill with rheumatism at Peenoo in Spiti, the villagers brought medicine, sugar, spices and tea.

They are of a mild and benevolent disposition, very far removed from the ferocity commonly attached to the character of a Tartar. I have had many instances of their humanity. At Peenoo, in Speetee, where I was confined to my bed for two days with rheumatism, I never experienced more attention

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Gartok: a famous market town of black tents

Editorial contextGartok (Garoo), the chief wool mart of western Tibet, is not a town of houses at all but a camp of black tents occupied for six months of the year. In winter the people move downstream and the Chinese governors live in a fort. Each July a fair of 10,000 to 12,000 people gathers there. Gerard never got there; this comes from informants.

Is a collection of black tents inhabited by pastoral tribes for six months. In winter, the Tartars retire chiefly to Eegoong on the bank of the river, two stages down the stream, and the Chinese governors reside at the Fort of Tuzheegung, where they have houses. Garoo is the most famous mart for wool in Chinese Tartary

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Daily lifePin valley, Spitic. 1818 to 1821

A frontier chief who refused 150 rupees

Editorial contextAt Peenoo in Spiti, Gerard asked the headman (styled Lafa) for leave to return by the Taree pass. The man sent back his 150 rupees and said the travellers were welcome to goats, sheep and blankets, but not to that road, though he would not fight them. Gerard admired his firmness and goes on to say that the Tartars of the Chinese frontier were as strict about strangers as officials at Peking; he had been turned back at four different points the year before.

The Chinese Tartars, on this remote frontier of their vast empire, are just as vigilant respecting the non-admission of strangers as their countrymen at Pekin : no sum of money, however great, will bribe them to infringe the orders of their superiors.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

BuildingsSpitic. 1818 to 1821

Houses of stone below and sun-dried brick above

Editorial contextGerard describes the flat-roofed, two-storey houses of Spiti: stone on the ground floor and unburnt (sun-dried) brick on the upper floor, which he hears is the rule across Ladakh too. In a footnote elsewhere he explains that the climate is so dry that such brick houses last as well as stone. Firewood is so scarce it is brought by yak and stacked on the roofs.

The lower half of the houses here, and I have heard, throughout most part of Ludak, is built of stone, and the upper of unburnt bricks : they are two stories high, and flat roofed.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

The people of Spiti are 'completely of Tartar appearance'

Editorial contextOn his side trip to Spiti (Piti), Trebeck described the people as 'completely of Tartar appearance in form and face', then went on to their dress, drinking, and the braided hair of the women decorated with amber and coral. It shows 'Tartar' used by British travellers for any Tibetan or Central Asian looking highlander.

The people of Piti are completely of Tartar appearance in form and face; the men are less robust and better grown than those more to the north, and the women are far from good looking.

George Trebeck (journal, edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)
