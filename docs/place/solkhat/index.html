CitiesStaryi Krym (Crim, Solkhat), Crimea1578 to 1579

Crim, the old city that gave the Crimean Tatars their name

Crim had a very old, high wall and many ancient mosques inside and outside the town with Arabic ('Chaldaic') inscriptions carved in large stones. Tatar and Turkish residents told him their ancestors remembered that Persians had once lived there, and that it had been the leading workshop of the mechanical arts. The ruins showed a once famous city, and he notes that the Tatars are now commonly called 'Crimean' after it.

For very many ancient Muslim temples or shrines, with Chaldaic characters cut into rather large stones, are seen not only in the city itself but beyond it. Quite a few Turkish or Tartar inhabitants, though fewer Greeks, report that their forefathers constantly remembered that the city was once inhabited by a people of Persians, and was once an outstanding and almost the chief workshop of the mechanical arts. ... From this place the Tartars are now commonly called Crimean.

Turcę seu Tartari nõ pauci admodũ incolę, Gręci tamen rariores hoc referũt, quod maiores sui constanter meminerint, eã ciuitatem à Persarum olim gente inhabitatam, præstantem ac primariam ferè officinam Mechanicarum artium quondam eam extitisse. … Tartari ab eo loco Crimenses vulgò nunc appellantur.

CitiesStaryi Krym, Crimea1765

Eski-Krim: from 'Cimmerium' to a wretched market town

Peyssonnel identifies the town the Tatars call Eski-Krim ('Old Crimea') with Ptolemy's Cimmerium and argues that its name, which the Tatars gave to the whole peninsula, is Cimmerium disfigured. Now only a miserable market town, it seemed to have once been large and flourishing, with monuments from remote antiquity, the Middle Ages and the Genoese period.

The town that the Tartars today call Eski-Krim is certainly the ancient Cimmerium of Ptolemy … its name Krim, which is also that of the peninsula of Crimea, is plainly the word Cimmerium … disfigured by the Tartars. This town, now no more than a wretched market town, appears once to have been large and flourishing. There are still several monuments of remote ages, of the Middle Ages and of the Genoese period.

Cette Ville qui n'est plus qu'un misérable Bourg, paroît avoir été autrefois vaste & florissante. Il y a encore plusieurs monumens des siècles reculés, du moyen âge & du temps des Génois.

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

Peyssonnel lived in Crimea but does not say he saw these ruins. His Cimmerium etymology is a guess; Eski-Krim is the medieval Solkhat or Qirim, the Golden Horde's seat in Crimea, and Qirim is usually explained from Turkic.

CitiesCrimea (Kaffa or Solkhat)1303

City, castle, palace: the merchants' town vocabulary

The glossary gives, column by column, Latin, Persian and Cuman words for places. A city (civitas) is 'saar' or 'chent' in Cuman, a castle is 'kalaa', a palace is 'saray', and a house is 'eu' (ev). The words for city, castle and palace are loans from Persian and Arabic (shahr, qal'a, saray), next to the Turkic 'kent' and 'ev'. The Italian merchants clearly needed these words for dealing with towns in the Horde's lands.

city: (Persian) shahr / (Cuman) shahr or kent; castle: (Persian) qal'a / (Cuman) qal'a; village: (Persian) deh / (Cuman) qala; palace: saray / saray; house: (Persian) khana / (Cuman) ev.

Working translation

Original wording

ciuitas ghachar saar ul chent castrum kala kalaa casale de gala palacium saray saray domus ghana eu

Read itCodex Cumanicus, Latin-Persian-Cuman glossary (Italian part, dated 11 July 1303), ed. Geza Kuun 1880

About Cumans (Qipchaqs) of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

The modern equivalents in the translation are the usual identifications of these spellings; the scribe's spellings are Italian.

BuildingsCrimea (Solkhat, Chersonesos)c. 1550

Crimea's ruined Greek cities with a thousand churches

Michalo says the Crimea was once so densely peopled by Greeks that single cities had a thousand churches, and that some cities, though ruined, still show by their walls and ruins how grand they were: Solkhat (called Krym by the Muscovites and once Theodosia by the Greeks) and old Chersonesos (Korsun), where the Rus' were baptised. He is clear about who built them: the Greeks, not the Tatars, who came later as settlers.

And even now some cities there, though ruined, show by the size of their circuits and of their ruins that they were once splendid, above all the one we call Solkhat, the Muscovites Krym, and the Greeks once called Theodosia, and the ancient metropolis of Korsun, which first gave baptism to the Ruthenian people

The nameStaryi Krym, Crimeawritten 1786 (history to 1333)

How the peninsula got the name Krim

In a history letter written before entering Crimea, Craven summarises its past: the Mongols or Tartars conquered it in 1237, and because they traded heavily at the town of Krim, the whole peninsula took that name, the only one Eastern peoples used. When she later reached the site (n198) she found 'not a vestige' of the ancient town left.

and, as the Tartars carried on a great trade in the town of Krim, the peninsula took the name of Krim, by which only it is known to the Orientals at this hour

Read itElizabeth Craven (letters published London, 1789)

About Mongols or Tartars of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

A second-hand summary of history from books she does not name.

CitiesStaryi Krymabout 1820

Staryi Krym, the old capital, in ruins

Holderness calls Starai Krim, 'once the capital of the peninsula', little better than a heap of ruins, its few inhabitants Armenian shopkeepers. In a footnote she says the ruins were very extensive and that the town had still been flourishing at the Russian conquest.

These ruins are very extensive, and we know that at the first conquest of the country by the Russians, it was a flourishing town, though not to be compared to what it was when held by the Genoese.

Saw itMary Holderness (footnote)

About Crimean Tatars; Armenian shopkeepers

Keep in mind

Staryi Krym (the medieval Solkhat) was a Golden Horde and early Crimean Khanate centre; her remark that it was once 'held by the Genoese' may confuse it with nearby Genoese Caffa.

CitiesBilohirsk (Karasubazar) and Stary Krym (Eski-Krym)1803

Karasubazar's trading khans and the ruins of old Krim

At the market town of Karasubazar he counts twenty-three mosques and describes the merchants' khans (warehouses) that look like old Gothic palaces. Eski-Krym, the old Tatar capital that gave the whole peninsula its name, now lies in ruins in a fertile plain, its gardens abandoned.

They reckon in Carasubasar, twenty-three mosques, and three Greek or Armenian churches. The khans, or houses of depôt for commerce, resemble old Gothic palaces… Eski-krym, anciently Krim, which, under the Tatars, gave its name to the whole peninsula, is situated, with its extensive ruins, in a fertile plain at the foot of the mountain

Saw itJean Reuilly (English translation, 1807)
