CitiesTyumenLegendary (Chingi-Tura existed by the 14th to 15th centuries)

Chingiden, the Tatar town where Tyumen stands

After conquering peoples along the Irtysh and Ob for Chingis, Taibuga asked leave to settle where he liked. He went with his household to the Tura river, 'built a town there and named it Chingiden; now on that place is the town of Tyumen'. Another page of the same volume says the Russian voevodas built Tyumen in 1586 'where before there was a Tatar town, Chingidi'. The Tatar builder and the later Russian town are named in sequence.

Prince Taibuga went off with all his household to the river Tura, and there built a town and named it Chingiden; and now on this place is the town of Tyumen.

Original wording

Изыде жъ князь Таибуга со всемъ домомъ своимъ на рѣку Туру и тамо созда градъ и нарече его Чингиденъ; нынѣ жъ на семъ мѣсте градъ Тюмень.

Legend or rumourSavva Esipov, Siberian Chronicle (1636), in Sibirskie letopisi (1907)

Keep in mind

CitiesTyumen13th century to 1580, and the 1770s

The 'town people' of the Tura and the old city of Tyumen

Georgi explains that one Tatar colony in western Siberia took to fixed dwellings and towns earlier than others, and so was called Turali, 'town people', from the Tatar word tura, a town. Their oldest seat was Chingi Tura, later called Tyumen, a name the Russians gave to the city they built in its place. He adds that when Yermak conquered them in 1580 their ruler lived in another town, Tura, rebuilt by the Russians in 1600 as Turinsk.

the Tatar colony … had more and earlier reason than other colonies to think of permanent, fixed dwellings or towns (Tatar tura), from which they were called Turali or Turalinzy, that is, town or settled people. … Their oldest fixed seat was Chingi Tura (the town of Chingi), which they later named Tyumen

Working translation

Original wording

fand die tatarische Kolonie … mehr und früher Veranlassung als andere Kolonien, auf beständige und feste Wohnsitze oder Städte (T. Tura) zu denken, wovon sie Turali oder Turalinzi das ist Stad- oder seßhafte Leute genennet wurden. … Ihr ältester, fester Sitz war Tschingi Tura (die Stadt Tschingi,) die nachher von ihnen Tiumen genannt ward

CitiesTyumen1828 (history from 1571)

Tyumen, the 'city of Chinggis'

Erman reports that the Tatars still called Tyumen 'Chingistora', the city of Chinggis, from the time followers of the Mongol khan settled on the Tura. Khan Kuchum later moved his capital to Isker, near today's Tobolsk, which Yermak attacked. The first Russian town in Asia, he says, was founded on the site of Chingistora in 1586.

This city is, to this day, called by the Tatars Chingistora, the city of Chingis, the name it was known by when some followers of the Mongolian Khan, Chingis, fixed their residence on the Tura. In 1571, Yermak, with 500 of his followers, marched against the Tatar city of Isker, eastward of this place, near where Tobolsk now stands

Heard itAdolph Erman (translated by W. D. Cooley, 1848)

CitiesTyumen1730s

Chimgi-Tura, the old Tatar court at Tyumen

Müller identifies the first seat of the Siberian Tatar princes with Tyumen. The remains of the old Tatar court, a small entrenchment between two ravines of the Tyumenka stream, were still visible, and the local Tatars still called the fort and the Russian town Chimgi or Chimgi-Tura, without knowing why. Müller argues the name comes from 'Chingidin', given by the founder Taibuga in honour of Chinggis Khan.

There one still sees the remains of the former Tatar court, which consisted of a small entrenchment and lay between two ravines of the Tyumenka stream, which flows through the town into the Tura. The Tatars there still call this fortress, and after it the town of Tyumen built beside it, Chimgi or Chimgi-Tura. Why, they do not know.

Working translation

Original wording

Man siehet daselbst noch die Ueberbleibsel des ehmahligen Tatarischen Hoflagers, das in einer kleinen Verschanzung bestanden, und zwischen zwo Klüften des Baches Tümenka, welcher durch die Stadt in die Tura fällt, gelegen gewesen. Die dortigen Tataren nennen diese Festung, und hiernächst auch die dabey erbauete Stadt Tümen, noch heutzutage Tschimgi, oder Zimgitura. Warum? wissen sie nicht.

Saw itGerhard Friedrich Müller (Sammlung Russischer Geschichte, vol. 6, 1761-1762)

The nameTyumen and Tobolsk1760s

'Turali': the Tatars who lived in towns

Discussing the word 'tura' (town or fortress in Tatar and Bukharan), Müller reports that the Bashkirs call the Tyumen Tatars, and after them the Tobolsk Tatars, only 'Turali'. On the next page he gives the Bashkirs' explanation: these Tatars had lived since ancient times in towns and fortresses, or at least in fixed places, while the Bashkirs often moved their dwellings about the steppe.

One may easily think here, since Tura in the Tatar and Bukharan language means a town or fortress, that perhaps the river Tura received its name from this old Tatar fortress of Chimgi. The Bashkirs have no other name for the Tyumen Tatars, and after them for the Tobolsk Tatars too, than to call them Turali

Working translation

Original wording

Man kann hier leicht auf die Gedanken kommen, da Tura in der Tatarischen und Bucharischen Sprache eine Stadt, oder Festung, bedeutet, daß vielleicht der Fluß Tura von dieser alten Tatarischen Festung Tschimgi den Nahmen empfangen. Die Baschkiren haben für die Tümenischen Tataren, und nach selbigen auch für die Tobolskischen, keinen andern Nahmen, als daß sie solche Turali nennen

Heard itGerhard Friedrich Müller (Sammlung Russischer Geschichte, vol. 6, 1761-1762)

Daily lifeTobol river, between Tobolsk and Tyumen1714

Tatar boat-haulers flogged by Russian soldiers

Going up the Tobol river from Tobolsk to Tyumen, the embassy's boats were towed the whole way by Tatars. In Tulishen's own Chinese words, there was no towpath, the men waded through mud until their legs bled, and the Russian soldiers kept whipping them until the envoy, unable to watch, told them to stop. It is one of the few moments where the author shows personal feeling.

All along the way the Ta-ta-la (Tatar) people hauled the towropes. The banks were thick with trees and there was no towpath, so they all walked in mud and water; the skin of their legs and feet was torn and blood ran down, yet the Russian soldiers still flogged them on. I could not bear to watch, and only when I rebuked them did they stop.

Original wording

途中皆搭搭拉之人挽縴岸傍林木叢宻無縴路俱行泥水之中兩足肌膚破損血水淋漓俄羅斯兵丁猶加笞楚催促余不忍視呵責方止

Daily lifeTyumen region1820

'No purse being necessary among these Tartars'

Walking toward Tyumen with sore feet, Cochrane stopped at a small Tartar village where he was fed for nothing. He shook hands with his host, received the Muslim blessing 'Peace be with you', and squatted on his heels with the family to eat. Hospitality to strangers comes up again and again in his account of the Siberian Tatars.

At the last station, a small Tartar village, I was regaled with pork, bread and eggs, at free cost, no purse being necessary among these Tartars. I partook of the wholesome fare a la Tartare, shaking hands first with the host, and receiving his blessing of "Peace be with you," then squatting on my hams, like the rest of the company.

Saw itJohn Dundas Cochrane (1824)

Keep in mind

CitiesTyumen1692

Tyumen, a walled town of Tatar traders

Reaching Tyumen on 25 June 1692, Brand found a large town with a rampart and walls, mostly fallen into decay. Most of its people were born Tatars who had settled there, many baptised into the Russian faith and the rest Muslims, who traded far into the Kalmyk lands and Bukhara and paid tribute to the tsar. He calls them a courteous people.

we reached the town of Tyumen, a fairly large place surrounded with rampart and walls, but mostly decayed; the inhabitants of this town are mostly born Tartars who have settled here, many of whom have taken the Russian faith, the others the Muslim faith; these people carry on great trade in foreign places, such as the Kalmyk and Bukharan lands

The nameWestern Siberia (Tura, Tobol, Irtysh, Ob)c. 1580 (narrated c. 1700)

Tatars by the rivers, reindeer people to the north, camel herders on the steppe

What Yermak is said to have heard about Siberia before setting out: a map in words that sorts the peoples. The Tatars live along the Tura, Tobol and Irtysh and travel by boat and horse; the Ostyaks and Samoyeds of the Ob ride reindeer and dogs and live on fish; Kalmyks, Mongols and the Kazakh horde ride camels on the steppe. 'Tatar' here is one people among many, not a label for all Siberians.

and along the Tura and the Tobol live Tatars, who travel in boats and on horses. The Tobol falls into the Irtysh; and on the Irtysh near its mouth is a tsardom and many Tatars; the Irtysh falls into the Ob, and the Ob falls into the sea by two mouths, and along it live Ostyaks and Samoyeds, who ride reindeer and dogs and feed on fish; and on the steppe the Kalmyks and Mongols and the Kazakh horde, who ride camels

Working translation

Original wording

по Турѣ же и по Тоболу живутъ Татара, ѣздятъ въ лодкахъ и на коняхъ. А Тоболъ палъ въ Иртышъ; и на Иртышѣ царство близь устья и многіе Татара, и Иртышъ палъ въ Обь, а Обь рѣка пала въ море двѣма устьи, а по ней живутъ Остяки и Самоѣдь, ѣздятъ на оленяхъ и псахъ и кормятся рыбами; а по степи Калмыки и Мунгалы и казачья орда, ѣздятъ на вельбудахъ

Daily lifeTura river, western Siberiac. 1609

Tartars and Samoyeds who keep cattle and boats

On the route down the Tura below Verkhoturye, Massa notes that the country is now inhabited by Tartars and Samoyeds who live by keeping cattle and also have boats. On the page before he mentions 'wild Tartars and Samoyeds' in the Ural forests who hunt costly furs for the Muscovites.

This place is now inhabited by Tartars and Samoyeds, who keep cattle as a means of subsistence, and have boats besides.

Heard itIsaac Massa's description of Siberia, as printed by Hessel Gerritsz in 1612 (English translation by F. J. Millard in the 1878 facsimile edition)
