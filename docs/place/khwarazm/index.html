Strange talesKhorezm oasis, near Dashoguz1840

A cemetery of giants at Umbarr

Editorial contextPassing an abandoned walled town, Abbott's interpreter told him it had been a Kalmyk capital, and that the late Khan stopped rebuilding it when diggers found huge human bones. The interpreter claimed to have seen skulls too big to hug that crumbled to powder in the air. Abbott doubts the giants but thinks the bones deserve study.

He said, it was supposed to have been the capital of Khaurism, under the Culmauks, whom we call Calmucks. That these were slain, in an invasion by Russia. That the late Khaun purposed rebuilding this city, and had walled it accordingly ; but that, in digging, some monstrous human bones were found, which proved it to have been a cemetery of giants.

Ali Muhummud, Abbott's interpreter, as reported by James Abbott

CitiesKunya-Urgench, Turkmenistan1740

The ruined city of Urgench and its gold

Editorial contextOn the way to Khiva the factors passed the old city they call 'Jurgantz' (Urgench), once large but now all in ruins except a mosque. Their Tartar companions prayed there and then went looking for gold that, they said, rains often washed out of the ruins. Urgench had been the great capital of Khwarazm destroyed by the Mongols in 1221 and again by Tamerlane.

…we came to the city Jurgantz, which appeared to have been a large place; but now was entirely in ruins, no other building remaining than a mosque. Here our company were very devout: they offered their prayers to heaven for their safe journey, and then went in search of gold, which they said, they had frequently found washed out by the rains from amongst the ruins of this city.

British factors Reynold Hogg and George Thompson, their 1740 journal as printed by Jonas Hanway (1753)

CitiesKonye-Urgench1840

Old Urgench, a brick fort melted into mounds

Editorial contextAbbott paced out the ruins of the old capital of Khwarazm: a town about a mile long with a square brick citadel and round bastions on a raised platform. The town walls, which he guessed were of unbaked brick, had slumped into low mounds.

The extent of this city was perhaps one mile by half a mile, with extensive suburbs on the north. At the east angle is the citadel, a brick fort of square figure, flanked by eight circular, and projecting bastions. … but the whole is now a ruin, as is the ancient city ; the walls of the latter, which were probably of unbaked brick, having subsided into mere mounds.

James Abbott

BuildingsKonye-Urgench1840

A brick tower said to be Genghis Khan's

Editorial contextAmong the ruins of Old Urgench Abbott saw a tall, tapering brick column that he says was put up by Genghis Khan, decorated only with bands of patterned brick and leaning from the wind. Nearby he describes a saint's tomb with a conical roof of coloured tiles on a 24-sided base, and a tiled tomb said to have been built by a lady of Genghis Khan's family for her lover.

On the north of the site, (I speak at hazard,) stands a brick column, erected by Chenghis Khaun. It is the frustrum of an extremely taper cone, surmounted by a cylinder, and is very sufficiently ugly. It has no base, and its sole ornaments have been zones of bricks, of alternating patterns.

James Abbott

Strange talesKonye-Urgench1844 (telling); 1370s to 1380s (events)

Timur's pyramid of skulls, as the Turkmens told it

Editorial contextSnowed in at a desert camp, Wolff listened to Turkmens talk of Alexander and Timur as if they had lived yesterday. One told how Timur punished the Khan of Khwarazm by building a pyramid of human skulls cemented with clay at Urgench, sparing only dervishes, scholars and poets.

It is remarkable to hear these Turkomauns speak of the exploits of Alexander and Timur, exactly as if of modern occurrence. … He made a pyramid at Organtsh entirely of skulls of men, cemented with clay. He spared the lives of none, except those of holy derveeshes, of the learned, and of poets, around whose houses he placed Karawl, i. e. guards.

A Turkmen in the Merv desert, as reported by Joseph Wolff
See the original page (p. 215)

About Turkmens of the Merv desert; Timur (Tamerlane)

Keep in mind

Oral tradition. Timur's skull towers are recorded elsewhere (Wolff also mentions one at Sabzevar), but this telling is folk memory.

Daily lifeKhwarazm to Crimeabefore 1395

Caravans on carts with no need for provisions

Editorial contextIbn Arabshah says caravans used to set out from Khwarazm and travel by cart (ajal) across the steppe to Crimea, a journey of about three months, without fear. They carried no food or fodder and took no guide, because the steppe was so thick with peoples and so full of security and food that they only ever stopped at some tribe, and only halted with people who honoured their guests.

Caravans used to leave Khwarazm and travel by cart, safe, without doubt or fear … The caravan carried no provisions and no fodder, and took no companion along, because of the multitude of the peoples and the abundance of security, food and drink among the retinues; they set out only from a tribe and halted only with someone who honoured his guest.

Khwarezm, largest city of the Turks, where skipping prayers meant a whipping

Editorial contextAfter forty days across the steppe by camel cart, by way of Saraichuk with its bridge, Ibn Battuta reached Khwarezm (Urgench), which he calls the largest and most crowded city of the Turks, ruled by an emir for Uzbek Khan. He praises its people as the best-mannered he had met, and describes a whip hung in every mosque to punish those who missed prayers.

This is the largest city the Turks have, and is very much crowded, on account of the multitude of its inhabitants. It is subject to the sultan Uzbek Khan, and is governed on his part by a great Emir, who resides within it.

Ibn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)
