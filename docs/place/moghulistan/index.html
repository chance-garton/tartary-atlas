What the sources say here: 4 passages

Daily lifeMoghulistanlate 15th century

No true Muslims until they settle in towns

At the beginning of the reign of Yunus Khan, all the Moghuls dwelt, according to their old custom, in Moghulistan ; they avoided all towns and cultivated countries [and regarded them] with great repugnance. They were Musulmans in nothing but the name

Editorial context

At the start of Yunus Khan's reign, the Moghuls still lived in the old way, shunning towns and farmland, and were Muslims in name only; neighbours even sold them as slaves like unbelievers. Yunus decided that they would never be good Muslims until they settled, and led them to Tashkent, though some rebelled and stayed on the steppe. Elsewhere (p. 58) Mirza Haidar records that an earlier khan had a horseshoe nail driven into the head of any Moghul who refused to wear a turban.

Mirza Haidar Dughlat (translated by E. Denison Ross, 1895)

Turkistan after the Tatars: nothing but ruins

Since the region has been devastated by the arms of the Tatars," he writes, "it is inhabited only by a scanty population. According to what I have been assured by a man who has travelled through the country, there is nothing to be seen in Turkistan but ruins, and more or less obliterated remains.

Editorial context

The editor quotes the fourteenth-century Arab geographer Shihab al-Din (al-Umari), who was told by a traveller that the land had been ruined 'by the arms of the Tatars'. From a distance a village looks well built, but up close its houses are empty, and the people are now all herders. Elias adds that the towns had been built by earlier settled peoples such as the Uighurs and Kara-Khitai, before the Mongol nomads made it their home.

Shihab al-Din (al-Umari), Masalik al-Absar, quoted in Ney Elias's introduction (1895)

Daily lifeMoghulistanMid-1400s

A khan raised up on a white felt

Shir Haji Beg having seated the Khan and Ais-doulet Begum on a white felt, according to the Tureh, or ancient Institutions of the Moghuls, they proclaimed him Khan.

Editorial context

Babur tells how his Mongol grandfather Yunis Khan was made khan: a leading beg seated him and his new wife on a white felt and proclaimed him, according to the ancient Mongol rules (tore). Erskine's footnote links it to the felt on which Genghis Khan was said to have been proclaimed.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

Daily lifeMoghulistanabout 1400

The first kumiss of spring, and a cup that fell

I have frequently heard my father (upon whom rest the mercy of God) relate that in the beginning of the spring the new kimiz had come in, and on that day, according to an ancient Moghul custom, a great feast had been prepared.

Editorial context

Mirza Haidar's father told him of a spring feast held, by old Moghul custom, when the new kumiss (fermented mare's milk) came in. Just as the amir offered the khan a cup, news came of an envoy from Timur demanding tribute, and the cup fell from the khan's hands. The amir consoled him with verses about not grieving over tomorrow.

Mirza Haidar Dughlat (translated by E. Denison Ross, 1895)
