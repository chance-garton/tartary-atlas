Telebuga lost in the mountains: a hundred thousand dead

Editorial contextReturning from a raid on Hungary, Nogai went by Brasov, while Telebuga tried to cross the mountains. A three-day crossing became fifty days of wandering. Famine set in, the soldiers began to eat people and then to die, and 'eyewitnesses said' a hundred thousand perished. Telebuga came out on foot with his wife and a single mare. The chronicler presents it as God's punishment.

Telebuga went across the Mountain; what should have been crossed in three days, he wandered for 50 days lost in the mountains, led by the wrath of God. And there was a great famine among them; and they began to eat people, and then began themselves to die, and a countless multitude of them died; eyewitnesses said this: the dead were a hundred thousand.

Tartar bailiffs, markets and justice under occupation

Editorial contextAfter the massacres the Tartars lured people back from the woods and set up a working occupation. Each village chose a Tartar 'king'; the invaders appointed about a hundred 'canesii' (Roger glosses them as bailiffs) who kept justice and collected horses, animals, arms and gifts. Roger's own master was one of them and ruled almost a thousand villages. 'We had peace and markets,' he writes. A few lines later the same officials summon villagers with gifts and massacre them.

and each village chose for itself a king from among the Tartars, whomever it wished. … They appointed Canesii, that is bailiffs, to do justice and to procure for them horses, animals, weapons, gifts and useful clothing. My master was one of these lords, and ruled almost a thousand villages. And there were about a hundred Canesii. We had peace and markets, and justice was kept for each person.

Working translation

Original wording

et quaelibet villa elegit sibi regem de Tartaris quem optauit. … Constituerunt Canesios id est Baliuos, qui iustitiam facerent, et eis equos animalia arma exenia et vestimenta utilia procurarent. Et sic procurator meus de istis dominis erat unus, et pene mille villas regebat. Et erant Canesii fere centum. Pacem habebamus et fora, iustaque unicuique iusticia seruabatur.

"Held not by Tartars but in Tartarus"

Editorial contextIn his opening letter Roger plays on the old pun that made 'Tartars' out of 'Tatars': whoever falls into their hands will feel he is held not by Tartars but in Tartarus, the pit of the underworld. He stresses that he writes from experience, having lived among them 'for a time and half a time', a biblical phrase for a long ordeal. The pun shows how the name itself carried a hellish ring for Latin Christians.

And let everyone know that I do not report these things rashly: whoever comes into the hands of these Tartars, it would have been better for him not to have been born, and he will feel himself held not by Tartars but in Tartarus. I report this as one who went through it. For I was among them for a time and half a time, in which death would have been a comfort, just as life was a punishment.

Working translation

Original wording

Et sciant cuncti haec me temere non referre; quod quisquis ad manus ipsorum deuenerit Tartarorum, si natus non fuisset melius esset ei; et sentiet se non a Tartaris sed in tartaro detineri. Hoc refero ut expertus. Fui enim per tempus et dimidium temporis inter eos; in quo mori solatium exstitisset, sicut supplicium fuit vita.

Master Roger (Rogerius), Carmen miserabile, in the 1885 Budapest edition (Chronica minora)

'They have tents, you have castles and cities': a Mongol threat

Editorial contextKuun quotes a letter from a Mongol commander to King Bela IV of Hungary, brought back by the Dominican Friar Julian. The Mongol calls the Cumans who fled into Hungary 'our slaves' and warns the king: the Cumans live without houses and move about with tents, so they might escape, but a king who lives in houses and has castles and cities cannot. It is a clear contemporary statement of the difference between steppe tents and settled towns, seen from the Mongol side.

For it is easier for the Cumans to escape than for you, because they, going about without houses, with tents, may perhaps escape. But you, living in houses, have castles and cities: how will you escape my hands?

Original wording

Facilius enim est Cumanis evadere quam tibi, quia illi sine domibus cum tentoriis ambulantes possunt forsitan evadere. Tu autem in domibus habitans habes castra et civitates, quomodo effugies manus meas?

A Mongol commander's letter to King Bela IV (c.1237), as brought back by Friar Julian; quoted by Geza Kuun (1880)

Dummies on spare horses to fake an army

Editorial contextBishop Benedict of Várad chased a small Tartar band that had sacked Eger. Knowing they were few but had many horses, the Tartars made dummies and figures, set them on the empty horses like riders and hid them behind a low hill. When they pretended to retreat and the 'army' of dummies came out in battle order, the Hungarians thought they had fallen into an ambush and fled, and were cut down.

They made a great many masks and figures, setting them on the empty horses as if they were soldiers. And they left those horses below a little hill, with a few servants left with them … And when these came out from under the hill with the masks, in battle order as had been agreed, the Hungarians, seeing this and thinking an ambush had been laid for them, turned their backs and quickly took to flight.

Original wording

A 13th-century poem calls the Tartars cannibals

Editorial contextIn his notes d'Avezac prints lines, copied by Thomas Wright from a Cotton manuscript, of John of Garland's poem On the Triumphs of the Church. It paints the Tartars as Satan's household and the Church's plague who eat human flesh: no lion eats a lion and no wolf tears a wolf, but these men roast human limbs on spits. D'Avezac traces the poet's 'reliable' information to atrocity letters from priests in central Europe.

A people exceedingly savage, of Satan's household, a plague to the Church, at odds with the faith, in love with slaughter. … Hungry, man gnaws man; no lion fills its slack belly with lion's flesh; no wolf tears a wolf with its claw; this people turns limbs spitted on hazel skewers over the fire.

Working translation

Original wording

Gens est sæva nimis, Sathanæque domestica, pestis Ecclesiæ, fidei dissona, cædis amans. … Esuriens hominem corrodit homo; leo nullus Carne leonina viscera laxa replet; Non lupus ungue lupum lacerat; gens ista colurnis In veribus vertit membra veruta foco.

John of Garland (De triumphis Ecclesiae, 13th century), quoted by Thomas Wright in Armand d'Avezac's notes

The nameHungary1129 and later

Hungarian hymns called the Cumans 'Tartars'

Editorial contextKuun shows how loosely the name was used in medieval Hungary. Hymns to St Ladislaus sometimes call the Cumans 'Tartars', and the chronicler Thuroczy says the chief of the Cumans whom King Stephen II took in (in 1129) was himself named Tatar. This is more than a century before the Mongol invasion of 1241, so 'Tatar' here is a personal name and a label for steppe Turks, not for the Mongols.

In the old Latin and Hungarian hymns of St Ladislaus the Cumans are sometimes called Tartars … Thuroczy, in his Chronicle, likewise names the supreme chief of the Cumans received into Hungary by King Stephen II 'Tatar': 'King Stephen loved the Cumans at that time; their chief, named Tatar, had fled with a few men to the king from the slaughter by the (Greek) emperor'.

Working translation

Original wording

In antiquis hymnis latinis et hungaricis sancti Ladislai Cumani interdum Tartari nuncupantur … Thuroczy in suis Chronicis, Cumanorum a rege Stephano II in Hungariam receptorum summum ducem itidem Tatar nominat: «Rex autem Stephanus, diligebat Cumanos tunc temporis; quorum dux nomine Tatar, qui a cede imperatoris (Graecorum), cum paucis, ad regem fugerat»

Geza Kuun, Latin introduction to his edition of the Codex Cumanicus (Budapest 1880), quoting the Hungarian chronicler Janos Thuroczy (1488)

Bait on the ice: how they crossed the frozen Danube

Editorial contextIn a hard winter the Danube froze over, which had not happened for many years, but the Tartars did not dare ride onto the ice. They drove horses and cattle onto the bank and left them unguarded for three days. The Hungarians, thinking the enemy gone, crossed and led the animals over the ice, which showed the Tartars that it would bear horses; they then crossed in one rush (as the next page tells).

And then in winter a great deal of snow and ice came, so that the Danube froze, which had not happened for many years past. … They brought many horses and animals onto the bank of the Danube and left no one to guard them for three days, so that the beasts seemed to be wandering without keepers, and none of the Tartars appeared in those parts.

Tartar women fought like men

Editorial contextAmong the cruelties of the occupation, Thomas reports that the Tartars' women armed themselves like men and rode boldly into battle. He adds that they treated captive women even more savagely than the men did, killing good-looking ones out of jealousy and disfiguring others to make them servants. Mongol women travelling and sometimes fighting with the army are also mentioned by Matthew Paris's informants.

Moreover the women of the Tartars, girded with arms in the manner of men, charged boldly into battle like men; they raged more cruelly against captive women, for if they saw any with a more beautiful face, of whom they might in any way fear jealousy, they at once drew a sword and killed them

Batu, 'king of kings', and the princes who invaded

Editorial contextRoger lists the Tartar leaders who entered Hungary. Batu is the 'king of kings'; under him a commander Roger calls Bochetor held most power, and Kadan was the bravest. Several more names follow in garbled Latin forms, and Roger gives the army as 500,000 men. It shows how a Latin writer who had met the invaders understood their chain of command.

The king of kings and lord of the Tartars who entered Hungary was called by name Bathus [Batu]. Under him the commander more powerful in the army was called Bochetor. Cadan [Kadan] was said to be the most valiant. Coacton, Feycan, Peta, Hermeus, Cheb and Ocadar were counted as the greater kings among the Tartars; though there were among them very many other kings, princes and mighty men, who invaded the kingdom of Hungary with five hundred thousand armed men.

Islam among the Cumans was called 'the Tartar rite'

Editorial contextKuun notes that Islam had spread among the Cumans and that later Latin church writers called their religion 'the Tartar rite'. He quotes John, deacon of Kukullo, saying that King Louis I of Hungary had the Cuman people, 'wrapped in the Tartar rite', converted to Catholicism. So in church Latin 'Tartar' could simply mean 'Muslim steppe people'.

The Muhammadan religion … spread also among the Cumans, and later Latin church writers called this religion of the Cumans 'the Tartar rite', for example John, deacon of Kukullo: 'He (namely Louis I, king of Hungary) also had the Cuman people, wrapped in the Tartar rite, converted to the Catholic faith'.

Working translation

Original wording

Religio Muhammedanica … etiam apud Cumanos propagata fuit, eam Cumanorum religionem scriptores ecclesiastici latini posteriores «ritum tartaricum» nominabant, ita e. g. Joannes diaconus Küküllőiensis: «Hic etiam gentem Chomanorum, ritui Tartarico involutam … ad fidem Catholicam converti procuravit (scilicet Ludovicus I. rex Hungariae)»

Geza Kuun, Latin introduction to his edition of the Codex Cumanicus (Budapest 1880), quoting John of Kukullo (14th century)

They spared the houses to winter in them

Editorial contextRoger watched Tartar and Cuman raiders return from a night of slaughter in the villages, loaded with spoil. Yet they burned neither grain, straw nor houses. He guessed, and later confirmed, that they meant to winter there and needed shelter and food for their horses. It is a telling detail: these herders used the settled country's buildings rather than destroying them.

Yet they burned neither the crops, nor the straw, nor any houses, but brought all these in to keep themselves alive. From this I firmly concluded that they meant to winter in those parts, or leave their families there, so that in winter they would find shelter and provisions for their horses. All of which I afterwards learned to be true.
