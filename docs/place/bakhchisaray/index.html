Strange talesBakhchysarai1768

Electric shocks at the khan's court taken for magic

After a fireworks show for the khan's birthday, de Tott staged 'a little chamber fire-work' of electrical experiments. The courtiers thought it sorcery; the khan asked to be shocked himself, and next day the whole town talked of nothing but electricity.

The first effects of this phenomenon excited such astonishment, that I had some difficulty in destroying the opinion of magic I saw arising in their minds… The Kam, however, had the air of comprehending me, and was himself desirous of being electrified. I gave him the stroke very moderately, but I handled the courtiers in such a manner as to obtain the Prince's approbation.

Saw itBaron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

CitiesBakhchysarai1829

Bakhchysarai, the Tartar capital in its valley

Riding south past Tatar cottages with minaret-shaped chimneys and the domed tombs of the khans, Alexander looked down on Bakhchysarai, the old capital of the Crimean khans. He describes flat-roofed, tiled houses filling a narrow valley and climbing its sides, with the palace in the middle. On the next page he walks streets lined with workshops where Tatar tailors, smiths, shoemakers and saddlers sat cross-legged, smoking as they worked.

we saw beneath us the singular and romantic Tartar capital of the Crimea. Buckteserai, the Palatium of Strabo, is seated in a narrow valley, through which runs the stream of the Tchuruksu. The flat-roofed and tile-covered houses are built in the bottom of the valley, and up the sides of the hills : in the midst of them is a most interesting object, viz. the Tartar Palace.

Saw itJames Edward Alexander (1830)

The Fountain of Tears inscription: 'never such a fountain'

Jesse copies the translated inscription on the Selsabil fountain in the palace vestibule (the famous 'Fountain of Tears'). It credits Khan Krym Giray with finding the spring and giving water to his people, and boasts that Damascus and Baghdad never saw its equal. He notes that the palace's rooms were small and the furniture mean and modern.

The town of Bagtché-Serai rejoices in the beneficent solicitude of the luminous Crim-Gheri-Khan: it was he, who with generous hand quenched the thirst of his countrymen … If there exists such another fountain, let it present itself. The towns of Scham and Bagdad have seen many things, but never such a fountain.

Saw itInscription on the Selsabil fountain, in an English translation printed by Captain Jesse

Low roofs, minaret chimneys and a wooden harem

Seen from the garden, the Khans' palace was a low building, in parts only one storey, with white roofs and chimneys shaped like little minarets. The harem was a square building of three lofty rooms with verandas and small wooden-grated windows, behind a high wall. The palace of the Crimean Khans, in other words, was modest in scale.

This harem is a square building, with verandas, having only three rooms: these are large and lofty … Each room has a double range of small windows, with wooden gratings … The appearance of the exterior of the palace, as seen from the garden, is that of a low building, in some parts only one story. Its roof is white, and the chimnies appear like so many minarets

BuildingsBakhchysarailate 1830s

Houses with fold-down wooden fronts

In Bakhchysarai's single long, narrow street the houses had no windows: the whole front was a set of wooden flaps that could be raised or lowered and used as counters. So every trade was on show, from a baker at his oven to a brewer of 'boussa' (boza, a fermented millet drink) and a tailor with his journeymen.

The houses have no windows, but the entire front wall consists only of wooden flaps, which can be let down or drawn up at pleasure, and which sometimes serve as tables or boards for goods. In one house you may see a baker at work … In the next you may overlook all the manipulations required in the preparation of the Turkish beverage entitled “boussa,”

Saw itJ. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

Daily lifeBakhchysarai1768

Tartar law: the victim's family carries out the sentence

A Jew's slave murdered his master and then converted to Islam hoping for pardon. De Tott explains that under Tartar law the injured family must execute the criminal themselves. Khan Maksud Giray refused to spare the convert, saying he would hand over his own brother if guilty; in the end the Jews were allowed to stone the murderer according to the law of Moses.

by the Tartar law the criminal must perish by the hand of the injured person, or by his heirs. It was objected then, but in vain, that a Turk could not be delivered up to the Jews. “I would deliver up my brother to them,” replied the Kam, “if he was guilty; I leave Providence to reward his conversion, if it be sincere; it is my duty to do justice.”

Saw itBaron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

'The sea once lay at the foot' of the cliffs

The house of the chief minister's sister stood below extraordinary rocks full of springs. On their summits, Craven says, were marks where huge cables had 'certainly passed and been tied', and the Tatars insisted the sea had once reached the foot of the cliffs and ships were moored there. At dinner she found Tatar cooking 'much grease and honey', and her hostess served two quarts of sherbet, coffee and sweets.

The Tartarians insist upon it that the sea once lay at the foot of them … and ships were fastened to them

Heard itElizabeth Craven (letters published London, 1789)

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

The belief is the Tatars'; the 'cable' marks were probably natural weathering grooves, but Craven gives no more detail than this.

The nameBakhchysarai, Crimeafounding legend, told 1666

How Bahcesaray was founded, 'as the Tatar historians say'

Evliya reports the Crimean Tatars' own story of their origins: the first conquerors of Crimea settled at Eski Yurt near Salachik, coming from Mahan, Ahlat, Kerman and Yesu, and from Kazan, Astrakhan and Sarai. As the Tatars multiplied and the old site grew too small, they settled at Bahcesaray and spread over the whole peninsula, while the 'Genoese infidels' stayed in their seaside fortresses. He names Jochi Khan and 'Artogmuz Khan' as the conquerors behind its first building.

According to the Tatar historians, the first conqueror of Crimea settled at the Eski Salachik yurt; Tatars came to Crimea from the cities of Mahan, Ahlat, Kerman and Yesu and from the lands of Kazan, Astrakhan and Sarai, and as they multiplied they no longer fitted into the old yurt, and settled at this Bahcesaray site.

BuildingsBakhchysarai1578 to 1579

Bakhchisaray: a stone palace, and mosques built from Christian ruins

The khan lives in a stone house in Bakhchisaray, a town between two mountains with a small river through it. Broniewski says the stone mosque and the khans' tombs were built from the ruins of Christian buildings. Next to it lies the town of Salachik, and a palace of the khans that, he says, the early Tatar princes adorned with buildings, mosques, tombs and baths. So he credits Tatars as builders, reusing older stone.

He has a not undistinguished town, and a principal stone house in which he always lives, called Bakhchisaray. ... A stone Muslim temple and the tombs of the khans have been built near the town out of the ruins of the Christians. ... The palace or house of the khans was sumptuously and magnificently adorned by the early princes of the Tartars with buildings, temples, tombs and baths.

A khan who built a hundred mosques

Halim Giray credits Khan Sahib Giray with building a mosque and a bathhouse beside the khan's palace in Bakhchysarai, and about a hundred mosques in other districts. The editor's footnote on the page before adds madrasas, shops, double bathhouses and the fortress at Or Qapi (Perekop) that guarded the isthmus. The builders named are the Tatar khans themselves, and the buildings are ordinary Islamic ones: mosque, bath, madrasa, palace.

Sahib Giray, just as he had a mosque and a bathhouse built beside the khan's palace in Bakhchysarai, also built about a hundred mosques in other quarters, and so succeeded in having his name remembered with blessing in the world.

Bakhchysarai: a city shaped like a funnel

Maria Guthrie calls the former khans' capital a 'romantic city' hidden in a valley: Tatar houses stacked in rings up the slopes around the palace, like an amphitheatre, beneath overhanging rocks carved by wind and water into shapes like towers and obelisks. On the previous page she complains that the streets are dirty, badly paved and too narrow for anything but a horseman or a small cart.

Only figure to yourself, my good friend, in a deep valley, bounded by a huge chain of pendant rocks, an assemblage of Tartar houses of uncouth forms, stuck as it were against the sides of the mountains, and placed in circles one above another, round the palace of their Chan (situated at the bottom of the valley), so as to represent a large amphitheatre, or rather funnel

Bahcesaray: a city of red-tiled houses in a gorge

Evliya describes the Crimean khans' capital as a great city lying in a wide gorge between two cliffs, about eight thousand paces long. The Churuk Su stream runs through the middle, crossed by forty-three stone and timber bridges. On both sides, storey above storey, stand masonry houses roofed with red tiles and set in gardens, climbing to the foot of the cliffs.

Through the very middle of the city runs the Salachik stream … and over this Churuk Su, inside the city, one crosses from one side to the other at forty-three places by stone and timber bridges; and to right and left of the city, storey upon storey, lower and upper, all are masonry buildings roofed from end to end with ruby-red tiles.

BuildingsBakhchysaraic. 1840

The Khan's palace: 'an assemblage of wooden houses'

Adèle defends the palace of the Crimean khans against visitors who sneered that it was only a cluster of wooden houses with crude paintings, divans and carpets. She agrees that is what it is made of, but says its charm lies in feeling, not materials. It is a useful witness to how the Tatar capital's palace was actually built: timber, painted walls, fountains and gardens.

" How," said they, " can any one apply the name of palace to that assemblage of wooden houses, daubed with coarse paintings, and furnished only with divans and carpets?" And these people were right in their way.

Saw itAdèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

BuildingsBakhchysarai1767

Building a house the Tartar way

Facing winter in a poor hut at the khan's capital, de Tott rebuilt it himself 'without departing from the method of building pursued by the Tartars'. On the next page he explains the method: a timber frame of posts, closely set uprights woven with hazel rods like a basket, packed with pounded earth and chopped straw, then plastered with lime and hair and painted. He thought it more solid and healthy than European peasant houses.

I drew the plan, I collected the materials, and superintended the work, without departing from the method of building pursued by the Tartars… I shall take this opportunity of describing the structure of the houses in Crimea; but these details, or the architecture of the Tartars, will be of more service to the lovers of oeconomy, than to disciples of Vitruvius.

Saw itBaron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

The Khans' graves upset a traveller's ideas

Kohl had been taught to think of the Khans as destroyers who burned Moscow and terrorised Vilna and Warsaw. In their burial ground he found neat white marble monuments among vines, shrubs and flowers, with thoughtful inscriptions, and he concluded that 'the records of history' were imperfect.

In the burial-place of the Khans I found many memorials strikingly at variance with our notions of these barbarians; so many traces of humanity and piety, that I felt more strongly than ever, how imperfect are the records of history.

CitiesBakhchysarai1767

A capital that grew from a garden palace

In a footnote de Tott explains that Bakhchysarai ('Palace of the Gardens') began as a country house of the khans; people settled round it and it eclipsed the old capital, Krim (Eski-Krym), which had shrunk to a village whose tombs alone showed its past importance.

This town, at present considered as the capital of the Crimea, was formerly nothing but a country house, called the Palace of the Gardens. The sovereigns by living there have drawn together a number of inhabitants; and this town… has successively usurped the pre-eminence over the ancient town of Crimea, which is now no more than a paltry village, where the tombs alone testify its ancient importance.

The khan's stone palaces and clay-daubed wooden houses

The khan had two residences, one at Bakhchysarai and one on the Alma. At Bakhchysarai the khan's 'palaty' (chambers), kitchens and stables were of stone, set low under a hill, with a church of the Virgin two versts away served by Greeks. The Alma court had one stone chamber and wooden houses plastered with clay, standing in a vineyard. It is a rare early note on Crimean Tatar building materials.

And in Bakhchysarai the tsar's chambers, kitchens and stables are of stone, and it stands low beneath a hill; and about 2 versts beyond the tsar's court is a church of the Most Pure Mother of God in the salt flats, and Greeks serve in it. And the court on the river Alma has one stone chamber, and wooden houses daubed with clay, and it stands on the bank in a vineyard.

The Crimean khan's walled palaces with iron gates

De Luca says the Crimean khan has five palaces (serails) and his heir two, one in the town where he lives (Bakhchysarai, spelled 'Bach* serai' on the previous page). The King has five palaces, and the Sultan two; one in the town where he resides … Each of these palaces is about a mile round and is surrounded by a high wall, but not a strong one; the gates are of iron, and the apartments they enclose are gilded and painted inside in fine colours.

Bakhchysarai: thirty-one mosques and seventy-five fountains

Reuilly describes the old khans' capital packed into a narrow valley, its main street lined with wooden shops. Because Catherine II left the town to the Tatars, no Russian townsmen lived there; the people were Tatars and Jews. He counts its mosques and fountains and describes the one-storey palace with its mosque, stables, terraced garden and cemetery of the khans.

The principal street, which is about a verst and a half in length, contains, almost the whole way along, two rows of miserable wooden shops. As the empress Catherine gave up this town to the Tartars, it contains no Russian burgesses, but is inhabited entirely by Tartars and Jews… Bachtchisarai contains thirty-one mosques, and seventy-five fountains.

CitiesBakhchysarailate 1830s

A town where only Tatars may live

Kohl reports that the Russian government had made the old capital a Tatar reserve: apart from officials, only Tatars were allowed to live there. The government had also preserved the Khans' palace. He found the town crowded and noisy with music, and a complete contrast to the new Russian cities of Sevastopol and Simferopol.

Baktshiserai has been declared their exclusive possession, and, with the exception of official personages, no other than a Tartar is allowed to reside in it. The government has also had the good taste to bestow much pains on the preservation of the palace of the ancient Khans, once the dreaded enemies of Muscovy.

Heard itJ. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

CitiesBakhchysarai, CrimeaApril 1786

Bakhchysarai: five thousand Tatars line the streets

Craven rode into Bakhchysarai, 'formerly the chief residence of the Khans', escorted by some hundred well-dressed Tatar horsemen.
