Daily lifeKhorasan1404

Tent people who sow melons by the rivers

The people of these tents have no other dwelling place, and they wander over the plains, during both winter and summer. In summer they go to the banks of the rivers, and sow their corn, cotton, and melons … The lord, with all his host, wanders in the same way over the plains, winter and summer.

Editorial context

Among the Zagatay tents of Khorasan, Clavijo notes that these people have no other homes. In summer they camp by rivers and sow corn, cotton and melons, in winter they move to warm districts, and Timur and his army wander the same way. On the next page he describes their food of sour milk boiled with bits of dough, cooked over dung fires.

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

CitiesNishapur1221

Nishapur levelled by the Tartars

the Tartars having found means to enter the place after a siege of only three days, spent a day and night in plundering it, and in massacring and reducing to slavery its wretched and defenceless inhabitants; the city was razed, and the ground on which it stood levelled so completely, that it is said a horse might have ridden over the whole site without stumbling

Editorial context

Fraser retells, from Persian histories via Pétis de la Croix, how Genghis Khan's son Tolui took Nishapur after three days, plundered it, killed or enslaved the people, and levelled the site so that a horse could ride over it without stumbling. He openly doubts the historians' death toll of 1,747,000.

James Baillie Fraser, following historians quoted by Pétis de la Croix

BuildingsTurkmen country between Herat and Merv1840

Why a felt tent beats a house

These Toorcumun tents are the most comfortable of dwellings … by removing a portion of the felt covering, this tent is open to the air in summer ; and in winter a fire lighted in the centre makes it the warmest of retreats, all the smoke rising through the skylight in the roof.

Editorial context

Lodged in a Turkmen camp of about 300 black tents, Abbott was won over by the felt tent. He explains that the felt can be lifted in summer, a central fire warms it in winter with the smoke escaping through a roof opening, and the whole home can be moved to a better spot.

James Abbott

Strange talesNishapur1221 (legend); told 1822

The poet who carried his own head

he drew his sword, and smote off the sheikh’s head ; but no sooner had it rolled to the ground than the body taking it up, ran with it a full mile, to the spot where the tomb now is, and with its own finger for a pen, and its blood for ink, wrote upon a stone the epitaph which has since been engraved upon the tomb.

Editorial context

Local legend held that the Sufi poet Attar was captured when the Mongols sacked Nishapur and later beheaded by his owner. His headless body then picked up its head, ran a mile to the site of his tomb and wrote its own epitaph in blood. The tomb's guardians, Fraser adds, told of villagers who stole lead from it and died.

Local tradition, as retold by James Baillie Fraser

Daily lifeDesert between Merv and Khiva1840

Slave caravans on the road to Khiva

They had brought grain from Khiva, and are returning thither laden with slaves, many of whom are natives of Heraut. … But the men are chained together by the throats at night, so that rest is scarcely possible, whilst the contact of the frozen iron with their skin must be torturous. For them also no carriage is found, they walk the whole way

Editorial context

Abbott travelled alongside caravans that had carried grain south and were returning to Khiva with captives, many from Herat. He describes the women riding with their captors and the men walking the whole way, chained by the neck at night.

James Abbott

Strange talesDesert between Bukhara and Merv1845

Russia is Gog and Magog

A Turkomaun present said, "The Russians shall be the conquerors of the world. They have now built a strong castle almost in the midst of the sea, not far from Khiva. … A derveesh sitting among us made the following remark ; "The great mullahs of Samarcand assert that Russia is the Jaaj-Majooj, i. e. Gog and Magog

Editorial context

In a caravan leaving Bukhara, a Turkmen told Wolff that Russia would conquer the world, having built a fort in the sea near Khiva. A dervish added that the great mullahs of Samarkand said Russia was Gog and Magog, the destroying peoples of the end times.

A Turkmen and a dervish, as reported by Joseph Wolff

BuildingsSarakhs1832

A Turkmen tent that travels on two camels

The tent or khirgah was spacious, and had a diameter of about twenty-five feet. The sides were of lattice-work, and the roof was formed

Editorial context

Invited into a Turkmen home at Sarakhs, Burnes found a khirgah (felt tent) twenty-five feet across with lattice sides, a roof of laths springing from a hoop three feet wide, and floors spread with felt and velvety carpets woven by the women. His host told him the whole house could go on one camel and its furniture on another.

Alexander Burnes (1834)

The nameKhorasan, near MashhadSeptember 1832

Villages emptied by 'the Tartars'

Tibris, a fine walled village, contains very few inhabitants, who are harassed by the Tartars. Some time ago it was destroyed by the Alamans, and most of the residents made slaves. On our approach, the villagers ran up to us, shedding a flood of tears

Editorial context

Near Mashhad the caravan passed villages wrecked by Turkmen raiders. At Tibris, which Mohan Lal says was 'harassed by the Tartars', most of the people had been carried off as slaves, and the survivors ran up weeping to ask the travellers for news of their captive families. Here 'Tartars' simply means the Turkmen slave-raiders.

Mohan Lal (1846 edition)

Daily lifeMerv to Bukhara1844

Turkmen elders who could advise but not punish

The Turkomauns my guides were, in the strictest sense of the word, masterless : for their Aga Sakals, ' lords with the beard,' have only a right to give advice, and to conduct them on their plundering expeditions, but they have no power to punish.

Editorial context

Wolff describes his Turkmen guides as truly 'masterless': their greybeards (aqsaqals) could give advice and lead raids, but had no power to punish anyone.

Joseph Wolff

Daily lifeMerv1832

Turkmens pray without mosques

All the Jews of Turkistaun assert that the Turkomauns are the descendants of Togarmah, one of the sons of Gomer, mentioned in Genesis x. 3. The Turkomauns have no mosque; they pray apart from each other, either in the field or the tent. Twice in the year they assemble in the desert, and prefer their prayer.

Editorial context

On his first journey Wolff noted that the Turkmens had no mosques, praying alone in the open or in their tents and gathering twice a year in the desert for common prayer. He also records a claim by the Jews of Turkestan that the Turkmens descend from the biblical Togarmah.

Joseph Wolff

BuildingsMervabout 1822

At Merv, only the burnt brick survived

there are no ruins to prove the existence of the marble palaces, and baths, and tombs, which have been vaguely attributed to it ; the chief remains are those of a fine large mosque, with a medressa, built by Timoor Shah ; of the mausoleum of Sooltaun Sanjer, and of a fine arsenal, all of which having been constructed of burnt brick, have partly escaped

Editorial context

Fraser doubts the tales of marble palaces at Merv. His informants said the main surviving ruins were a mosque and college credited to Timur, the tomb of the Seljuk sultan Sanjar and an arsenal, which lasted because they were built of fired brick.

James Baillie Fraser

CitiesMerv1840

Merv abandoned when its dam broke

Merv was one of the most ancient cities of Asia. … Merv has probably changed masters as often as any city in the world. … During the misrule and anarchy of the last sixty years the ancient dam of the Moorghaub was neglected and carried away. The city in consequence became uninhabitable, and was utterly abandoned.

Editorial context

Abbott gives a short history of Merv: founded, he says, by fire-worshippers, long Persian, and fought over by Turkic tribes. He explains that the old city emptied when the dam on the Murghab river was neglected and swept away during decades of disorder, leaving a deserted ruin beside a new village of mud huts.

James Abbott
See the original page (p. 51)

Keep in mind
