The nameTabriz1844

Twin brothers named Mogul and Tatar

Muhammed, son of Izhak Khan Kerahe, Tatar of the family of Ghengis Khan. The ancestors of Ghengis Khan were Oolinjah Khan and Olamgoo, a Mogul, who had twins : the name of the one was Mogul Khan, and the other Tatar Khan, from whom all the Tatars descend, as the Moguls do from Mogul Khan.

Editorial context

In a Tabriz prison Wolff met a captive chieftain who wrote out his family tree for him. It calls him a 'Tatar of the family of Genghis Khan' and gives a traditional origin story: an ancestor had twin sons, Mogul Khan and Tatar Khan, from whom the Moguls and the Tatars descend.

Muhammad Khan Qarai (in Wolff's translation of his autograph)

Keep in mind

Daily lifeTabrizc. 1306 to 1321

Spices come by the Tartar road through Baghdad and Tabriz

from this side, namely the dominion of the Tartars, Christian merchants will be able to direct their steps as far as India, since there are many who have already gone and come back. ... it should be known that from the parts of the Tartars, namely from Baghdad and from Tabriz, for a long time past and at present almost all goods of small weight and great price are carried to the Mediterranean Sea

Working translation

Original wording

ab hac parte scilicet dominii Tartarorum, vsque in Indiam ipsi mercatores Christiani poterunt dirigere gressus suos, cùm multi sint, qui iam iuerunt & redierunt. … sciendum est quod à partibus Tartarorum, scilicet à Baldach & à Thorisio, à magno tempore citra conducta sunt & cōducuntur ad præsens ad Mare Mediterraneum, quasi omnia mercimonia modici ponderis, & magni prætii

Editorial context

Sanudo, a Venetian with merchant experience, argues that the Pope should ban trade with Mamluk Egypt because goods can come instead through the lands of the Tartars. Christian merchants, he says, can travel from the Tartar dominions all the way to India, and many have gone and come back, whereas the Sultan lets no Christian pass; light, precious spices such as cloves, nutmeg and mace already reach the Mediterranean via Baghdad and Tabriz.

Marino Sanudo Torsello (Liber secretorum fidelium crucis, c. 1321; ed. Bongars, Hanau 1611)

Daily lifeTabrizc. 1288 to 1300

Women who ride into town with bow and arrows

and it is they who govern the kingdom or the household, and they buy and sell, and they are fierce and warlike. They ride like men, and I often saw them entering cities girded with bow and arrows like men.

Working translation

Original wording

et ipse sunt, que gubernant regnum vel domum, et emunt et vendunt, et sunt feroces et bellicose. Equitant autem vt viri, et frequenter vidi eas intrantes ciuitates cum arcu et sagittis accinctas vt viros.

Editorial context

Riccoldo says Tartar women are held in great honour, run the realm or the household, buy and sell, and are fierce and warlike. He adds from his own eyes that they ride like men and that he often saw them entering cities armed with bows and arrows. Elsewhere on the same page he explains their tall crowns, topped with a foot to remind them they are still under their husbands.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1873)

The nameTabrizc. 1320s

'Saracenized Tartars' in Persia

This Persia is inhabited by Saracens and by Saracenized Tartars, and by schismatic Christians of various sects, that is Nestorians, Jacobites, Greeks, Georgians and Armenians, and by a few Jews.

Working translation

Original wording

Ista Persida habitatur per Saracenos et per Tartaros saracenatos, atque per Christianos schismaticos diversarum sectarum, seu per Nestorianos, Jacobitas, Græcos, Georgianos, Armenos, et per paucos Judæos.

Editorial context

Jordanus, who lived in Tabriz, lists Persia's peoples: Saracens (Muslims), 'Saracenized Tartars', and Christians of many sects, plus a few Jews. The phrase captures the Mongol rulers of Persia after their conversion to Islam around 1295. He calls their ruler simply 'the emperor of Persia'.

Jordanus Catalani of Séverac, O.P. (Latin text, ed. Coquebert de Montbret, Recueil de voyages IV, Paris 1839)

The nameTabrizabout 1340

'Moccoli, that is, Tartar brigands'

And for the extortion that the Moccoli, that is, Tartar brigands, practise along the road

Working translation

Original wording

E per forza che fanno i Moccoli, cioè Tartari Scherani per cammino

Editorial context

Listing the tolls on the road from Ayas in Lesser Armenia to Tabriz, the capital of the 'lord of the Tartars' in Persia, Pegolotti adds a last charge for the extortion of the 'Moccoli, that is, Tartar brigands' along the way. 'Moccoli' is the Italian merchants' form of 'Mongols', and he treats it as a plain synonym for Tartars. In the Cathay chapter he likewise warns that the road beyond Tana is full of Moccoli, 'that is, armed men'.

Francesco Balducci Pegolotti (ed. G. F. Pagnini, 1766)

The nameTabriz, Azerbaijan (Iran)c.1230

'The Tatars are exceedingly numerous'

He sent an envoy to each of them and gave news of the Mongols' coming; the substance of the message was that the Tatars are exceedingly numerous, and this time more than ever before.

Working translation

Original wording

پیش هریك رسول فرستاد و از حال وصول مغول خبر داد و مضمون پیغام آنك تاتار بغایت بسیارند و این نوبت زیادت از هر بار

Editorial context

When a large Mongol army under Chormaghun crossed the Oxus, Sultan Jalal al-Din of Khwarazm, though on bad terms with the caliph and the rulers of Anatolia and Syria, sent envoys to each of them. Rashid writes 'Mughul' (Mongol) in his own voice, but gives the substance of the warning as 'the Tatars are exceedingly numerous'. The same army is Mongol in the narrative and Tatar in the alarm.

Rashid al-Din (Persian text ed. E. Blochet, 1911)
