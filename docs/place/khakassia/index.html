Strange talesAbakan1822

Siberian inscriptions that look European

Klaproth admits that the letters on the inscribed stones of South Siberia look more European than Asian, with shapes like Greek and Slavonic letters. He warns that this does not mean they were carved after the Russian conquest, since many stand on ancient gravestones. The script was still undeciphered in his day.

On examining the characters of these inscriptions, one cannot hide from oneself that they look more European than Asian. One easily recognises several Greek and Slavonic letters. Yet one cannot reasonably conclude from this that these inscriptions are later than the Russian conquest of Siberia

Working translation

Original wording

En examinant les caractères de ces inscriptions, on ne peut pas se dissimuler qu'ils ont plutôt un air européen qu'asiatique. On y reconnaît facilement plusieurs lettres grecques et esclavonnes. Cependant on ne peut raisonnablement conclure pour cela que ces inscriptions soient postérieures à la conquête de la Sibérie par les Russes

Who ancient inhabitants of South Siberia

Read itJulius Klaproth

Keep in mind. These are the Yenisei runic inscriptions, later shown (1893) to be in an Old Turkic script; the likeness to Greek letters is only in shape.

Strange talesAbakan steppe near Askiz1772

Feeding butter to a stone woman's mouth

On the Abakan steppe, crowded with ancient graves fenced with stone slabs, Pallas found carved standing stones which, like other stone images on the Yenisei steppes, were said to have been set up long before the time of the Kirgis. One, a tall bellied stone with a large open-mouthed woman's face, was called Kurtejak-tash, the 'woman stone'. Hunters heading out for sable prayed to it for luck and smeared fat or butter into its mouth.

This stone is known among the heathen Tatars of this region by the name Kurtejak-tash (woman stone) or Ulu-Kurtejak (the great woman), and especially in autumn it is earnestly asked for luck by the heathens travelling past to the sable hunt, and fed with a little fat or butter, which the worshippers devoutly smear into the open mouth of the face.

Working translation

Original wording

Diese Wake ist unter den Nahmen Kurtejaktasch (Weiberstein) oder Ulu-Kurtejak (das grosse Weib) bey den heydnischen Tataren dieser Gegend bekannt, und wird sonderlich im Herbst, von den auf die Zobeljagd vorbey reisenden Heyden, fleißig um Glück angesprochen und mit etwas Fett oder Butter, welches die Anbeter in den ofnen Mund des Gesichts andächtig schmieren, gefuttert.

Who Heathen Tatars of the Abakan (Beltirs and other Khakas)

BuildingsAbakan steppe (Khakassia)early 1790s

Steppe full of ancient graves of the 'Chud', an unknown people

Crossing the 'Tatar steppe' to the Abakansk fort on the Yenisei, the botanist Sievers found the whole area full of ancient grave monuments of a people still unknown, whom Siberians simply called Chud. He saw no inscriptions on the many standing stones. Nearby, he notes, Abakansk had been built in the previous century with palisades and five small cannon against raids by Tatars and Kirgiz.

On the whole Tatar steppe and round about here everything is full of very ancient grave monuments of a nation unknown until now, which in Siberia are simply called Chud. On the many standing stones I saw inscriptions nowhere.

Working translation

Original wording

Auf der ganzen tatarischen Steppe und hier herum ist alles voll von uralten Grabmälern von einer bis jetzt unbekannten Nation, die nur schlechthin in Sibirien Tschudi genannt werden. An den vielen aufgerichteten Steinen sah ich nirgends Inschriften.

Who Unknown ancient builders ('Chud'); Sagai and other steppe Tatars living there now

BuildingsMinusinsk, Krasnoyarsk Krai1829

Kurgan graves of 'a race now extinct'

At Krasnoyarsk Erman studied a collection of bronze objects dug from the kurgans (burial mounds) of the Minusinsk district, including round mirrors decorated with animals. The local Tatars told him the mounds belonged to 'a race now extinct, and totally different from theirs'. Erman noted that similar mirrors were still used in Buryat Buddhist rites, and wondered whether the builders were really so different from living Siberians.

The most important consideration, however, is, that these mirrors are found in graves which, as the present Tatar inhabitants of the circle maintain, belong to a race now extinct, and totally different from theirs.

Who Tatars of the Minusinsk basin (Khakas); unknown ancient builders

Saw itAdolph Erman (translated by W. D. Cooley, 1848)

Keep in mind. Erman saw the objects in a private collection, not the graves; the 'extinct race' is local Tatar belief. Modern archaeology links most Minusinsk bronzes to Bronze and Iron Age cultures (such as Tagar) long before the Turkic-speaking Khakas.

Daily lifeKhakassia (Yenisei-Abakan steppe)1772

A Kachin shaman in ribbons and printed cotton

Pallas saw a young Kachin shaman perform at the Karysh mine. The man was said to have been mad, or to have played mad, for years before taking up the craft, and still sometimes 'witched' all night until he collapsed. As a beginner he had no drum, only a paddle-shaped rattle stick; a shaman earned the right to a drum by sacrificing a horse and stretching its hide over it.

I saw the costume of a young Kachin sorcerer, who for many years before taking up the trade had been mad or had pretended to be mad, at Karysh… He performed his tricks without a cap and wore only brightly embroidered leather stockings and on his body a tight, very dirty smock of brightly printed cotton cloth (kitaika).

Original wording

Ich habe den Aufzug eines jungen Katschinzischen Zauberers, der viele Jahre, ehe er das Handwerk angefangen, närrisch gewesen oder sich närrisch angestellt hatte, in Karysch gesehn… Er gauckelte ohne Mütze und hatte nur bunt ausgenähte lederne Strümpfe und am Leibe einen engen, ganz beschmutzten Kittel, von bunt gedrucktem baumwollenen Zeuge (Kitaika) an

BuildingsSagai steppe (Khakassia)early 1790s

Birch-bark yurts gleaming white on the Sagai steppe

Staying some days with the Tatars by Lake Ilkul on the Sagai steppe, Sievers praises their fine horses and cattle, sold to merchants as far as Moscow, notes that traders always bring brandy because 'one bargains better with a drunk Tatar than a sober one', and says he saw no sign of religion. Their yurts, he writes, were clean and roomy and all built of birch bark, shining a dazzling white against the green meadows and hills.

Their yurts are clean, roomy, and all built of birch bark, which in sunshine give off a dazzling whiteness into the distance and contrast most pleasantly with the green of the meadows and hills.

Working translation

Original wording

Ihre Jurten sind reinlich, geräumig, und alle von Birkenrinde gebauet, die bey Sonnenschein eine blendende Weisse in … der Ferne von sich geben und aufs Angenehmste mit dem … Grün der Wiesen und Berge contrastiren.

Who Sagai Tatars (Khakas)

Saw itJohann Sievers, letters from Siberia, in Neueste Nordische Beyträge vol. 7 (1796), edited by P. S. Pallas

BuildingsAbakan1822

Stone statues left by Cumans and other Turks

Presenting drawings of inscribed stone columns near Abakan on the upper Yenisei, Klaproth notes that they closely resemble the stone statues scattered over the steppe north of the Caucasus. Those, he says, are the only monuments the Cumans and other Turkic peoples left behind there.

Moreover, these same statues closely resemble those seen fairly often on the vast plains north of the Caucasus, between the Black Sea and the Caspian, which are the only monuments that the Cumans and other Turkic peoples have left us of their stay in those regions.

Working translation

Original wording

D'ailleurs, ces mêmes statues ressemblent beaucoup à celles qu'on voit assez fréquemment dans les vastes plaines situées au nord du Caucase, entre la mer Noire et la mer Caspienne, et qui sont les seuls monumens que les Comans et autres peuples turcs nous ont laissés de leur séjour dans ces contrées.

Who ancient Turkic peoples of South Siberia; Cumans of the steppe

Read itJulius Klaproth (paper read to the Asiatic Society, 2 December 1822)

Keep in mind. Klaproth worked from drawings made by others (Müller's artist Lursenius, Strahlenberg, Pallas), not from the stones themselves.

The nameYenisei (Khakassia)1895 (about 6th to 16th century)

'Abakan Tatars': the Kirghiz' descendants in 1895

Radloff notes that the Yenisei inscriptions were left mostly by the Kirghiz, and that their descendants still form a large part of the Turkic population there, 'the Abakan Tatars and Soyots'. In the nineteenth century Russian usage called many Siberian Turkic groups 'Tatars'. The same people who carved runic stones in the early Middle Ages were called Tatars by the time Radloff wrote.

When the Russians came to know them in the sixteenth century in the southern part of the Yenisei province, the Kirghiz were complete Turks, and even now descendants of the Kirghiz form a large part of the Turkic population of that area, the Abakan Tatars and Soyots.

Working translation

Original wording

Als die Russen sie im XVI. Jahrhundert im südlichen Theile des Jenisseischen Gouvernements kennen lernten, waren die Kirgisen vollkommene Türken und noch jetzt bilden Nachkommen der Kirgisen einen grossen Theil der türkischen Bevölkerung desselben Gebietes, der Abakan-Tataren und Sojonen.

Who Kirghiz (Khakas), called Abakan Tatars in Radloff's time

Read itWilhelm Radloff, Die alttuerkischen Inschriften der Mongolei (1895)

Keep in mind. Radloff's own ethnographic judgement; his claim that the Kirghiz were first a non-Turkic people rests on a Chinese remark about red hair and blue eyes.

Daily lifeKhakassia (Yenisei-Abakan steppe)1772

Kachin weddings and the bride who must lie flat

Pallas reports that the Kachin Tatars, rich herders who had not taken up farming or Christianity, keep one wife, court for years, and hold two weddings. The groom's father must set up a new yurt for the couple, while the bride's father supplies the household goods. After the betrothal the father-in-law never sees his son's wife again, and if they meet she must throw herself flat on the ground until he has passed.

The first wedding is held at the bride's father's, the second at the groom's, where the groom's father must set up a new yurt for the young couple… Except at the first wooing the father-in-law never sees his son's wife again, never goes into the son's yurt, and if he does meet his daughter-in-law, custom requires that she throw herself flat on the ground.

Working translation

Original wording

Die erste Hochzeit wird beym Brautvater, die andre beym Bräutigam gefeyert, wo des Bräutigams-Vater dem jungen Paar eine neue Jurte aufstellen muß… Ausser bey der ersten Freywerberey sieht der Schwiegervater seines Sohns Frau niemals wieder, geht nie in die Jurte des Sohns und geschieht es ja, daß er seiner Schwiegertochter begegnet, so will die Gewohnheit, daß sich diese platt auf die Erde niederwirft

Who Kachin Tatars (Khakas)

Heard itPeter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)
