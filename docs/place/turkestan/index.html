Mongol and Tatar: twin sons, and a name that swallowed the rest

Katib Celebi tells the Islamic-Persian genealogy: the children of Turk son of Japheth spread over Turkestan, most of them nomads living in tents. Later Alinja Khan had twin sons, Mongol and Tatar, who divided the kingdom, so two clans arose. Eight Tatar rulers reigned before their line died out, and the Mongol line followed. Then comes a clear statement of the naming problem: 'Mongol' became a generic name for many tribes, so that by extension people say 'Turk', 'Tatar' and 'Mongol' for all of them, although the Mongols were really one kind of Turk.

From one womb he had two sons, one named Mongol and the other Tatar, and he divided his whole kingdom between these two sons … The said word became a generic name for many tribes and peoples, and it is applied by extension even to those who are not of them, so they say Turk and Tatar and Mongol; but in truth the Mongols were one kind of Turk.

Strange talesTurkestan (Hazrat-e Turkestan), Kazakhstan1750s (reported)

The 'tailed Tatars' of Turkestan

The interpreter Aralov, who had often been to the town of Turkestan, told Rychkov of a despised clan of under a hundred families nicknamed the 'tailed Tatars'. The story went that their ancestors killed the son of a local saint, who cursed them so they could not ride horses, and they grew a tail-like mark. Aralov swore he had seen such a mark on a naked child. Rychkov leaves it to the reader: 'whether this is true or not'. His real point follows: there the name Tatar is an insult, and the Tatars of Orenburg and Kazan are called Nogais.

he cursed them with a prayer, so that they should be unable to ride on horseback, and supposedly from that time they received on their bodies a mark like a tail … The said interpreter declares that he himself saw one child of those people naked, and assures that it really had such a mark behind. Whether this is true or not is left to free judgment.

Daily lifeKazakh steppe; Turkestan (Hazrat-e Turkestan)1820s

Winter dead hung in trees until spring

Levshin reports that in some clans of the Middle and Great Hordes, rich people who died in winter were not buried at once but hung in trees wrapped in felt or linen, and in spring carried to Turkestan to be buried near the shrine of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi, the Kazakhs' revered saint. He then explains how graves were dug with a side niche so the earth would not press on the body.

Rich people who die in winter, in some clans of the Middle and Great Horde, instead of being buried are hung in trees, wrapped in felt or linen; and in spring they are carried to Turkestan

The nameTurkestanLegendary antiquity (told as by Timur, c.1350s)

Tatar and Moghul, twin sons of a Turkish king

Timur asks his father for the family history, and hears the Turkic origin legend: the Turks descend from Yafet Aghlan, son of the biblical Japhet, and a later king had twin sons named Tatar and Moghul who split Turkestan between them. Tatar's eight sons and Moghul's nine founded the tribes, who then fought each other on the plains. The same story is told by the Khivan khan and historian Abu'l-Ghazi in the seventeenth century.

when his fifth son Aljeh Khan ascended the throne, the all gracious God bestowed on him twin sons, one of which was called Tatar, the other Moghul ; when they were grown up, Aljeh Khan divided the kingdom of Turkestan between them during his life-time

Who Turks, Tatars and Mongols (as legendary ancestors)

Legend or rumourAttributed to Timur (Persian text of Abu Talib al-Husayni, translated by Charles Stewart, 1830)

Keep in mind. A genealogical legend, retold in a work whose authorship is disputed.

The nameTurkestan (Uzbekistan and Xinjiang)c. 1809

'The name of Tartary is unknown in those regions'

Setting out the geography north of the Hindu Kush, Elphinstone divides the lands into Turkestan (west of the Pamirs) and 'Chinese Turkestan' (east of them), using the local name for Turk-speaking country. Then he adds a blunt note: the people who live there do not call it Tartary. The word was a European map label, not a local one.

All the country west of Beloot Taugh, and north of the Oxus, is called Toorkistaun, a term which may be extended to the east of Beloot Taugh, as far as there is reason to think the Turkish language is spoken … The name of Tartary is unknown in those regions.

Who Uzbeks and other Turkic peoples of Central Asia

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

Keep in mind. Based on what his Central Asian informants told him, not on a visit.

Daily lifeTransoxiana, the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestanc.1400

Chinggis Khan's law set above Islam

Ibn Arabshah says Timur spoke Persian, Turkish and Mongol but no Arabic, and held to the 'Chinggis Khan rules' (the yasa), treating them like branches of Islamic law. So, he says, did all the Chaghatay, the people of the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestan, whom he calls rabble. For this reason, he adds, leading jurists declared Timur an unbeliever.

He held to the rules of Chinggis Khan, which are like the branches of jurisprudence in the Islamic faith … And so do all the Chaghatay and the people of the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestan, that rabble: all of them put the rules of the accursed Chinggis Khan ahead of the rules of Islam.

The nameBeijing; Turkestan; Sarai; Tabriz1307

One great khan in Cathay and three Tartar kings

Hayton closes his history with the Tartar world as it stood when he wrote: a great emperor, Temur Khan, sixth of the line, ruling from the city of Jong in Cathay, and three other Tartar kings, Chapar in Turkestan, Toqta in Comania and Kharbanda (Oljeitu) in Tabriz, who all owe him reverence and take their quarrels to his court. It is a clear picture of 'Tartary' as a family of Mongol states, not a single city or civilisation.

The great emperor of the Tartars, the one who now holds the lordship, is named Temur Khan, and he is the sixth emperor. He holds the seat of his empire in the kingdom of Cathay, in a very great city called Jong … There are also three other kings of the Tartars, who have very great power, and all pay reverence and obedience to the great emperor.

The nameEast Turkestan (Xinjiang)1870

Vambery: East Turkestan, 'also called Chinese Tartary'

In his introduction Vambery places the Uighur homeland in 'today's East Turkestan or Chinese Tartary, as it is also called'. For a nineteenth-century scholar, 'Tartary' is just an older map name for the region where the Kutadgu Bilig was written, next to the modern term. He also claims Samarkand and Karshi as Uighur foundations from the meaning of their names, an etymological guess.

If we therefore describe the homeland of the Uighurs as present-day East Turkestan, or Chinese Tartary as it is also called, it must be noted that some colonies lay outside these borders.

Read itHermann Vambery, introduction to his edition Uigurische Sprachmonumente und das Kudatku Bilik (Innsbruck 1870)
