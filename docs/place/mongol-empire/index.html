The khan strikes the red-hot iron

To remember the escape from Irgana-kon, Abu'l Ghazi says, the Mongols held a yearly ceremony: a piece of iron was heated red-hot, the khan struck it first with a hammer, then the tribal chiefs and officers in order of rank, then the common people. It ties Mongol kingship to smithing and iron.

They kindle upon that Day a great Fire, wherein they put a Piece of Iron to make it red-hot; after which the Chan gives the first Stroke with a Hammer upon the Piece of Iron, and then all the Heads of the Tribes and other Officers of Distinction, each according to his Rank, give it a Stroke with a Hammer

Keep in mind. He says the Mongols 'still' observe it, but gives no date or place for the rite.

BuildingsMongol empire (general)13th century (with older Turkic material)

Only craftsmen spared; 'houses of the dead' for nobles

Describing Mongol ways of war and burial, Hammer says they killed all prisoners of war except the craftsmen, whom they dragged off as slaves: a key point for who built things in the Mongol empire. Princes' funerals included the killing of concubines and captives; burial places were kept secret, but those of the great were marked by stone monuments or 'houses of the dead'. A stallion, a mare and a foal went into the grave so the dead would ride and have mare's milk in the next life, and a stuffed horse hide was set up over the grave.

Prisoners taken in war they killed, all except the craftsmen, whom they dragged off with them as slaves. … Their burial place was kept secret, but that of the nobles was marked by stone monuments or houses of the dead. A stallion, a mare and a foal were given to the dead man in the grave, so that in the other life he would at once have a mount and not lack mare's milk.

Strange talesMongol empire13th century (told 1819)

Subjects of five colours, Amazons and dog-heads

Listing the peoples conquered from Chinggis to Khubilai, the chronicle gives the 'five colours': blue Mongols, red Chinese, black Tibetans, yellow Sartagul (Central Asian Muslims) and white Solongos (Koreans). Then come four more peoples: Amazons, one other, people with an eye on the chest, and dog-headed people. Old wonder-lore sits beside a real map of the empire.

The blue Mongols, over four hundred thousand households; the red Chinese; the black Tibetans; the yellow Sartagul; the white Solongos... and four further peoples: the Tsug-te Amazons, the Yon-ru, those with one eye on the chest, and those with dogs' heads.

Daily lifeMongol empire (general)13th century

Felt idols, the god Natagai, and a smiths' New Year

Hammer gathers the old Mongol religion from Rubruck, Marco Polo and Rashid al-Din: idols of felt and silk, offered the heart of each slaughtered animal; prayer facing south; worship of sun, stars and elements, with food and drink dedicated before meals; a chief god Natagai honoured with his whole family; libations to the four quarters outside the tent. He lists three yearly feasts: the smiths' festival recalling the exodus from Ergene Kun, the khan's birthday, and the herd festival with mare's milk poured for Khubilai's ten thousand white horses. In a footnote he quotes Rashid al-Din that on New Year's night Chinggis Khan's family hammered iron on an anvil and gave thanks.

The Mongols had idols of felt and silk. When they slaughtered an animal, the heart was offered to the idols, and they prayed facing south, as the Muslims face the Kaaba. They worshipped the sun and the stars together with the elements … Their principal god was called Natagai, and he was worshipped with his whole family by all Tatars.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Mongolen hatten Götzenbilder aus Filz und Seide. Wenn sie ein Thier schlachteten, wurde den Götzenbildern das Herz dargebracht, und sie betheten gegen Mittag gewandt, wie die Moslimen gegen die Kaaba. Sie betheten die Sonne und die Sterne sammt den Elementen an … Ihr vorzüglicher Gott hiess Natagai, und ward mit seiner ganzen Familie von allen Tataren verehrt.

Who Mongols ('all Tatars') before their conversion to Islam

Read itJoseph von Hammer-Purgstall (1840), compiling Rubruck, Marco Polo and Rashid al-Din

Keep in mind. A patchwork of travellers' reports; Schmidt objected in the margin that no trace of the smiths' feast survived among Mongols, and Hammer answered with Rashid al-Din.

Daily lifeMongol Empirec. 1275-1292

Units of ten, blood from a horse's vein and dried milk

Polo explains the decimal army (officers over ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand, the 'toman'), scouts on every side, and how little gear a rider carried: two leather milk bottles, a small pot and a small tent. In emergencies they rode ten days without a fire, drinking blood from their horses' veins, and carried dried milk paste that churned into a meal inside the bottle as they rode.

And in case of great urgency they will ride ten days on end without lighting a fire or taking a meal. On such an occasion they will sustain themselves on the blood of their horses, opening a vein and letting the blood jet into their mouths, drinking till they have had enough, and then staunching it.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Keep in mind. Blood-drinking is also reported by Herberstein, Schiltberger and others; Polo describes the practice rather than a scene he witnessed.

Daily lifeMongol empire (Ilkhanate)late 13th century

Raised on black felt: a khan election Hayton saw twice

Hayton says the Tartars still make their lord the way they made Genghis: the whole people gathers in a field, the chosen man is seated on a black felt, the nobles and Genghis's kin lift him high and set him on a rich throne, and all kneel to him. He adds that he himself was present at two such elections, and marvels that for all their conquests they never changed their first custom.

When they want to elect their lord (and I have been twice at the election of the emperor of the Tartars, and saw how all the Tartars gathered in a great field), they seated the one who was to be their lord on a black felt, and set a rich throne in their midst.

Daily lifeMongol Empire and Chinac. 1275-1292

Ghost weddings for dead children

Polo reports that when a boy and a girl died unmarried, their families arranged a marriage between them, drew up a contract, burned it so the couple would know in the next world, and burned paper pictures of the dowry. Yule notes this is really a Chinese custom, though he trusts Polo that Mongols practised it too.

And marry them they do, making a regular contract ! And when the contract papers are made out they put them in the fire, in order (as they will have it) that the parties in the other world may know the fact, and so look on each other as man and wife.
