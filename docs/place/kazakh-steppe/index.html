Kumiss in the tent of a Kazakh chief

Travelling in Tartar dress with Kazakh guides, the factors reached the horde of the chief Jean Beek (Janibek Batyr). He sat on a carpet in his tent, rose to welcome them, drank a large dish of kumiss and made them drink too. The next page describes Kazakh felt tents, horse meat, drunkenness on fermented mare's milk, the dried cheese called 'crute', and their rule of never harming a guest while he is under protection.

This Tartar-chief was sitting on a carpet in his tent; he rose, and bid us welcome, and making us sit down with him, he took a large dish of kumees, which he drank, and made us do the same. We gave him some small presents, which he immediately divided amongst the company.

Winter dead hung in trees until spring

Levshin reports that in some clans of the Middle and Great Hordes, rich people who died in winter were not buried at once but hung in trees wrapped in felt or linen, and in spring carried to Turkestan to be buried near the shrine of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi, the Kazakhs' revered saint. He then explains how graves were dug with a side niche so the earth would not press on the body.

Rich people who die in winter, in some clans of the Middle and Great Horde, instead of being buried are hung in trees, wrapped in felt or linen; and in spring they are carried to Turkestan

The nameKazakh steppe1840

Kazakh, Kirghiz or Cossack?

Abbott explains a naming tangle: the steppe people between Khiva and Orenburg call themselves Kazakhs (Kuzzauks), but the Russians call them Kirghiz to tell them apart from Russia's own Christian Cossacks. The people themselves, he says, do not know the name Kirghiz.

The kingdom of Khaurism is separated from the Russian district of Orenburgh by a considerable belt of steppe, held by Kuzzauks, whose chief, or Sooltaun, is nominally tributary to Russia. The Russians call this people Kirgheez, to distinguish them from their own Kuzzauks, or Cossacks, who are Christians, but the name is unknown to the people themselves.

'Nogai' graves of heaped stone, with gold horse-trappings inside

Besides their own cemeteries, Levshin says, the Kazakh steppes hold many ancient graves that the Kazakhs attribute to the Nogais who once lived there. These stand out for their height and size, often being huge heaps of stones brought from far away, and sometimes contain gold and silver horse-harness ornaments and coins. The attribution is Kazakh tradition; archaeologists now date many such kurgans to far older peoples.

Besides the cemeteries belonging to the Kirghiz-Kazakhs, in their steppes there are many ancient graves which they attribute to the Nogais, who once lived in this country. The Nogai graves are distinguished by their height and extent, and often consist of huge heaps of stones brought from far away; sometimes gold and silver ornaments for horse harness, coins and so on are found in them.

Grey felt for the poor, red cloth for sultans, reeds for the poorest

After describing the Kazakh yurt (a round tent of wooden lattice covered with felt, with a smoke hole at the top, 'exactly like the Kalmyk ones', 4 to 8 arshins high), Levshin shows how its covering marked rank. Ordinary Kazakhs used grey felt, the rich and noble white felt, some great sultans red broadcloth lined with silk, and the very poorest covered their homes with matting, turf and reeds.

The kibitkas of ordinary Kirghiz are usually made of grey felts; the noble and rich use white felts for this; and some powerful sultans of the Middle and Great Horde have yurts covered with red broadcloth and lined with silk fabrics. By contrast, the very poorest cover their dwellings, instead of felt, with matting, turf and reeds.

Inside a Kazakh sultan's tent

At the Ilek River the embassy met its first Kazakh camp: about 50 tents of white or brown felt. Meyendorff then visited the sultan's large round tent, which mixed luxury and plain nomad gear: carpets, tiger skins, a woman's tall gold headdress set with turquoise and rubies, raw meat on hooks and leather bags of mare's milk.

the floor was covered with a carpet, wearing apparel hung on a cord, and skins of tigers were stretched against the sides; a rich diadem of gold, very high, and ornamented with turquoise and rubies, the head dress of Kirghiz women ; by their side might also be seen raw meat hanging on hooks, large leather bags of mare's milk, and some wooden vessels of different kinds

Clay tombs that looked like cities

The nomadic Kazakhs built lasting tombs of clay mixed with chopped straw, sometimes of unburnt brick, with a small prayer chamber inside. Rich families brought craftsmen from Bukhara to make them. Near the Syr Darya they were so many that from a distance they looked like cities.

All the tombs are of clay, mixed with chopped straw, except that of Khodja, which is of unburnt bricks. In the interior of these sepulchres there is another, smaller, where the Kirghiz pray. These monuments become more numerous as you approach the Sir, and appear like cities. The rich Kirghiz frequently bring artificers from Bokhara to make their clay tombs

'Kirghiz-Kaisak' is a foreign name: they call themselves Kazak

Levshin opens his history by saying that Europe gives the Kazakhs a name neither they nor their neighbours use. They call themselves Kazak, and Persians, Bukharans and Khivans know them by that name; the Chinese say Hasak. Russia too called them the Kazakh horde until the 18th century, when the prefix 'Kirghiz' was added, partly to tell them apart from Russia's own Cossacks.

and that to this day they call themselves nothing but Kazakhs (Kazak). Under this same name they are known to the Persians, Bukharans, Khivans and other peoples of Asia. The Chinese, softening the initial K, say Hasak. Until the 18th century Russia too did not know 'Kirghiz-Kaisaks', but called them Kazakhs, the Kazakh horde.

A brick 'Tatar prayer-house' whose builders were forgotten

Pallas rode out into the steppe beyond the Orenburg line to see a building the Russians called the Tatar Koshena. It was a square brick shrine with a pointed Gothic portal and a twelve-sided funnel dome, standing in a Kazakh cemetery. He thought it was not very old, yet neither the Kazakhs nor the Bashkirs remembered who had built it. On a following page he notes it is all of red brick, much like ordinary European brick.

It is still in perfectly good condition and, at least to judge by the woodwork inside, seems to be of no extraordinary antiquity. Nevertheless the memory of its building has been lost among the Kirgis as well as the Bashkirs.

'We are Kazakhs, not Kirghiz'

Meyendorff noticed that the steppe people the Russians called Kirghiz never used that name themselves. They called themselves Kazakh, which some said meant 'horseman' and others 'warrior', and they believed the Bashkirs had given them the name Kirghiz.

they never make use of this name among themselves ; they invariably style themselves Kasak ; which, according to some, signifies horseman, and to others warrior. They say the Bachkirs call them Kirghiz, but are ignorant from whence the name is derived

Steppe cemeteries of stone and clay: pyramids, towers, a khan's domed tomb

Levshin calls a Kazakh cemetery one of the most curious sights for a traveller: after the empty steppe the eye rests on pyramids, towers and high enclosures built of stone and clay, spears hung with horsehair and ribbons, yurt lattices, stone or wooden turbans, a saddle, bow and arrows on a famous rider's grave. Over the grave of Abulkhair Khan stood a square vaulted building. The Kazakhs, a nomadic people, built lasting monuments for their dead.

the eyes come to rest either in the shade of a tree, or on pyramids, towers and high enclosures built of stones and clay. In one place he sees spears with horse manes, ribbons and kerchiefs fluttering from them; in another, the lattices that served as the walls of a kibitka … Over the grave of Abulkhair Khan on the Olkeyek stream … a square vaulted building was built

BuildingsKazakh steppe1820s

Who built the ruins of the steppe? 'Not our ancestors'

Levshin's chapter on ruins begins by admitting that it is hard to say who built them, because they are of different kinds: some are Buddhist temples plainly built by Mongols or Dzungars, some look like mosques, some are ordinary Asian dwellings, some are too ruined to judge. Materials differ too: big stones, brick, clay. The Kazakhs, he says, only agree that the buildings were not their ancestors', and they call old graves and a few ruins 'Nogai'.

It is hard, and hardly even possible, to determine what people built these buildings, for they are of various kinds. Some, keeping signs of temples devoted to Lamaist worship, clearly belong to the Mongols or Dzungars; others resemble Muslim mosques; a third kind are remains of ordinary Asian dwellings … The present Kirghiz-Kazakhs in general say nothing plausible about them, except that they do not belong to their ancestors

Daily lifeKazakh steppe1820

'Houses mean slavery'

Meyendorff explains why well-off Kazakhs refused to farm: they feared becoming serfs tied to the soil and liked to say they were as free as the birds. An old saying warned that they would lose their freedom once they began living in houses and farming, and the fate of the Bashkirs under Russia seemed to prove it.

It is therefore easy to imagine why Kirghiz in easy circumstances never cultivate the ground … they have an ancient tradition which says "The Kirghiz shall lose their liberty whenever they begin to live in houses

Daily lifeIlek River, Kazakh steppe1820

A horse thief's punishment

A Kazakh horse thief was sentenced to death, but the elders asked for mercy as a good omen for the journey with the Russians. Instead he was blackened with soot, tied to a horse's tail and whipped by a laughing crowd, while his own horse was slaughtered and shared out among the onlookers.

The robber, half naked, having about his neck some stripes of black felt, was forced by two men on horseback who carried large whips, to run to the nearest tent, where his face was blackened with soot and marched through a group of Kirghiz; they then tied him to the tail of a horse, and forced him to hold a cord between his teeth.

Russia's Tatars are 'Nogais' to the Kazakhs; 'Tatar' is a cursed name

In a footnote to his chapter on ruins Levshin notes that Kazakhs call the Tatars living in Russia only 'Nogais'. The word 'Tatar' itself they did not use; in their view it belonged to some base people cursed by a Muslim prophet. Bukharans and Khivans thought the same. The same story was reported by Rychkov seventy years earlier (W175).

The Kirghiz-Kazakhs call the Tatars living in Russia nothing but Nogais. The (word) Tatar itself is not used by them at all, and in their opinion belongs to some base people cursed by a certain Muslim prophet. The Bukharans and Khivans think the same, and also call the Russian Tatars Nogais.

Horsehair and bullets left at a holy place

At the same brick shrine birch poles were hung with tufts of horsehair and rags, and the ledges held pebbles, lead bullets, an arrow and gunpowder. Pallas explains that the Kazakhs believe leaving hair from a horse's tail at a holy place helps the horse, and that leaving some small thing is a general Tatar custom. The Tatars of his own escort added offerings after praying.

For the Kirgis believe that it is good for their horse if some hair from its tail is left at so holy a place; and in general it is a Tatar custom, at such prayer-houses or other holy places they visit out of devotion, to leave behind something they carry with them.

Forty outlaw horsemen: a Kazakh origin legend

Among several Kazakh origin stories Levshin gives one from the journal of Captain Rychkov (1771): seven sons of a Crimean khan, cheated of their inheritance, went into the steppe, joined other exiles to make a band of forty, lived by raiding, stole wives and multiplied. Forty is kyrk in Turkic, so they were called Kyrk-Kazak, 'forty Cossacks' or riders. Levshin himself calls most such traditions absurd but prints them as he found them.

This band, forty men strong, for a long time lived by plundering the lands around them; at last it stole wives for itself and multiplied. Forty is expressed in Turkic by the word kyrk, and so this crowd was called Kyrk-Kazak, that is, forty Kazakhs, or riders. Their descendants kept the name of their ancestors.

A khan who rules by consent

A Kazakh khan held power of life and death, Meyendorff says, but his only real check was public opinion: unhappy followers simply rode off and chose another leader. A khan kept power through courage, gifts and a friendly mullah to interpret the law.

The khan has in fact the power of life and death among the Kirghiz, who have no security against his tyranny, except public opinion, and no where is it so powerful as among the pastoral tribes. The discontented party quickly desert an unjust judge, and choose another for themselves.

Muslims without mosques or mullahs

Levshin describes Kazakh Islam as loose: they count themselves Sunnis alongside Turks, Tatars, Bukharans and Khivans and practise polygamy, but do not keep the fasts or ablutions, find praying five times a day too hard, and have no mosques and no mullahs chosen from among themselves. Sometimes elders read prayers with others kneeling around them.

Fasts and ablution, a very sensible institution of Muhammad, the Kirghiz do not observe; they find praying five times a day hard for themselves; they have no mosques and no mullahs chosen from among themselves. Sometimes prayers are read by old men in the presence of many people kneeling around them

Seven wolves chasing two horses: the end of the world in the stars

In a chapter on Kazakh knowledge of the sky, Levshin says herdsmen navigated the trackless steppe by the stars and told time by them 'as a European looks at his pocket watch'. They saw the Great Bear as seven wolves chasing two horses, a white and a grey gelding; when the wolves catch and eat the horses, the world will end.

Not separating the stars that make up the Great Bear … the Kirghiz say that they consist of 7 wolves chasing two horses fleeing from them, Ak-buzat (the white gelding) and Kuk-buzat (the grey gelding), and that when the former catch the latter and eat them, the end of the world will come.
