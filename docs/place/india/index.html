Strange talesCentral Asia ('India' of Prester John)c. 1200 (legend)

Prester John's son cut to pieces, his daughter wed to Chinggis

Sanudo tells how the Tartars, once tributaries of 'David, son of Prester John, king of India', rose against him under Chinggis. Their best weapons were clubs with bows and arrows; most fought on foot, some rode oxen, a few rode asses and colts. David was cut limb from limb with all his family except one daughter, whom Chinggis took as wife. A blend of the Prester John legend with the real defeat of the Kereit ruler Toghrul.

Their best weapons were clubs, with bows and arrows: for the most part they were on foot; a smaller part sat on oxen; the smallest on asses and beasts of burden and colts. So King David, hearing of their coming ... was cut limb from limb with his whole family except his only daughter: which daughter Chinggis Khan took as his wife.

Legend or rumourMarino Sanudo Torsello (Liber secretorum fidelium crucis, c. 1321; ed. Bongars, Hanau 1611)

Keep in mind. A legend repeated from older crusade-era books; the Mongols were famously mounted archers, not ox-riding foot soldiers.

Strange talesNorthern Indialate 13th century

Eyes that could bore through brass: a captive poet's Tatars

Elias quotes the Persian poet Amir Khusrau of Delhi, who had been a prisoner of Mongol raiders and describes them with hatred: narrow piercing eyes, faces set on necks, noses spreading from cheek to cheek. Elias warns that the poet was 'burning with dread and resentment' and omits 'offensive details'. On a later page Elias notes that Khusrau calls the same people Tatars, Turks and Moghuls.

There were more than a thousand Tatar infidels and warriors of other tribes, riding on camels, great commanders in battle, all with steel-like bodies clothed in cotton ; with faces like fire, with caps of sheepskin, with heads shorn. Their eyes were so narrow and piercing that they might have bored a hole in a brazen vessel

Who Mongol ('Tatar') raiders of India

Saw itAmir Khusrau, quoted in Ney Elias's introduction (1895)

Keep in mind. Hostile caricature by a former captive, quoted in translation by the editor.
