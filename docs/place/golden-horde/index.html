Meat cured under the saddle

Here Schiltberger insists he saw it himself: on long journeys Tartars bleed their horses and cook and drink the blood, and they slice meat, salt it and ride on it under the saddle until it dries and softens. Every morning the khan drinks mare's milk from a golden cup on an empty stomach.

I myself have seen them bleed [their horses] and drink the blood after they have cooked it. … I have also seen when they are long on a journey, that they take a piece of flesh, cut it into slices, place it under the saddle, and ride on it, and eat it when they were hungry ; but they salt it first

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879; bracketed words are the translator's)

A hundred thousand huts, and no wood or stone

Describing the Horde under the kingmaker Edigü, Schiltberger says the people roam winter and summer with families and herds, and a hundred thousand huts go up whenever the khan camps. Elsewhere (p.50) he says Tartary stretches three months' journey with no wood or stones, only grass and shrubs.

The vassals in Tartary wander about in winter and summer, with their wives and children, and their cattle, and when the king encamps, there must be erected one hundred thousand huts.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

Daily lifeGolden Horde steppe near Tana (Azov), Russia1430s

The nomad Horde sows its grain

From the Venetian Josafa Barbaro, who lived at Tana, Hammer reports that Ulu Muhammad kept his camp moving for eleven years without letting the Horde sow in March and wait for the harvest, so it suffered grain shortage and lived on meat and milk alone. Normally, in February the camp announced the sowing day; those who wanted to grow grain drove out with wagons and seed to fields a day or two from the camp. The harvest was rich: fifty-fold for grain and a hundred-fold for millet. Nomads, in other words, also farmed.

Otherwise it was the custom that in the month of February the day on which sowing should take place in March was proclaimed in the camp. On that day those who wished to grow grain drove with their wagons and seed to the fields, usually one or two days' journey from the court camp, and tilled them. The harvest was plentiful, for the soil gave fifty-fold of the grain

Original wording

An diesem Tage fuhren die, so Getreide bauen wollten, mit ihren Wagen und Samen auf die gewöhnlich vom Hoflager Eine oder zwey Tagreisen entlegenen Felder und bestellten dieselben. Die Ernte war ergiebig, denn der Boden gab das Fünfzigfache des Kornes

Strange talesGolden Horde steppec. 1405-1410

A widow warlord with four thousand women archers

A powerful Tartar widow named Sadur Melik came to Edigü with four thousand women and girls to avenge her husband. Her women rode to war with sword and bow like men. When the kinsman who had killed her husband was captured, she beheaded him herself. Schiltberger says he was there.

He was brought before the woman ; she ordered him to kneel, and drew her sword, and cut off his head at one blow, and said : " Now am I revenged." I was present there, and I also saw this.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

The court is the 'orda', the queen is the 'khatun'

A page of ranks and offices shows how the Horde's court looked to Italian traders. Latin 'curia' (court) is Persian 'ordu' and Cuman 'orda', the word behind English 'horde'. An empress is 'khan khatun', a queen 'sultan khatun', a lord is 'bey' or 'khoja'. The khan's court, the orda, was the capital in the steppe.

lord: (Persian) khwaja / (Cuman) bey or khoja; crown: taj / tagz; court: (Persian) ordu / (Cuman) orda; empress: (Persian) wife of the padishah / (Cuman) khan khatun; queen: (Persian) wife of the king / (Cuman) sultan khatun.

Working translation

Original wording

dominus ghoya bey ul coia corona taz tagz curia ordu orda imperatris xen i padixa can catonj regina xen y patia soltan catonj

Read itCodex Cumanicus, Latin-Persian-Cuman glossary (Italian part, dated 11 July 1303), ed. Geza Kuun 1880

BuildingsTsarev, lower Volga (probably the Golden Horde capital, New Sarai)August 1669

Ruins of an imperial city, its stones carried off to build Astrakhan

Near Tsaritsyn Struys passed the ruins of Czaresgorod, the 'Imperial City', which he says Tamerlane destroyed. It had been built of hard stone, some remains of which could still be seen, but most of it had been carried off to Astrakhan to build that city. This matches the known reuse of brick and stone from the ruined Golden Horde capital, though Struys does not say who built it.

we passed near the ruins of Czaresgorod, or Imperial City, which was destroyed by Tamerlane during the war we spoke of above. It was built of hard stones, of which some remains are still seen, but most were carried to Astrakhan and served to build it.

Original wording

nous passâmes prés des ruines de Czaresgorod ou Ville Impériale, qui fut détruite par Tamerlan pendant la guerre dont nous avons ci-dessus parlé. Elle étoit bâtie de pierres dures dont on voit encore quelques restes, mais la plupart furent portées à Astracan, & servirent à la construire.

Daily lifeGolden Horde steppe (lower Volga)c. 1405-1410

Raising a khan on white felt

Schiltberger says the Great Tartars sow only millet, eat no bread and drink no wine, but drink mare's and camel's milk and eat horse and camel meat. The khan and his people live in the open fields winter and summer. A new khan is set on a white felt and lifted up three times, carried round the tent, and given a golden sword.

It is also to be noted, that when they choose a king, they take him and seat him on white felt, and raise him in it three times. Then they lift him up and carry him round the tent, and seat him on a throne, and put a golden sword in his hand.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

About Tartars of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

He witnessed several quick changes of khan while with Chekre, but does not say which enthronement he saw.

The Lord's Prayer in the language of the Horde

The German Franciscan part of the Codex contains Christian texts turned into Cuman for preaching. This is the start of the Lord's Prayer: 'Our father who art in heaven, blessed be thy kingdom, thy will be done as in heaven so on earth, give us this day our daily bread'. Elsewhere in the Codex the word for God is 'Tengri', the old Turkic name of the sky god, as Kuun's word index shows.

Our father who art in heaven. Blessed be thy kingdom (khanate). Thy will be done, as in heaven so on earth. Give us today our daily bread.

Three tribes of the Great Tartars

Schiltberger names three tribes among the 'Great Tartars': Kayat, Inbu and Mugal. The editors identify these as the Kerait, the Uighurs and the Mongols, so he correctly grasped that the Horde was a mix of peoples with the Mongols as one part. He also notes that Russia paid tribute to the Tartar king.

Item, the kingdom of Rewschen, which is tributary to the Tartar king. It is to be noted, that there are three tribes amongst the Great Tartars. One is called Kayat, the other Inbu, the third Mugal.

Heard itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

A Tatar history: 'The habitations of the Chans were stone buildings'

At Mozdok Klaproth bought a history of the Tartars in the Nogai dialect and translates its list of where the khans lived: Sarai, the Akhtuba, Madzhar, and vice-khans at Astrakhan and Kazan. He notes that the word he renders 'stone buildings' is 'Madshar' itself. The Nogais near the ruins also told him it had been the residence of Khan Mamai.

The habitations of the Chans were stone buildings. The residence of one Chan was Urisch, and the residence of another Chan was Churssan… Dshan Beg Chan's residence was at Ack Adil, with the name of Ssaray Aldy… Jaick Chan's was Madshar. There were three vice-chans; at Adshdruchan was the place of abode of Timur Ckutlu-Chan, Borki Chan's habitation was at Ckathatdr, and Sheich Aly Chan's residence at Ckasan.

Read itAnonymous history of the Tartars in the Nogai dialect, as translated by Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

Keep in mind

A late native chronicle of unknown date and author; place names are Klaproth's transcriptions.

One language and one script 'in all these kingdoms'

Kuun quotes a Franciscan missionary from the order's annals: he decided first to learn the language of the land, and 'by the grace of God' learned Cuman and the Uighur script, which were used in common 'throughout all these kingdoms'. It shows the Horde as a literate world with a shared Turkic language and an old Uighur script, not a land without writing.

But I, after taking counsel, wished first to learn the language of the land, and by the grace of God I learned the Cuman language and the Uighur script, which language and script are used in common throughout all these kingdoms.

Ruined cities between Kazan and Astrakhan, their stones reused

Near Simbirsk de Bruyn was told of very old and important cities between Kazan and Astrakhan, among them 'Achtoeba'. Nothing of it remains, he says, because all its stones were carried away to build Astrakhan and other places. The name almost certainly points to the Akhtuba branch of the Volga, where the ruins of the Golden Horde capital lay; Struys (W249) reported the same stone-robbing in 1669.

Moreover, it is said that there are very ancient and very considerable cities between Kazan and Astrakhan, among others Achtoeba … It is also known that the town of Achtoeba stood on this river, but not the least trace of it remains, all the stones having been carried away to build Astrakhan and some other places.

Original wording

Au reste on affirme qu'il y a des villes fort anciennes & fort considerables entre Casan & Astracan, & entr'autres Achtoeba … On sait aussi que la ville d'Achtoeba étoit située sur cette riviere, mais il n'en reste pas les moindres vestiges, toutes les pierres en aiant été transportées pour bâtir Astracan & quelques autres places.

What ‘the Horde’ meant

Schlözer explains that orda, from which the German word Horde was corrupted, is Turkish and Tatar ordu: first a field camp, then the sutlers' quarter of the camp and everyone who trailed after the army. Because the Mongols and Tatars of Kipchak long stayed nomads and warriors, Russian annals added a third meaning: the headquarters of the khan to whom the Russians paid tribute. ‘Going to the Horde’ meant appearing before the overlord as supplicant, vassal or litigant, just as Greek princes after 1453 went ‘to the Porte’ of the sultan.

Because the Mongols and Tatars in Kipchak long remained nomads and mere warriors, a third meaning arose in the Russian annals, by which Orda denoted the headquarters of the Tatar commander, namely the one under whose yoke the Russians stood. To go to the Horde now meant to appear before the supreme ruler as a supplicant, as a vassal, as a defendant or as an accuser.

Original wording

Weil nun die Mogolen und Tataren in Kaptschak lange Zeit Nomaden und blosse Krieger blieben: so kam in den rußischen Annalen eine IIIte Bedeutung auf, nach der Orda das Hauptquartier des tatarischen Heerführers, und zwar desjenigen, unter dessen Joche die Russen stunden, bezeichnete. In die Orde gehen, hieß nun so viel, als wie Supplicant, wie Vasall, wie Beklagter, oder wie Ankläger, vor dem Ober Regenten erscheinen.

The Tartars of the West and their kings

Polo calls the Golden Horde the 'Tartars of the Ponent' (of the West). Their first king, 'Sain', conquered Russia, the Cumans, Alans, Circassians, Goths of Crimea and Khazaria. He lists the later khans down to Toqta, who reigned in Polo's time. Yule's note points out that 'Sain' (the Good) was really Batu's own surname, so Polo turned one khan into two.

The first lord of the Tartars of the Ponent was Sain, a very great and puissant king, who conquered Rosia and Comania, Alania, Lac, Menjar, Zic, Gothia, and Gazaria ; all these provinces were conquered by King Sain.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe (Golden Horde)c. 1313 to 1321

Khan Uzbek turns Muslim, with an army counted in tens

Sanudo warns the Pope that the Sultan of Egypt, with gifts and treaties, had drawn 'the Emperor of the Tartars named Husbecho, who rules in Gazaria and other northern parts' to accept Islam. Trustworthy people told him Uzbek's cavalry was so vast that, his army being organised in tens, he could take just three of every ten and still field an almost unbelievable host.

the said Sultan so changed and drew the Emperor of the Tartars, named Uzbek, who rules in Gazaria and other northern parts, that he received the law of the faithless Muhammad ... For it is asserted by some trustworthy people that the said Uzbek has so numerous a multitude of horsemen that, taking three out of ten, since he has his army ordered by tens, from the threes alone he puts so large a force in the field that it seems almost incredible.

CitiesDasht-i Kipchak (Golden Horde)1236-1255

Batu's campaign west and his capital, Coc-orda

Abu'l Ghazi tells how Ögedei gave Batu an army to conquer the Russians ('Uresses'), Circassians and Bulgars. After a forty-day feast Batu set out, 'filled all the West' with his exploits, returned to the Kipchak steppe and died in his capital, which Abu'l Ghazi calls Coc-orda (probably Kök Orda, the 'Blue Horde'). The name shows the royal camp, the orda, serving as the capital.

gave him a numerous Army to go and subdue the Uresses, the Zerkass, the Bulgars, and other neighbouring Nations … Batu-sagin Chan after he had filled all the West with the Glory of the great Exploits which he atchieved upon that Occasion, at length return'd happily into his own Country of Dasht Kipzak, where he died some time after in the Capital of the Country called Coc-orda.

Read itAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

About Batu Khan and the Mongols of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

Written four centuries after Batu; he does not describe the capital or mention Sarai here.

Daily lifeDasht-i Kipchak (Golden Horde)c. 1257-1266

Berke, first Muslim khan of the Golden Horde

Batu's brother and successor Berke ('Burga'), Abu'l Ghazi says, met merchants from Bukhara on the road, was won over by their explanations of Islam, adopted it on the spot, and on his return ordered his subjects to follow. It is an early step in the Horde's move to Islam, the religion of the later Volga and Crimean Tatars.

he met upon the Road with several Merchants of Great Bucharia and falling into discourse with some of them, particularly upon the Articles of the Mahometan Faith, he was so pierced with the Explanations which these Men gave him of it, that he resolv'd off-hand to embrace that Religion

Read itAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

The Lord's Prayer in Tartar

Schiltberger ends his book with the Lord's Prayer written phonetically in Armenian and in 'Tartar', a rare early European sample of a Turkic language as a captive heard it. It begins 'Atha wysum' ('our father').

Roughly: 'Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name, thy kingdom come...' in a Turkic language.

Original wording

This is the Tartar Pater Noster. Atha wysum chy chockta sen algusch ludur senung adung kel suii senung hauluchig belsun

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)
