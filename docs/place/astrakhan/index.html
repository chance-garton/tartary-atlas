A Kalmyk prince with a wooden castle and a piano

Not every Kalmyk lord lived in a tent. Prince Sered-Dscheh, a colonel in the Russian army, had a large wooden castle on the left bank of the Volga, fortified with Russian-style outworks after the Napoleonic wars and furnished with mahogany, mirrors, clocks, a billiard table and a piano. Even so, he held court out on the steppe for part of the summer.

He resides on the left bank of the Volga, in a large wooden castle, thirty paces in front, which he fortified with Russian outworks when he returned from the French war … His castle is well fitted up in the interior, and contains a number of saloons, with mahogany furniture, lustres, mirrors, a billiard table, piano-forte, a number of clocks, &c.

Heinrich August Zwick (English edition, 1831)

How Mongol conquerors became the Turkic 'Tatars' of Kazan and Crimea

Bichurin explains how the name moved: the Mongol lords on the Volga were few among Turkic subjects, married into them, took their language, customs and Islam, and so disappeared, reappearing under the Russian name 'Tatars' as a seemingly new people ruling Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea. A footnote adds that the Tatars do not call themselves by that name but Uzbeks, Nogais and so on.

The conquerors, taking from the Turkestanis through marriage their manners and customs, their language and religion, little by little were completely lost, so that at last the very name of the Tatans disappeared, and under the Russian name of Tatars they became as it were a new people, which afterwards ruled in the tsardoms of Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea.

Tea with salt and butter

The Kalmyk princess offered Cook a dish of tea. The tea was good, he says, but the Kalmyks put salt and butter into it; he drank it out of politeness and then hurried off to get rid of it, while the Russian major laughed at him. It is an early Western description of Mongolian salted milk tea.

Glazed tiles and mosaic on a khan's tomb

Pallas describes the finest building at the Selitrennoye ruins as a khan's family mausoleum with a prayer house above, its foundation walls two fathoms high. The brickwork was the most elegant he had seen among any Tartar ruins, set with glazed tiles in green, yellow, white and blue, with stucco flowers, shell-work and mosaic panels. He adds that whole cargoes of its bricks had been carried off to build Astrakhan.

In the whole brickwork, which consists of beautiful broad squares, disposed in the most regular manner, there is a degree of taste and elegance of which I have no where seen an instance among the ruins of the Tartars. The outside of the walls is not only embellished in all the interstices between the bricks with glazed earthen ornaments, of a green, yellow, white, and blue colour,

Peter Simon Pallas, quoting his own earlier Travels (vol. 3), in the English translation (1812 edition)

Sarai, the Horde's capital, and its ruins on the Akhtuba

In his survey of the towns of Kipchak, Hammer places Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, on the Akhtuba channel of the Volga, where ruins stretched forty versts into the steppe and at least ten versts along the river. He takes from the Persian historian Wassaf that Batu founded it, and from coins that there were several Sarais: the old and the new (the second only an extension of the first), with Gülistan apparently a suburb, and a third called Sali Serai. Elsewhere (p. 140 to 141) he says Batu built Sarai in his last years, as he had earlier built Kazan.

Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans of the Golden Horde, whose ruins [lie] forty versts into the steppe from the bank of the Volga and at least ten versts along the Akhtuba … There were, as the coins show, three Sarais: the old and the new, the latter being only an extension of the old, just as Gülistan, which often appears on coins, seems to have been only a suburb of the residence Sarai

How a Kalmyk kibitka is put up

Camped in bitter cold above Astrakhan, Bruce's detachment was lent sixty kibitkas by the Kalmyk prince Dasan. He describes them closely: 24 feet across, folding lattice walls six feet high, a round wooden crown raised on lances with holes for the rafters, and a felt covering laid from the bottom up. He found them warmer and more weatherproof than a house.

they are all round, the sides being made of a kind of checkered wicker-work, and the cross sticks neatly jointed for folding together or extending … they raise with their lances a round board, three feet diameter, with a hole in the middle of it … the large hole serves for their chimney, the small holes receive the ends of so many strait rafters

Peter Henry Bruce (published 1782)

Abu'l-Fida's entry on Seray (Sarai), capital of 'the states of Berke', says the present ruler is Uzbek, that the city lies in a plain on the Volga north-west of the Caspian, and that merchants crowd there and do a great trade in Turkish slaves. He calls it a modern city, founded by a descendant of Chinggis Khan named Sain Batu, and adds day-length information from a man of the city.

The prince now reigning is called Uzbek. Sarai lies in a plain, north-west of the Caspian Sea … It is a place much visited by merchants, and there is a great trade there in Turkish slaves. The town is of recent origin; its founding is attributed to a descendant of Chinggis Khan called Sain Batu.

Daily lifeAstrakhan1769 to 1770

Nose rings of gold and pearls

Gmelin describes how Astrakhan Tatar women braided their hair down the back, ending for girls in a long black silk tassel threaded with silver and for married women in a ribbon. Their finery included earrings and gold or silver rings, sometimes set with pearls or gems, worn in the nostrils. With a touch of irony he argues that if Europeans find it natural to pierce ears, Tatar women may fairly do the same to their noses. On the same page he describes the Sunni Tatars praying together in the open field at the end of Ramadan, kneeling and rising in step 'like a company of soldiers at drill'.

Finally, Tatar women's finery includes earrings and silver and gold rings, sometimes adorned with pearls or precious stones, which are worn in the nostrils. If in Europe it does not seem unnatural to mutilate the ears for earrings, one may fairly grant Tatar ladies the pleasure of performing a similar operation on their noses.

The nameCrimea, Kazan and Astrakhan13th to 15th centuries, written 1823

How Turks came to be called Tatars

Klaproth explains the mix-up. When Chinggis Khan's son was sent west, the steppes north of the Caspian and Black Sea were full of Turkic peoples (Cumans, Pechenegs, Bulgars), who became subjects of the Kipchak khanate. Its rulers were Tatars (Mongols) but its people mostly Turks. When the empire broke up into the khanates of Crimea, Kazan and Astrakhan, the khans were still descendants of Chinggis, so the states kept the name 'Tatar' even though their armies and people were Turkic. On the previous page he tells how Chinggis himself got his birth name from a captured chief of the 'White Tatars'.

Its rulers were Tatars, but its subjects mostly Turks. Towards the end of the fifteenth century this empire broke up into several small ones, of which those of Crimea, Kazan and Astrakhan were the most important. Their princes descended from [Chinggis Khan] and were Mongols or Tatars, but their armies no longer were … Nevertheless these khanates kept the name 'Tatar', because the khans were Tatars.

'Ask a Kazan Tatar if he is a Tatar'

Klaproth says that if you asked a so-called Tatar of Kazan or Astrakhan whether he was a Tatar, he would say no, and that he called his language Türki or Turuk, not Tatar. Remembering that his forefathers had been conquered by the Mongols or Tatars, he took 'Tatar' as an insult meaning robber. Klaproth concludes that Tatars must not be confused with Turks, rebukes the historian Schlözer for doing so, and adds that Chinese historians never called the Manchus 'Ta-ta', so the missionaries' phrase 'Tartar-Manchu' came only from their habit of calling every Central Asian a Tartar.

Ask, however, a so-called Tatar of Kazan or Astrakhan whether he is a Tatar, and he will answer no; just as he calls his language not Tatari but Türki or Turuk. Remembering that his forefathers were subjugated by the Mongols or Tatars, he considers 'Tatar' a term of abuse, which to him means the same as 'robber'.

'This is how Abraham lived'

While he sketched, the Tatars crowded round him, as surprised by his clothes as he was by theirs. The women spun and carded wool for the tent felts and burned dried cow dung for fuel. He found their way of life close to that of the Arabs, and thought this must be how Abraham and the patriarchs lived, content in tents as Europeans are in palaces.

Their way of life is quite close to that of the Arabs, and they seem as content with their dwellings as we are with palaces and the finest houses. This brings to my mind the ancient way of the Orientals, and I imagine that this is how Abraham and the other patriarchs lived

Khan Janibek's giant cooking pot

The buildings of the Russian saltpetre works at Selitrennoe, Gmelin notes, were mostly built from the rubble of the Tatar ruins. Among the old boiling kettles of the works lay one so huge that the local Tatars told a tale about it: that it was a relic of their past, in which Khan Janibek had twelve oxen cooked at once for his feasts. Gmelin says flatly that it is a Crown saltpetre kettle.

[the dwellings of] some people living here are mostly built from Tatar rubble … In the boiling works there are still furnaces and kettles. Among the latter lies one … The Tatars tell a fable that it is a Tatar relic and that Khan Janibek had 12 oxen cooked in it at once at his feasts. But it is certainly a saltpetre kettle belonging to the Crown.

Strange talesAstrakhan steppe1716

Hunting the vegetable lamb

German authors claimed that a 'Tartarian lamb' (barometz) grew as a plant near Astrakhan, half animal and half vegetable, eating the grass around it. Bell walked many miles with local Tartars to find it and found only a dry prickly bush. The Tartars themselves, he reports, laughed at the story. A rare case of a traveller testing a legend on the spot.

In search of this wonderful plant I walked many a mile, accompanied by Tartars who inhabit these deserts; but all I could find out were some dry bushes, scattered here and there, … After further enquiry of the more sensible and experienced among the Tartars, I found they laughed at it as a ridiculous fable.

John Bell (Travels, 1763)

Crimean guides who led a Turkish army to starve

Asked how the Muscovites had inflicted a great defeat on the Turks when a river diversion was attempted, Possevino's informant said it was not the Muscovites but the Crimean Tatars who did it. Though allies of the Turks, they feared a heavier yoke if the scheme succeeded, so they offered themselves as guides and led the Turkish troops through vast forests and foodless country until nearly all died of hunger and toil.

... the Muscovite had inflicted [so great a defeat on the Turks], when he had ordered the Don to be diverted into the Borysthenes: he answered that this was done not by the Muscovites but by the Precop Tartars, though allies of the Turks, who resented the scheme because they foresaw a heavier yoke hanging over them. So, offering themselves as guides to the Turkish forces, they led them round through huge forests and places without supplies, so that almost all of them perished of hunger and toil.

CitiesAstrakhan and Sarai, lower Volga, RussiaWinter 1395 to 1396

Walls of ice at Astrakhan, and Timur's razing of Sarai

After defeating Tokhtamysh, Timur resolved to level Astrakhan. Its people used the hard winter to build walls of ice blocks cemented by pouring water over them, but Timur marched through the deep snow, emptied the town, razed it and had its headman (kelanter) Mahmudi drowned under the ice that was meant to protect him. He then went on to Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, and laid it waste, as revenge for the Horde's earlier destruction of a city of the Chagatai realm. The loot of both cities was divided among the army.

In spite of the severe winter, which still gave the inhabitants of Astrakhan the advantage of fortifying themselves with walls of ice, joined together by pouring water over them, and in spite of the deep snow, he marched on Astrakhan, emptied the town of its inhabitants and had it razed … From Astrakhan Timur moved to Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans, and laid it waste
