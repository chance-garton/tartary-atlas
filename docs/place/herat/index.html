BuildingsHerat1830

The ruined Musalla of Herat, built by a Timurid king

Editorial contextNorth of Herat Conolly explored a 'pile of magnificent ruins', the Musalla, which he was told was built by a king of the house of Timur to receive the body of Imam Reza, then abandoned when the saint was buried at Mashhad. He describes a towering iwan, a court a hundred paces square decorated with mosaic of white quartz and coloured enamel tile, and domed halls. On the next page he counts the remains of twenty minarets.

A mile to the north of the city are the remains of what anciently was the wall of Heri, … is a pile of magnificent ruins, … the "Moosullah," or place of worship, built by a pious king of the house of Timour, to receive the remains of the Imaum Reza

Arthur Conolly (1834)
See the original page (p. 19)

About Timurid dynasty (house of Timur)

Keep in mind

The reason given for its building is local tradition; historically the Musalla complex was built by Timurid rulers in the fifteenth century.

BuildingsTurkmen country between Herat and Merv1840

Why a felt tent beats a house

Editorial contextLodged in a Turkmen camp of about 300 black tents, Abbott was won over by the felt tent. He explains that the felt can be lifted in summer, a central fire warms it in winter with the smoke escaping through a roof opening, and the whole home can be moved to a better spot.

These Toorcumun tents are the most comfortable of dwellings … by removing a portion of the felt covering, this tent is open to the air in summer ; and in winter a fire lighted in the centre makes it the warmest of retreats, all the smoke rising through the skylight in the roof.

James Abbott

CitiesKushk valley, north of Herat1839

A capital made of black tents

Editorial contextThe 'capital' of the Jamshidi tribe was mostly a spread of round black felt tents on a light wooden frame, darkened by smoke. Abbott notes only a few mud huts and a so-called fort that looked like a run-down farmyard.

The black tents which form the capital of the Jumsheedees are of thick felts, supported by a light and moveable framework of wood. Their shape is circular, and their dark hue is received from the smoke of fires lighted within : but sometimes from the colour of the wool. Khooshk however has a few mud huts and a fort, (so called) resembling a dilapidated farm yard.

James Abbott

Daily lifeHerat1358 (as told)

Reading the future in a mutton bone

Editorial contextBefore helping his ally Malik Husayn retake Herat, Timur strips a leg of mutton to the bone and performs 'the usual ceremonies' to read an omen from it; the sign is good, and the city falls by surprise at dawn. The translator adds a footnote that the Tartars play with sheep bones instead of dice. Omens run all through these memoirs.

At this time there was a stewed leg of mutton before us, the bone of which was sticking out ; I took it, and having stripped it of all the meat, I resolved within myself to draw from it an omen, whether Melk Hussyn would be successful in his enterprize. I performed the usual ceremonies, and the result was favourable

Attributed to Timur (translated by Charles Stewart, 1830)

BuildingsHerat (Gazur Gah)1830

White marble tombs of Timur's descendants at Gazur Gah

Editorial contextAt the shrine of the Sufi saint Khwaja Abdullah Ansari on the hills above Herat, Conolly walked through a walled garden where trees shaded 'several tombs of white marble built over the dust of the descendants of Timour-lung'. He adds that Timur's descendants built summer palaces and gardens on the same hill.

We deposited our slippers here, and then passed into a walled garden, the trees of which shaded several tombs of white marble built over the dust of the descendants of Timour-lung.

Arthur Conolly (1834)

The nameKushk valley, north of Herat1839

'Tartar' as a look, not a nation

Editorial contextAbbott calls the Jamshidis Turkish by descent and 'decidedly Tartar' in their features, using 'Tartar' as a racial type. He adds an insult that recurs in the book: that all Tartars fear artillery.

The Jumsheedees are of Toorkish descent, as their habits and physiognomy imply. They are short, stout, very dark, with decidedly Tartar features. … They are arrant cowards, and like all Tartars, superstitiously fearful of artillery.

James Abbott
See the original page (p. 11)

About Jamshidis

Keep in mind

Prejudiced generalisation. The Jamshidis are usually counted today among the Persian-speaking Aimaq peoples, not as Turks.

The nameHerat1825

'Moghuls are a tribe of Tartars'

Editorial contextCorrecting an earlier traveller who listed 'Moghuls' among the people of Herat, Fraser explains that Moghuls are properly one tribe of Tartars, and that in India the word was used loosely for Muslims in general because many descended from the Mughal conquerors.

Moghuls are, in truth, a certain tribe of Tartars, very few of whom could possibly be in Herat as part of its permanent population. Probably Captain Christie inserted this word in his journal somewhat loosely, annexing to it the same meaning which is given to it in Hindostan, where it is often applied to Mussoolmans in general

James Baillie Fraser (footnote)
