BuildingsDon steppe near Azov1437

Burial mounds with stone crosses, credited to the Alans

Describing the land around Tana, Barbaro says the countless mounds on the steppe are graves, each topped with a pierced stone holding a stone cross. He credits them to the Alans, a Christian people destroyed by the Tartars, not to the Tartars themselves.

These have been Christen men, and were chased awaie and destroied by the Tartares. In that region are hills, ryvers, and plaines : wheare are to be seene an infinite nombre of little hills forced in signe or steede of sepultures, and on the toppe of everie of them a great stone with an hole : wherein standeth a crosse of one peece made of an other stone.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

About Alans (As), 'chased awaie and destroied by the Tartares'

Keep in mind

The crosses may be later Christian additions or Barbaro's reading of stone markers; most steppe kurgans are far older than the Alans.

Daily lifeDon and Volga steppe (Astrakhan campaign)1569

Crimean guides who led a Turkish army to starve

Asked how the Muscovites had inflicted a great defeat on the Turks when a river diversion was attempted, Possevino's informant said it was not the Muscovites but the Crimean Tatars who did it. Though allies of the Turks, they feared a heavier yoke if the scheme succeeded, so they offered themselves as guides and led the Turkish troops through vast forests and foodless country until nearly all died of hunger and toil.

... the Muscovite had inflicted [so great a defeat on the Turks], when he had ordered the Don to be diverted into the Borysthenes: he answered that this was done not by the Muscovites but by the Precop Tartars, though allies of the Turks, who resented the scheme because they foresaw a heavier yoke hanging over them. So, offering themselves as guides to the Turkish forces, they led them round through huge forests and places without supplies, so that almost all of them perished of hunger and toil.

cladem Moscus intulisset, cùm Tanaim deriuari iussisset in Boristhenem: respondit, hoc non à Moscis, sed à Tartaris Præcopensibus, licèt Turcarum fœderatis, accidisse, ægrè ferentibus id fieri, vnde grauius sibi iugum pendere prospicerent. Quamobrem cùm sese duces itineris Copijs Turcicis exhibuissent, per ingentes syluas, & loca commeatus egena, eas circumduxisse, vt fame, ac labore pleræq; omnes fermè interierint.

Antonio Possevino, S.J. (Moscovia, Vilnius 1586), reporting an unnamed informant 'N.'

About Crimean ('Precop') Tatars; Ottoman Turks

Hearsay. The river named is garbled: the real 1569 Ottoman plan was a Don to Volga canal to reach Astrakhan (a reader of this copy wrote 'Volgam' in the margin).

Strange talesDon steppe near Azov1437 to 1438

Venetian treasure-hunters dig a kurgan

Tipped off by a story that a Tatar woman in Cairo had told of Alan treasure, Barbaro and six partners hired 120 men and dug into a great mound. Instead of gold they found layers of ash, millet husks and fish scales, then stone vessels, glass-covered brick beads and part of a silver ewer. The dig was so big that locals renamed the mound 'the cave of the Franks'.

we founde in the botome certein vessels of stone, some of them with asshes, some with coles, some emptie and some full of fishe back bones. We founde also v or vi beadestones as bigge as oranges made of bricke and covered with glasse … We founde also halfe the handle of a little ewer of sylver, made with an adders hedde on the toppe.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

About Alans; Venetian merchants of Tana

Keep in mind

The treasure story came at third hand; Barbaro repeats the local explanation that the Alan lord Indiabu faked a grave to hide his wealth from the Tartar emperor.

BuildingsDon steppe near Azov1438

Tall carts carrying round felt houses

Barbaro watched the whole horde pass Tana and describes its vehicles: two-wheeled carts taller than Italian ones, closed with reed mats and covered with felt or cloth depending on rank. Some carried a house built on a timber ring with half-hoops above, filled with reed mats and covered in felt, which was lifted off when they camped.

This people carie with them innumerable cartes of twoo wheeles higher than ours be, which are closed with mattes made of reades, and parte covered with felte, parte with clothe … Some of these cartes carie their houses vpon them which are made on this wise. They take a cercle of tymber, whose dyameter is a pase and an halfe, crossed within foorthe with other halfe cercles

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

About Tatars of the Golden Horde (the camp of Küchük Muhammad)

Keep in mind

Scribal abbreviations in the Tudor text (w^th, pte) have been expanded.

The nameDon and Dnieper, Kulikovo field1380

Not grey wolves but the pagan Tatars: the 'Khinova' comes to Rus

The poet builds the Horde's approach from omens: creaking carts between the Don and the Dnieper, the 'Khinova' marching on the Russian land, grey wolves howling by the river Mecha. Then he turns the image: those were not grey wolves but the pagan Tatars, come to take the whole Russian land. Sreznevsky's commentary suggests 'Khinova', an old poetic name for steppe enemies also used in the Lay of Igor, may hide the name of the Huns.

The Khinova is marching on the Russian land. Grey wolves are howling; by the river Mecha they mean to cross into the Russian land. Those were not grey wolves: the pagan Tatars have come, they mean to pass through making war and take the whole Russian land.

Working translation

Original wording

Идетъ Хинова на Рускую землю. Сѣріе волци воютъ, на рѣкѣ хотятъ на Мечи поступити въ Рускую землю. То ти были не сѣріе волци, придоша поганые Татарове, хотятъ проити воюючи, взяти всю землю Рускую.

Zadonshchina (Kirillo-Belozersk text), ed. I. I. Sreznevsky, 1858

About Golden Horde army of Mamai (Tatars)

Keep in mind

Poetic imagery. The manuscript reads 'хинела'; 'Хинова' is Sreznevsky's correction (footnote on the same page), and the Huns etymology is his guess (commentary, n54 p.49).

BuildingsBabskaya, lower Don (Rostov Oblast)1769

Stone figures 'in Tatar shape' on the Don mounds

Near the Cossack settlement of Babskaya on the Don, Gmelin saw two stone statues standing on burial mounds, one of a man and one of a woman 'in Tatar shape', so weathered that little more could be made out than that they were carved from gypsum stone. He was told many more stood between there and Bakhmut, and that in such mounds the dead lay facing east, men with swords and gold and silver rings, though Cossack treasure-hunters had already emptied most of them.

Not far from the Babskaya stanitsa I saw on two kurgans two statues, one of which shows a man and the other a woman in Tatar shape. … In the kurgans on which such memorial columns stand, the bones of the people buried inside are always found laid towards the east

Working translation

Original wording

Ohnweit der Staniza Babskaja sahe ich auf zwei Kurganen zwei Statuen davon die eine einer Manns- und di … eine Weibs-Person in Tatarischer Gestalt vorstellt. … In den Kurganen, auf welchen solche Ehren-Säulen stehen, findet man die Knochen der darinnen begrabenen Menschen allezeit nach Morgen zu gelegt

Samuel Gottlieb Gmelin

About Tatars (as Gmelin reads the statues and graves)

Keep in mind

The right edge of the page is cut off in the scan. 'Tatar shape' is Gmelin's impression; such steppe statues were made by several earlier peoples.

BuildingsPontic steppe (between Don and Danube)late 9th century

Magyars: 'a race of the Turks' living in tents

Before they moved to Hungary, the Magyars lived on the steppe north of the Black Sea. Ibn Rusta calls them a race of the Turks, says their chief rides out with twenty thousand horsemen, and that they have tents (qibab) and move with the grass and pasture. In winter they camped by the rivers and fished.

The Majghariyya are a race of the Turks; their chief rides out with about twenty thousand horsemen … They have domed tents and travel with the grass and the pasture, and their country is wide.

Working translation

Original wording

والمجغرية جنس من الترك ويركب رئيسهم فى مقدار عشرين الف فارس … ولهم قباب يسيرون مع الكلأ والخصب وبلادهم واسعة

Ibn Rusta (Arabic text ed. M. J. de Goeje, 1892)

About Magyars (Hungarians), called Turks

Keep in mind

'Turks' here is a broad label for steppe horsemen; the Magyars spoke a Finno-Ugric language.

Strange talesUpper Don RiverAbout 1600 (first printed 1773)

A giant's grave and a stone table on the Don

On the upper Don the book lists a 'Volotova mogila', a giant's grave (volot is the old Russian word for a giant), at the mouth of the Mecha, and further down the 'Don beseda' (Don meeting-place), 'a stone table and stone vessels'. Nearby were the Tatar fords into Rus. These folk names for kurgans and rocks sit side by side with military route data.

and at the mouth of the river Mecha, on the upper side, is the Giant's Grave. … And below the mouth of the Sosna, on the Don, is the Don Meeting-place, a stone table and stone vessels.

Working translation

Original wording

а устьѣ рѣки Мечи съ вышія страны Волотова могила. … А ниже устья Сосны, на Дону, Донская бесѣда, каменной столъ и каменныя суды.

Book of the Great Map, first edition 'Drevniaia rossiiskaia idrografiia' (1773)

About Unnamed (folk landmarks on the Tatar frontier)

Keep in mind

The 1838 edition reads 'Bolotova' (marsh) where the 1773 edition reads 'Volotova' (giant's).

CitiesDon River (as Carpini says)c. 1220s-1230s

A trading city drowned by diverting its river

Carpini tells how the Mongols took the rich, crowded city of Orna, a port full of Christians and Muslim merchants: finding it hard to storm, they dammed the river that ran through it and drowned the city with everything in it. He goes on to the sack of Kiev and the Russian towns.

After that they came before the city of Orna, which was very populous, and there were many Christians there, Khazars, Russians, Alans and others, and some Saracens … The Tartars, seeing that it was hard to take by force, decided to stop the river that runs through the city, and so they drowned it with everything that was in it.

Working translation

Original wording

Aprés cela ils vindrent deuant la cité d'Orna qui estoit fort peuplée, & là y auoit plusieurs Chrestiens Gazares, Russes, Alans, & autres, & quelques Sarasins … Les Tartares voyans qu'il estoit difficile de la prendre de force, ils s'aduiserent d'arrester la riuiere qui passe par ceste ville, & ainsi la submergerent auec tout ce qui estoit dedans.

John of Plano Carpini (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

About Tartars (Mongol armies); Khazar, Russian and Alan Christians; Muslim traders

Keep in mind

Carpini places Orna on the Don, but the story matches the Mongol flooding of Urgench in Khwarazm (1221), and historians have often suspected a confusion; treat the location as uncertain.

BuildingsSteppe between the Kuban, Terek, Don and DnieperSeptember 1793

Stone statues on the burial mounds

Along the Tashla Pallas saw the rough-hewn stone statues that stand on burial mounds across the steppe from the Kuban to the Dnieper, always facing east. From their dress and faces he judged that a people related to the Mongols made them. In a footnote on the next page he quotes Rubruck, who credited them to the Cumans, and argues that different monuments on the steppe came from different nations. (Most are now thought to be Cuman (Polovtsian) work of the eleventh to thirteenth centuries.)

If we may judge from the costume and form of the face, they uniformly appear to have been made by a people related to the Mongolians, who formerly inhabited those countries. … All these statues are erected on moderately large sepulchral hills, and their faces are exposed towards the East.

Peter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

BuildingsIlovlya River (Lawla), between the Don and the VolgaJuly 1769

A grave mound with furnaces: laboratory or metalworker's tomb?

Near a Don Cossack farmstead Lepekhin examined a grave mound that had been dug open. The top of the page is damaged, but it can be made out that inside were furnace-like structures, slag, pieces of crucibles and a human skeleton, and that much iron had been found. He decides it is more likely a tomb than a workshop, because 'the old Tatars' buried people of rank with the tools of their trade, and concludes that the old inhabitants of the Volga were skilled in all the useful arts.

that the old Tatars had the custom of burying persons of distinguished rank and station with all the tools and gear they had chiefly worked with in their lifetime. For the rest, this mound may be a Tatar laboratory or the tomb of a famous metallurgist among them; it is all one to me.

Working translation

Original wording

daß die alten Tatarn im Gebrauch hatten, Personen, deren Rang und Stand sich auszeichnete, mit alle dem Geräthe und Zubehör zur Erde zu bestatten, womit sie in ihrem Leben vornehmlich zu schaffen gehabt hatten. Uebrigens mag dieser Hügel ein tatarisches Laboratorium, oder das Grabmahl eines berühmten Metallurgen unter ihnen seyn; das ist mir einerley.

Ivan Lepekhin (German translation, 1774)

About 'The old Tatars' and the ancient inhabitants of the Volga country

Keep in mind

Calling the mound Tatar is Lepekhin's inference from what he had read about Tatar burial; the damaged scan hides the full description of the finds.

The nameDon RiverOctober 1743

'The Cossacks are a species of Tartars'

Passing through Cossack villages on the Khoper, Hanway describes clean, well-dressed people and women with crescent-shaped caps. He then files the Cossacks under 'Tartars', explaining that their name means freebooters. It shows how loosely an eighteenth-century Englishman used 'Tartar' for any steppe people on Russia's frontier, even Orthodox Russians.

The Cossacks are a species of tartars: their name signifies free-booters; but in these parts they are civilized, and faithful to the Russian government,

Jonas Hanway (Historical Account, 1753)
