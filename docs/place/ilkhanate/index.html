Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

A horse on a pole for the dead lord

Riccoldo describes Tartar funerals: cooked meat, clothes and money buried with the dead, and for a great lord a horse ridden to exhaustion, killed, stuffed with green grass and hung up on a stake, told to be ready whenever its master rises again. He adds that they once buried up to twenty living slaves with a lord until Christians shamed them out of it, and that Tartars even paid bishops to be buried in Christian cemeteries.

and the horse falls, and he guts it and empties everything from the horse's belly and fills it with green grass, and then drives a great stake in from behind and makes the stake come out at the mouth, and so leaves the horse impaled, and hangs it up and orders it to be ready whenever its lord wishes to rise

Raised on black felt: a khan election Hayton saw twice

Hayton says the Tartars still make their lord the way they made Genghis: the whole people gathers in a field, the chosen man is seated on a black felt, the nobles and Genghis's kin lift him high and set him on a rich throne, and all kneel to him. He adds that he himself was present at two such elections, and marvels that for all their conquests they never changed their first custom.

When they want to elect their lord (and I have been twice at the election of the emperor of the Tartars, and saw how all the Tartars gathered in a great field), they seated the one who was to be their lord on a black felt, and set a rich throne in their midst.

Original wording

Strange talesIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

A shaman who seemed to fly

Riccoldo says the Tartars honour above all men certain 'boxitae' (bakhshis), priests of idols from India, wise and grave, who practise magic with the help of demons, show many marvels and foretell the future. One of their leaders was said to fly, but, Riccoldo reports, it was found that he only walked just above the ground without touching it and sat on nothing solid.

For one of the greater among them was said to fly, but according to the truth it was found that he did not fly, but walked close to the ground without touching it, and when he seemed to sit he was held up by nothing solid.

Working translation

Original wording

Nam et vnus maior inter eos dicebatur volare, sed secundum veritatem repertum est, quod non volauit, sed ambulabat iuxta terram et eam non tangebat, et quando videbatur sedere, nulla re solida sustentabatur.

Heard itRiccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

Execution called 'sleep', and a blank space for the lord's name

Two sharp details of Mongol court manners. When writing to a superior, a Tartar leaves a gap where the lord's name belongs and writes the name out in the margin. When a man is condemned, the lord says 'cut him and make him sleep', because they do not call death death, and the condemned simply holds out his arms to be bound.

When they write to their superiors they keep such reverence that they do not write their superior's name in its place, but leave an empty space and afterwards write the name outside, in the margin, in another place.

Working translation

Original wording

Cum scribunt suis maioribus, tantam reuerenciam habent et seruant, quod non scribant nomen sui maioris in suo loco, sed dimittunt spacium vacuum et postea scribunt nomen de foris in margine in alio loco.

Saw itRiccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1873)

Strange talesIran (Ilkhanate)c. 1280s

A 'magician' who dug up a jewel and won the Ilkhan's trust

In a footnote from the Syriac chronicler Bar Hebraeus, d'Ohsson tells of Abd al-Rahman, a slave's son who escaped the sack of Baghdad, worked as a carpenter in Mosul, and then claimed spirits had taught him magic. Taken to the Mongol ruler Abaqa, he asked to be brought to the treasure castle of Tala, paced the ground, pointed to a spot, and there a ring with a precious stone was dug up. From then on he was believed to command demons, and under Ahmad he ran the whole kingdom.

he began to measure the ground this way and that; then he pointed to a spot where they should dig, and himself stood some distance away. Digging, they found a ring set with a stone of great value; it was taken to Abaqa. This having confirmed the words of the so-called magician, everything he said was believed

Working translation

Original wording

il se mit à mesurer le sol de côté et d'autre; puis il indiqua un endroit où l'on devait creuser, et se tint lui-même à quelque distance. En creusant on trouva un anneau, où il y avait une pierre d'un grand prix; on le porta à Abaca. Ce fait ayant confirmé les paroles du prétendu magicien, on ajouta foi à tout ce qu'il disait

Why the Tartars became Muslims

Riccoldo explains the Ilkhanate's conversion to Islam from a missionary's frustrated point of view: the Tartars had neither law nor prophet, found Islam an easy law, and Muslims gave them rich gifts to convert, so that now 'for the most part' they have become Saracens. He adds that many still like Christians but find Christianity too hard and are offered no presents.

they took up the law and became Saracens, above all because the Tartars had neither law nor prophet. So now for the greater part the Tartars have become Saracens. For the Saracens buy them and give them very great gifts so that they will become Saracens.

Working translation

Original wording

acceperunt legem, et facti sunt Sarraceni, maxime quia ipsi Tartari nec legem nec prophetam habebant. Vnde modo pro magna parte Tartari facti sunt Sarraceni. Nam et Sarraceni eos emunt et dant eis maxima dona, vt ipsi efficiantur Sarraceni.

Saw itRiccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

About Tartars (Mongols of the Ilkhanate)

Keep in mind

A partisan explanation; the Ilkhan Ghazan converted in 1295, within Riccoldo's stay.
