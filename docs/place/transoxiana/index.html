BuildingsShahrisabz1404

Timur's palace at Kesh: glazed tiles, and a doubt about who began it

At Kesh (Shahrisabz), Timur's birthplace, the envoys toured palaces said to have been under construction for twenty years: brick arches covered in glazed tiles, a court paved with white stone around a great pool, and a gateway in gold and blue tiles. Clavijo noticed the lion-and-sun device over the door and argued that an earlier lord of Samarkand must have begun it, since Timur's own device was three circles. A rare case of a medieval visitor questioning a building's attribution.

On the top of this doorway there was the figure of a lion and a sun, which are the arms of the lord of Samarcand ; and, though they say that Timour Beg ordered these palaces to be built, I believe that the former lord of Samarcand gave the order

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

About Timur (Timour Beg) and an earlier lord of Samarkand

Keep in mind

Clavijo's reasoning about the arms is his own inference.

CitiesTransoxiana (Uzbekistan)1826 (about earlier centuries)

Towns for Persians, open country for Turks

Erskine explains why the Turki language absorbed so many Persian words. The cities north of the Oxus were mostly lived in and farmed by the older Persian-speaking people, the Sarts or Tajiks, because the Turks long disliked town life and farming, mocking wheat as 'a weed'. The Turkish rulers held court in these cities, but the townspeople were largely not Turks.

The cities of Samarkand, Bokhara, Ahsi, Andejan, and Tashkend, as well as the other towns to the north of the Oxus and Jaxartes, were chiefly inhabited by Persians, the Turks long retaining their aversion to the life of a town, and refusing to submit to the drudgery of agriculture for the sake of supporting themselves on the top of a weed, as they call wheat in derision.

William Erskine, preface (1826)

About Turks and Tajiks (Sarts)

Keep in mind

Editorial generalisation; Babur himself says the people of Andijan were all Turks.

BuildingsAmu Darya (Oxus) crossing, south of KarshiFebruary 1825

A Kazak camp of felt tents and reed huts

Just across the Oxus, Moorcroft passed through a Kazak owal (aul, a tent village) of khirgahs (felt tents) and kappahs, round huts of mats and reeds with conical tops. Saddled horses waited at the doors, and the cattle were stabled in pits dug in the ground and roofed with branches, grass and sand.

we passed through an owal, or station of Khirgahs and Kappahs, or circular huts of mats and reeds, with a conical top. Horses stood saddled at the doors of the tents, and mares and foals, and many camels, were grazing in the adjoining jangal. The cows were stabled in holes made in the ground, roofed with branches of trees and grass, and covered with sand and bushes.

William Moorcroft (edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)

Daily lifeTransoxiana (Uzbekistan)c.1333 to 1334

A khan deposed for breaking Genghis Khan's Yasa

Ibn Battuta visited the camp of Sultan Tarmashirin, a devout Muslim khan who prayed in a mosque inside his camp. He explains that Genghis Khan's law book, the Yasa, required an annual assembly where nobles could depose a ruler who had broken its rules. Tarmashirin had abolished that assembly, so, after Ibn Battuta left, the Tartars and their nobles deposed him and he was killed.

Now, Tarmashirin had entirely abolished the observance of this day, which gave very great offence. Some time, therefore, after we had left the country, the Tartars, together with their nobles, assembled and deposed him, appointing for a successor one of his relations

Ibn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)

About Chaghatayid Mongols ('the Tartars') under Tarmashirin

Keep in mind

The deposition happened after he left, so he reports it second-hand; his account of the Yasa assembly is not confirmed by other sources.

Daily lifeTransoxiana, the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestanc.1400

Chinggis Khan's law set above Islam

Ibn Arabshah says Timur spoke Persian, Turkish and Mongol but no Arabic, and held to the 'Chinggis Khan rules' (the yasa), treating them like branches of Islamic law. So, he says, did all the Chaghatay, the people of the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestan, whom he calls rabble. For this reason, he adds, leading jurists declared Timur an unbeliever.

He held to the rules of Chinggis Khan, which are like the branches of jurisprudence in the Islamic faith … And so do all the Chaghatay and the people of the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestan, that rabble: all of them put the rules of the accursed Chinggis Khan ahead of the rules of Islam.

About Timur, the Chaghatay, the people of the Dasht (Golden Horde Tatars)

BuildingsTransoxiana (Uzbekistan)c.1350s (as told)

Black felt tents and a room dug underground

Lost on a freezing night while hunting, young Timur and his companions saw black felt tents that at first looked like sand hills. The family took them for thieves, then gave them a room built underground, lit a fire, fed them soup and lent them flea-ridden blankets. Timur says that after becoming ruler he rewarded them.

About this time we saw some black (felt) tents or huts, upon which my companions said, " these are hillocks of sand and dust," … the inhabitants of which supposing I was a thief, hallooed out and prepared to attack me ; but when I told them all the circumstances, they were ashamed, and having cleared out a room which was constructed under ground, lighted a fire for us

Attributed to Timur (translated by Charles Stewart, 1830)

About Nomads of Transoxiana

Keep in mind

The word 'felt' is the translator's addition in brackets. The story may be literary.

The nameTransoxiana1398

Turks, Tartars, Moguls and Tajiks

Two footnotes explain the troops sent to scout the mountains of Kafiristan. 'Turks' are the descendants of Moguls and Tartars; 'Tadgics' (Tajiks) are the Persian-speaking townspeople of Transoxiana and Iran, who are neither. It is a neat 1720s key to how Tartar, Mogul and Turk overlapped.

The Turks are those who descend from the Moguls and Tartars… The Tadgics are the inhabitants of the towns of Transoxiana, and all the country of Iran, who are neither Tartars, Moguls, nor Turks.

Translator's footnote (François Pétis de la Croix, carried into the 1723 English)

BuildingsHisar (Tajikistan)1504

A prince in exile sleeps in a felt tent of cross-poles

Driven out of his homeland, Babur set off for Khorasan with two or three hundred followers, most on foot. They had only two proper tents; Babur gave his to his mother and slept each night in a small felt tent on flexible poles. Erskine's note explains this alachuq was a felt-covered frame that folded up easily.

Such was our distress, that among us all we had only two tents. My own tent was pitched for my mother, and they erected for me at each stage a felt-tent of cross-poles, in which I used to take up my quarters.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

CitiesKarshi1365 (AH 767)

Repairing a Mongol khan's palace at Karshi

Before marching on Samarkand, Timur orders the palace of 'Kepee Khan' at Karshi repaired, a fortress built at the town gate and other buildings put up. The translator's note explains that the old town of Nakhsheb took the name Karshi (Turkic for 'palace') from a palace built there by a Mongol prince, the Chaghatayid khan Kebek.

I prepared for marching to Samerkund, but first gave orders to repair the palace of Kepee Khan, whose name in the Turky language, was Karshy ; and I commanded that a fortress should be erected at the gate of the town, and some other buildings constructed.

Attributed to Timur (translated by Charles Stewart, 1830)

About Chaghatayid Mongols; Timur

Keep in mind

The wording makes 'Karshy' the palace's Turkic name; the footnote is by the translator.

The nameTransoxiana (Uzbekistan)14th century

Timur's four tribal vizirs and the Barlas

Ibn Arabshah opens his life of Timur by explaining that the Turks, like the Arabs, are divided into tribes. The four chief officers of the Chaghatayid sultan came from four tribes, the Arlat, Jalayir, Qauchin and Barlas, and Timur was the son of the Barlas chief. On the same page he reports a Persian genealogy linking Timur to Chinggis Khan through the female line, and explains Timur's title Gurkan as Mongol for 'son-in-law', since he married royal daughters.

The Turks have tribes and clans that almost match the tribes of the Arabs, and each of these vizirs was from a tribe … the first of them called Arlat, the tribe of the second called Jalabir [Jalayir], the tribe of the third called Qauchin, and the tribe of the fourth named Barlas; and Timur was the son of the fourth of them.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

About Chaghatay Turks (Timur's people)

Keep in mind

Ibn Arabshah wrote as an enemy of Timur. The print misspells Jalayir as Jalabir; the tribal list matches other Timurid sources.

The nameCentral Asia (Chu valley and Transoxiana)1120s to about 1211, as told in 1221

The Karakhitai: steppe exiles who swapped carts for camels

The envoy tells how Ta-shi (Yelu Dashi), a Khitan of the fallen Liao house, fled north-west with the frontier tribes, moving from pasture to pasture for years. Blocked by rocks and snow, they abandoned their carts, loaded their goods on camels, conquered the Muslim lands and founded an empire. After a regent princess was executed for killing her husband and a weak ruler took over, the realm decayed, and the few Khitan left had adopted Muslim customs and dress. It is a compact picture of a nomad people turning into a settled empire and then dissolving into its subjects.

They were obliged to leave their carts behind, and to carry their baggage on camels. Thus they arrived in the country of the Hui-ho (Mohammedans), took possession of the land and founded an empire. … the empire fell into decay, and was finally destroyed by the Hui-ho. At the present day there are few of these people left, and they have adopted the customs and the dress of the Hui-ho.

Wu-ku-sun Chung-tuan (Wugusun Zhongduan), as written down by the recorder of the Pei shi ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Karakhitai (Western Liao), Khitan exiles

Keep in mind

Told from the Jin side; Bretschneider points to the fuller Liao history account in his Part II. The Changchun diary (W113, p.73) blames the fall on the Naiman prince Guchluk and the Sultan of Khwarazm rather than simply 'the Hui-ho'.
