The nameLiaodong1368 to 1618

How the Tartar borderlands split into three kingdoms

Semedo's origin story for the new enemy: after expelling the Tartars, Hongwu made the Tartar borderlands tributary and divided them into states with titles. These grew until they split into three kingdoms, East, West and North. The western and northern ones broke away at once; the eastern one stayed loyal until the Chinese, for no reason, decided to crush it, and it turned on them. The eastern kingdom is the Jurchen (Manchu) state.

When Hum Vu, the founder of the family that today holds the Chinese sceptre, shook off from his empire the Tartar [who had ruled it for ninety years], … leaving the borders of Tartary as vassals, he reduced them to tributaries, dividing them into states with different titles and dignities. These afterwards grew so much that they separated into three kingdoms: one to the East, another to the West, and another to the North.

BuildingsLiaodongc. 1637 to 1642

The famous Wall: 'more fame than effect'

Ending his tour of the provinces with Liaodong, famous for ginseng, Semedo notes that as the frontier with the Tartars it is badly wasted and partly held by them. Here, he says, runs the celebrated wall of three hundred leagues, which has more fame than effect: for all its size the enemy has reduced it to a miserable state. The Wall is Chinese, and in his view failing.

It [the root] is called ginseng. But being the frontier with the Tartars, [the province] is much destroyed, and even partly possessed by them. Here is where one sees that so celebrated wall, running for the space of three hundred leagues, with more fame than effect: for in spite of its greatness and capacity, the enemy has put it in a miserable state.

Working translation

Original wording

Llamase Ginsem: pero por ser frontera a los Tartaros muy destruida, antes parte posseída dellos. Aqui es que se vè aquella tan celebre muralla corriendo por espacio de trecientas leguas, con màs fama que efecto: porque sin embargo de su grandeza i capazidad, la tiene el enemigo puesta en miserable estado.

Álvaro Semedo, S.J. (Spanish edition by Manuel de Faria e Sousa, Madrid 1642)

BuildingsLiaoning1682

New houses of mud and rubble among old walls

In place of the old cities, Verbiest saw only broken walls of brick and stone, with a few new houses thrown up on the outskirts. Some were of earth, some built from the rubble of the old buildings, and most were thatched rather than tiled. The capital, which he calls Kin-Yam (Shenyang), was the exception: fairly whole, with the remains of an old palace.

One can see nothing through the whole Country but old ruined Walls with Rubbell, Bricks and Stones. In the out skirts of these Citys there have been of late some few houses built, but without any order. Some of them made of Earth, others of the Rubbish of the old Buildings, the most part of them covered with straw or thatch, and but few with tyles.

Ferdinand Verbiest (English translation, 1686)

Daily lifeLiaodong1621 (described 1654)

Shaved heads, queues and horse-hoof sleeves

When the Tartars took a city in Liaodong, they proclaimed that no one would be killed who cut his hair and wore Tartar dress. Martini then breaks off to describe their manners: head and beard shaved except the moustache, a long braided tuft at the back, a low round cap trimmed with beaver or sable. The next pages add long robes with sleeves cut like a horse's hoof, a sabre hung point forward, silk or horse-skin boots, broad saddles, and the remark that they are like 'our Tartars of Europe' but not so barbarous.

For the Tartars (that I may say something of their Manners, as my subject gives me occasion) doe shave both the Head and Beards reserving only the Mustachoes, which they extend to a great length, and in the hinder part of their heads they leave a Tuft, which being curiously woven and plated, they let hang down carelesly below their shoulders;

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

The nameManchuria (Liaodong)1616 to 1662 (written 1700)

'Eastern Tartars': a Manchu dynasty from a small horde chief

Careri opens his chapter on the 'Eastern Tartars' by saying their princes' origin is so obscure that everyone who writes of it tells fables. They began only in this century from 'a small Horde chief, or captain of outlaws or wandering Tartars' called Tien-mim (Nurhaci). He goes on to divide Tartary into Eastern (Manchu lands, poor, with only two or three small towns) and a far more powerful Western Tartary, both mostly living in tents.

The origin of these princes is so obscure that whoever has tried to speak of it has fallen into fables. They had their beginning in this century from a small chief of a Horde, or captain of outlaws or wandering Tartars, who was called Tien-mim

Original wording

E' così oscura di questi Principi l'origine, che chiunque ha voluto parlarne ha dato nelle favole. Eglino hanno avuto principio in questo secolo da un picciol Capo d'Horda, o Capitano di fuorusciti, o Tartari erranti, che si chiamava Tien-mim

Giovanni Francesco Gemelli Careri (Giro del Mondo, part IV, Naples 1700), compiling Martini and other Jesuit histories

CitiesLiaoning1682 (describing damage from the conquest wars)

Towns wiped out by the Manchu war

Verbiest saw almost nothing left of the many towns and villages of Liaodong. He was told why: the 'petty King of the Tartars' who started the war, with only a small army, forced the local people to take up arms and then destroyed their homes so his soldiers had nothing to go back to. It is a clear case of a named cause for ruins, reported sixty years after the event.

There is now not the least footsteps remaining of a great number of Towns and Villages which were here before the Warr. Because the petty King of the Tartars, who began to kindle this Warr having but a very small Army, caused the Inhabitants of those places to take Armes, which places he forthwith destroyed

Ferdinand Verbiest (English translation, 1686)

About Manchus (the 'Tartars' of the Qing founders)

Keep in mind

Verbiest saw the ruins; the explanation of how they came about is what he was told. The 'petty King' is presumably the Manchu founder Nurhaci, whose armies took Liaodong from 1618.

Daily lifeLiaodong1618 (described 1654)

Servants and horses burned with the dead

When the Chinese ignored his complaints, the Tartar king (Nurhaci) vowed to celebrate his father's funeral with the lives of 200,000 Chinese. Martini explains that when a Tartar of rank died, servants, women and horses, with bows and arrows, were thrown into the funeral fire to serve him in the next life, a custom he says they gave up after conquering China.

For it is the custom of the Tartars when any man of quality dyeth, to cast into that fire which consumes the dead Corps, as many Servants, Women, and Horses, with Bows and Arrows, as may be fit to attend and serve them in the next life: Though now since they conquered China, they have left off this barbarous custome,

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars (Nurhaci's people)

Keep in mind

Reported, not witnessed; Manchu funeral sacrifice (of wives and servants) is attested, but the scale is probably exaggerated.

BuildingsLiaodong and Manchuria1709-1711

A palisade of wooden stakes around Liaodong

On the imperial map survey of Tartary, Pierre Jartoux found the ginseng country walled off from the province of Liaodong by a barrier of wooden stakes (the Willow Palisade), patrolled by guards to keep Chinese from slipping out to dig the precious root. He adds that two or three thousand still sneaked through, risking their freedom.

The places where ginseng grows are entirely separated from the province of 'Canton', called Liaodong on our old maps, by a barrier of wooden stakes that encloses the whole province, around which guards patrol constantly to prevent the Chinese from leaving it and going to look for this root.

Working translation

Original wording

Les endroits où croît le gin-seng, sont tout-à-fait séparés de la province de Canton, appelée Leaotong dans nos anciennes cartes, par une barrière de pieux de bois qui renferme toute cette Province, et aux environs de laquelle des Gardes rôdent continuellement pour empêcher les Chinois d'en sortir, et d'aller chercher cette racine.

Pierre Jartoux, letter from Peking, 12 April 1711

About Manchus (Qing state); Chinese ginseng poachers

Keep in mind

'Canton' is an error in this reprint (the province meant is Guandong, i.e. Liaodong, not Canton in the south). The volume is a later Toulouse reprint.

Daily lifeLiaodongAugust 1618

Captives sacrificed before the battle of 1618

Semedo translates a memorial sent to the Wanli emperor by the President of the Council of War in 1618. It reports that the Tartars had massed to 'take this world of China', broke through the walls, seized Chinese prisoners and sacrificed them the day before the battle, acclaiming their own king as king of Beijing. Semedo says he translated it at the time and later found it printed in Europe.

At this time, with great power and multitude, entering the walls, they seized some of our men in order to sacrifice them; and they sacrificed them the day before the battle, with great acclamations of their king, calling him already King of Peking.

Working translation

Original wording

En este tiempo con grã poder i multitud, entrando los muros, prendieron algunos de los nuestros para sacrificarlos; i sacrificaron el dia antes de la batalla con grãdes aclamaciones de su Rey, llamandole ya Rey de PeKim.

President of the Chinese Council of War, memorial of 1618, as translated by Álvaro Semedo (Spanish edition, Madrid 1642)

Daily lifeLiaoning1682

A ring of 3,000 guards closes on the game

The emperor hunted almost daily. Verbiest describes the Manchu ring hunt: 3,000 guardsmen spread out in a circle at least 3,000 paces across, then walked inward in step until it was only 300 paces wide, trapping everything inside. He watched two or three hundred hares taken in less than a day, and elsewhere over a thousand deer, besides bears, boars and more than sixty tigers.

they Reduced this great Circle to another much less, which had about 300 paces in the Diameter : So that all the Beasts which had been stayed within the first, found themselves taken in this last as in a Net … I saw taken in this manner two or three hundred Hares in less then one day, without counting an Infinite of Wolves and Foxes.

Ferdinand Verbiest (English translation, 1686)

The nameJinzhou, LiaoningNovember 1831

Manchu 'Tartars' on the emperor's pay

Arriving at the harbour of Kin-chow (Jinzhou), about fifteen leagues from Mukden (Shenyang), Gützlaff was told the Manchus of the area were numerous but lived idly, mostly in the direct or indirect employ of the emperor, a description of the hereditary banner garrisons. He adds that there seemed little jealousy between them and the Chinese labourers, and that the Manchus looked little different from the Chinese.

The Mantchou Tartars who live hereabouts are numerous, and lead an idle life, being principally in the employ of the Emperor, either directly or indirectly. There seems to be but little jealousy between them and the labouring class of Chinese.

Karl Gützlaff (1832)

About Manchus ('Mantchou Tartars')

Keep in mind

This comes from 'the persons with whom I conversed', not from his own observation inland.

BuildingsJinzhou, LiaoningNovember 1831

Granite houses and beds heated from below

Gützlaff found Kin-chow small and plain. Its houses were built of the local granite, with few comforts apart from brick sleeping platforms warmed by a fire lit underneath (the kang). It is a precise, humble picture of domestic building on the edge of 'Tartary'.

Kin-chow itself has very little to attract the attention of visitors ; it is not a large or handsome place. The houses are built of granite ( which abounds here) ; and are without any accommodations, except a peculiar kind of sleeping places, which are formed of brick; and so constructed, that they can be heated, by fires kindled beneath them.

Karl Gützlaff (1832)

Daily lifeLiaodong1621

Portuguese cannon against the Tartars, 1621

In 1621 the city of Macau sent the emperor three large cannon with gunners. One burst at a demonstration in Beijing, killing three or four men, but the guns were still sent to the Tartar frontier. The Tartars, not knowing the new weapon, came too close, were hit by a shower of iron fragments, and fled; afterwards they were more careful.

Nevertheless they were sent to the frontier of the Tartars, who, not knowing of the new weapon and coming nearer than was good for them, tasted a shower of pieces of iron, from which many fell, and all fled. Afterwards they became cautious.

Original wording

Todavia embiaronse a la frontera de los Tartaros, que no sabiendo de la nueva arma, i llegandose màs de lo que les convenia, provaron un rocîo de troços de hierro, de que cayeron muchos, i todos fueron huyendo. Despues se acautelaron.

Álvaro Semedo, S.J. (Spanish edition by Manuel de Faria e Sousa, Madrid 1642)

CitiesLiaoyang1618 to 1621 (as Martini tells it)

Storming a city behind a wooden wall

Martini describes how the Tartars, armed only with sabres and bows against Chinese muskets, took 'Leaotung', which he calls the chief city of the province of Liaodong. The front ranks carried thick boards as a moving 'wooden wall' to soak up the musket fire, ladder-men followed, and cavalry came behind. Before the Chinese could reload, the Tartars were over the walls. He credits their speed and nimbleness, 'in which they excel all Nations'.

the scaling ladders being applied, before they could charge again, they were upon the Walls and enterd the City ; for such is the quickness and nimbleness of the Tartars (in which they excel all Nations, and in which also they place their chief art) that in a trice, they either prevail in their Designs, or retire :

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

Keep in mind

An event before Martini reached China, told from report; he runs several Liaodong campaigns together.

CitiesLiaoyangabout 1621

Nurhaci pulls down unlucky walls and builds new ones

After taking 'Leaotung' and then Guangning, Martini says, the Tartar king returned to Liaodong rich with plunder. His soothsayers told him the old walls were unlucky, so he pulled them down, built new ones, and proclaimed himself emperor of China though he held only its edge. A page earlier Martini explains that Chinese soldiers and citizens fled their houses because the Tartars destroyed any city that resisted but only plundered those that surrendered.

And because his Southsayers had perswaded him that the standing of the old Walls were unfortunate, he beat them down, and compassed them about with new, fortifying them with new Munitions, and there proclamed himself Emperour of China:

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars under Nurhaci

Keep in mind

Martini's report; historically Nurhaci moved his capital to Liaoyang in 1621 and to Shenyang in 1625.
