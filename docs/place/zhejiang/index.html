BuildingsHangzhouabout 1346

Hangzhou's temples and ten thousand stone bridges

The Khan sent the legates home through Manzi, southern China, with its 'thirty thousand great cities'. Campsay (Hangzhou), the old Song capital, is to Marignolli the finest city in the world, full of buildings and temples, with thousands of monks living together. The ten thousand carved stone bridges he takes from earlier writers and cannot quite believe. These were Chinese buildings in a Chinese city under Mongol rule; he does not credit any of them to the Tartars.

And among them is the most famous city called Campsay, more marvellous, more beautiful, richer and larger, with a greater population, more riches and delights, more buildings and above all temples of idols … than any city that exists in the world, or perhaps ever existed. There, writers write, are ten thousand fine bridges of stone with sculptures and images of armed princes; it is incredible to one who has not seen it, and yet perhaps they do not lie.

Working translation

Original wording

Et inter illas est civitas famosissima nomine Campsay mirabilior, pulchrior, dicior, & maior cum maiori populo, & pluribus diviciis, & deliciis, edificiis, & maxime ydolorum templis … quam aliqua civitas, que sit in mundo, vel forte fuerit unquam. Ubi scribunt scribentes esse decem millia poncium nobilium de lapide cum sculpturis & ymaginibus principum armatorum, incredibile est non videnti, & tamen forte non menciuntur.

Saw itGiovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

About Chinese of Manzi (southern China) under the Great Khan

Keep in mind

The bridge count is taken from earlier writers, as he says. Dobner's footnote wrongly identifies Campsay with Cambay in Gujarat.

Daily lifeZhejiang1645 to 1646

Fighting harder for their hair than for their emperor

The Manchus took the south of Zhejiang easily, Martini says, until they ordered everyone to cut their hair in Tartar fashion. Then soldiers and townspeople alike rose up, fought 'more desperately for their Hair' than for king or kingdom, and drove the Tartars back across the Qiantang river. Elsewhere (p. 108 to 109) he says the Tartars made refusing the haircut high treason, and many Chinese chose death instead.

But when they commanded all by Proclamation to cut off their Hair, then both Souldier and Citizen took up Arms, and fought more desperately for their Hair of their Heads, than they did for King or Kingdom, and beat the Tartars not only out of their City, but repulst them to the River Cienthang

Saw itMartino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars and the Chinese of Zhejiang

Keep in mind

Martini was living in Zhejiang at the time, but he did not witness every battle he describes.

Strange talesHangzhouc.1346

The rope trick at a Mongol governor's feast

At a banquet given by the emir Qurtay at El Khansa (Hangzhou), the Khan's jugglers performed. A ball on a strap was thrown up out of sight; a boy climbed the strap and vanished; the master followed with a knife and threw down the boy's limbs, then put them together and the boy stood up. Ibn Battuta says he was so shaken he had palpitations. The Muslim judge beside him swore it was all illusion.

he then threw the hand of the boy upon the ground, then his foot ; then his other hand, then his other foot ; then his body, then his head. He then came down, panting for breath, and his clothes stained with blood. … The juggler then took the limbs of the boy and applied them one to another

Saw itIbn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)

About Yuan Mongol court entertainers

Keep in mind

He says he witnessed it; a judge present called it trickery. Some scholars doubt how much of the China section is first-hand.

Strange talesShaoxing, Zhejiangc. 1280s

A Tibetan lama loots the Song tombs and builds a pyramid of bones

Under Khubilai a Tibetan lama in favour with the Mongols dug up the tombs of the Song emperors near Shaoxing, reportedly finding much gold and jewels, and piled the bones with those of oxen and horses into a pyramid. The outraged local officials jailed him, but Mongol lords and court lamas got Khubilai to free him and return most of his treasure, which, Gaubil says, the Chinese never forgave.

His passion for gold and silver drove him to dig up the dead bodies of the Emperors and great men whose tombs were near Shaoxing. He found there, it is said, much gold, silver and jewels. From these bones, mixed with the bones of oxen and horses, he made a pyramid.

Working translation

Original wording

Sa passion pour l'or & l'argent le porta jusqu'à déterrer les corps morts des Empereurs, & des Grands dont le tombeau étoit près de Chaohing. Il y trouva, dit-on, beaucoup d'or, d'argent, & de bijoux. De ces ossemens mêlés avec des os de bœuf, & de chevaux il fit une pyramide.

Read itAntoine Gaubil, translating the Chinese annals

About Mongols (Yuan court of Khubilai); a Tibetan lama; Chinese

Keep in mind

CitiesShaoxing1646

Shaoxing, the cleanest city in China, surrenders

Martini, who knew Zhejiang well, pauses as the Tartars cross the Qiantang to praise Shaoxing: not the largest city but the cleanest and most comely in China, circled by fresh water, with wide streets paved in white square stone, a navigable canal down the middle, stone bridges and arches. On the next page he says its houses are built of the same square stone, 'which I observe no where else in China'. It submitted without resistance.

This City in bigness yields to many others, but in cleaness and comeliness it surpasses all: it is so invironed with sweet waters as a man may contemplate its beauty by rounding it in a Boat ; it hath large and fair Streets paved on both sides with white square stones, and in the middle of them all runs a Navigable Chanel,

Saw itMartino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars; Chinese of Zhejiang

Keep in mind

A Chinese city, not a Tartar one; it matters here as a city the Tartars took.
