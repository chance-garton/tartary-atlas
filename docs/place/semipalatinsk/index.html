BuildingsSemey (Semipalatinsk)1829

The 'palaces' of Semipalatinsk were lama cells

It is probable that the Palaty whose ruins I saw at Semipalatinsk were a very similar establishment, whose cells, once covered with paintings, served as dwellings for lamas. In the steppe of the Volga Kalmyks, near Sarepta, the Moravian Brethren's colony, one finds out in the open fields cages topped by tall posts and containing, beside a Buddhist idol, a small collection of religious books.

Working translation

Original wording

Il est probable que les Palates dont j'ai vu les ruines à Semipolatinsk, étaient un établissement tout semblable dont les cellules, jadis couvertes de peintures, servaient d'habitation à des lamas. Dans la steppe des Kalmuks du Wolga, voisine de Sarepta, colonie des frères Moraves, on trouve en plein champ des cages surmontées de poteaux élevés et renfermant, près d'une idole du culte bouddhistique, une petite collection de livres religieux.

Editorial context

The town of Semipalatinsk ('seven palaces') took its name from old ruins. Humboldt, who saw them, thought they were a Buddhist establishment like Ablai-kit, whose painted cells had housed lamas. He compares the little roadside shrines of the Volga Kalmyks near Sarepta: cages on tall poles holding an idol and a few religious books.

Alexander von Humboldt (footnote)

Strange talesIrtysh steppe between Semey (Semipalatinsk) and YamyshevoNovember 1723

A Russian envoy guesses that ancient Russians lay under the mounds

Among us people are buried with their wealth, and especially in ancient times, before the Christian law, more than seven hundred years ago, they were buried with all their war-gear and great mounds of earth were heaped over them; and it may be that in ancient times some of our peoples lived in these places … [Darzha] said that among them writing began only a hundred years ago.

Working translation

Original wording

У насъ погребаются люди съ богатствомъ, а паче что въ древнія времена еще до христіанского закона, лѣтъ слишкомъ за семь сотъ погребалися со всѣмъ военнымъ уборствомъ, и насыпали на нихъ великіе бугры земли, и можетъ быть, что въ древнія времена нѣкоторые изъ нашихъ народовъ въ сихъ мѣстахъ жили … сказалъ, что у насъ де только сто лѣтъ какъ грамота началась

Editorial context

Continuing the argument about the robbed mounds, Unkovsky told Darzha that in pagan times, over seven hundred years earlier, Russians had buried their dead with war-gear under great earth mounds, so perhaps some of 'our peoples' had once lived here. He blamed the digging on Kalmyks, Ostyaks or Kazakhs, and said Russian law punished grave-robbers with death. Darzha, doubtful, replied that his people had only had writing for a hundred years and could not know. A striking early example of a claim about who once lived on the steppe, made on the spot and without evidence.

Ivan Unkovsky, recording his own words and the reply of the Dzungar envoy Darzha (ed. N. I. Veselovsky, 1887)
See the original page (p. 156)

About Dzungars (Oirats); Russians; Kazakhs; Ostyaks

Keep in mind

Unkovsky offers this as a guess in a diplomatic sparring match, not as knowledge; he himself calls it 'perhaps'.

CitiesSemey (Semipalatinsk), Kazakhstan1826

A wooden frontier town with Tatar minarets

It is fairly large, has only wooden houses, most of them small and plain, 4 wooden mosques, a trading hall likewise of wood, the very dilapidated customs buildings and, especially towards the north, many Kirghiz yurts. … The foreign dress of the many Tatars, the veiled women, the slender minarets from which the monotonous call to prayer so often rings out, give the town an oriental air.

Working translation

Original wording

Sie ist ziemlich groß, hat nur hölzerne Häuser, von denen die meisten klein und unansehnlich sind, 4 hölzerne Metschets, einen gleichfalls hölzernen Kaufhof, die sehr verfallenen Zollgebäude und, besonders nach Norden hin, viele Kirgisenjurten. … Die fremde Tracht der vielen Tataren, die verschleierten Frauen, die schlanken Minarets, von denen so oft der monotone Ruf zum Gebet erschallt, geben der Stadt einen orientalischen Anstrich.

Editorial context

Meyer describes the town beside the Semipalatinsk fort: all its houses, its four mosques and even its trading hall were of wood, with Kazakh yurts on the edges. The Tatars' dress, veiled women and minarets with the call to prayer gave it an 'oriental' look. A good picture of what a 'Tatar' town on the Irtysh really looked like in the 1820s.

Carl Anton Meyer (diary printed in Ledebour's Reise, part 2, 1830)

BuildingsSemey (Semipalatinsk), Kazakhstan1719 to 1720

The 'Seven Palaces' in the steppe

It is very surprising to find such a regular edifice in the middle of a desert. Some of the Tartars say it was built by Tamerlane, called by the Tartars Temyr-Ack-Sack or Lame-Temyr; others by Gingeez-Chan. The building, according to the best information I could obtain, is of brick or stone, well finished, and continues still entire. It consists of seven apartments under one roof,

Editorial context

Bell did not visit this building on the upper Irtysh, but reports what he was told: a regular brick or stone edifice of seven rooms under one roof, standing alone in the steppe. Local Tartars credited it to Tamerlane or to Genghis Khan. Its rooms were full of scrolls in the writing of the 'Tongusts, or Kalmucks' (probably meaning Tanguts, that is Tibetans, or Kalmyks); Bell bought a bundle from a soldier in Tobolsk and gave some to Sir Hans Sloane. The Tibetan or Kalmyk writing suggests a Kalmyk (Oirat) Buddhist building rather than a work of Tamerlane, and the ruin, 'Sem Palaty', later gave the city of Semipalatinsk (Semey) its name.

John Bell (Travels, 1763)
See the original page (p. 193)

About Kalmyks; local Tartar informants

Keep in mind

Hearsay: Bell never saw the building, and the Tamerlane and Genghis Khan attributions are local legend.

BuildingsIrtysh steppe between Semey (Semipalatinsk) and YamyshevoNovember 1723

Opened burial mounds all over the steppe: whose were they?

Travelling across the steppe we saw a great many dug-open mounds … like opened cellars, visible all over the steppe. … He was asked: what people were buried here? He said: supposedly of their own people. To this he was told that by their faith they burn their dead and throw them in the water, give them to dogs and birds for food, and do not bury them with gold or with any wealth at all.

Working translation

Original wording

Ѣдучи въ пути степью, видѣли множество разрытыхъ бугровъ … яко погребы раскрытые, по всей степи видны. … Спрошено у него: Какіе люди тутъ погребены были? Оной сказалъ, будто ихъ народу. На сіе сказано ему, что по ихъ вѣрѣ умершихъ людей жгутъ и въ воду бросаютъ, собакамъ и птицамъ на кормъ отдаютъ, а съ золотомъ и ни съ какимъ богатствомъ не погребаютъ.

Editorial context

On the way home Unkovsky saw many burial mounds dug open 'like cellars' across the steppe. The Dzungar envoy Darzha had heard that Russians dug them for golden stirrups and cups, and said the dead were of his own people. Unkovsky objected that by their religion Kalmyks burn their dead or give them to dogs and birds, and never bury them with gold, and suggested that perhaps some of 'our peoples' had lived there in ancient times; Darzha still held that they were his. An early recorded argument about who built the kurgans.

Ivan Unkovsky (ed. N. I. Veselovsky, 1887)
See the original page (p. 155)

About Dzungars (Oirats); unknown ancient builders

Keep in mind

Unkovsky saw the robbed mounds; his account of Kalmyk funeral custom is a generalisation made to win an argument.

BuildingsSemipalatinsk1771

The 'Seven Palaces' were mud-brick and Bukharan

At present only decayed masonry of three buildings remains; the position of the rest can only be inferred from the rubble; the building style is poor and easily recognised as Bukharan, and the buildings were laid out without any order.

Working translation

Original wording

Gegenwärtig ist nur noch von dreyen Gebäuden verfallenes Mauerwerk vorhanden; die Lage der übrigen läst sich nur noch aus dem Schutt schliessen; die Bauart ist schlecht und leicht vor Bucharisch zu erkennen, auch sind die Gebäude ohne einige Ordnung angelegt gewesen.

Editorial context

The fortress of Semipalatinsk ('seven palaces') on the Irtysh was named after ruins twelve versts upstream, famous in earlier travel books. Pallas went to see them: the remains of a few buildings of slate slabs set in clay and of unburnt brick, one a windowless hut, laid out without order. He judged the work poor and plainly Bukharan, and counted the seven 'palaces' only by including rubble heaps.

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)
See the original page (p. 500)

About Bukharans (Central Asian settlers), per Pallas

Keep in mind

The identification as Bukharan is Pallas's judgement from the building style; modern scholars link the site to a Zunghar (Oirat) Buddhist monastery.

Clay ruins in the steppe, credited to Bukharan farmers

but the weather had destroyed them entirely, because all the buildings had been made only of clay. In the field nearby one could see some channels in the ground, into which the inhabitants of this area, who are said to have been Bukharans, had led water

Working translation

Original wording

allein das Wetter hatte sie ganz zernichtet, weil alle Gebäude bloß von Leim aufgeführet gewesen waren. Auf dem Felde dabey sahe man einige Rinnen in dem Erdreiche, in welche die Einwohner dieser Gegend, welche Bucharen gewesen seyn sollen, Wasser geleitet haben

Editorial context

South of Semipalatinsk the party saw the foundations of a house of six rooms, which Gmelin calls the former dwelling of a Kalmyk 'idol-worshipper'. Others had stood there, but weather had destroyed them because all were built only of clay. Irrigation channels in the fields he credits to Bukharans, since, he explains, the Kalmyks do no farming at all and even their ruler has no fixed seat but is always on the move.

Johann Georg Gmelin (German original, Göttingen, 1751)

About Kalmyks (Dzungars) and Bukharans

Keep in mind

Who built the houses is Gmelin's inference from hearsay and from what he knew of Kalmyk life.

BuildingsSemey (Semipalatinsk), Kazakhstan1826

The vanished 'seven palaces' of Semipalatinsk

This new fortress is laid out on the high, very steep right bank of the Irtysh. Only a few paces from it lie the barely recognisable foundations of 3 old buildings, and about 14 versts further west are the likewise faint traces of the 4 other 'palaces'.

Working translation

Original wording

Diese neue Festung ist auf dem hohen, sehr steilen rechten Ufer des Irtysch angelegt. Nur wenige Schritte von derselben entfernt liegen die kaum mehr erkennbaren Grundlagen von 3 alten Gebäuden und etwa 14 Werst weiter nach Westen befinden sich die gleichfalls schwachen Spuren von den 4 andern Palaten.

Editorial context

Semipalatinsk means 'seven-palace town' in Russian. Meyer notes that by 1826 the old 'palaty' that gave the place its name were almost gone: barely visible foundations of three buildings beside the new fort, and faint traces of the other four about 14 versts west. He does not say who built them.

Carl Anton Meyer (diary printed in Ledebour's Reise, part 2, 1830)
