A Tibetan prince at the sacred mountain: where the Mongol khans came from

That youngest son of King Khri gum btsan po, named Nya khri, or in Mongolian Borte Chino, took a wife named Go(a) Maral from the land of Kongpo, went with her into Mongolia and came to the mountain Burkhan Khaldun on the shore of Lake Baikal.

Working translation

Original wording

Jener jüngste Sohn des Königs K'ri gum btsan-po, mit Namen Na k'ri oder mongolisch Porta Zeba, nahm sich aus dem Lande rKoii-po eine Gattin G-n-ö Maral mit Namen, ging mit ihr in die Mongolei und kam an den Berg Porhan Galtona am Ufer des Päigal -Seees.

Editorial context

Jigme Namkha begins the Mongol royal line not with the wolf and doe of the Secret History but with a son of the Tibetan king Drigum Tsenpo, called Borte Chino in Mongolian, who went into Mongolia with his wife and came to the mountain Burkhan Khaldun. The local people, learning of his noble descent, made him their ruler. It is a Buddhist retelling that ties the Mongol khans to Tibet's royal house.

Jigme Namkha (translated from the Tibetan into German by Georg Huth, 1896)
See the original page (p. 10)

About Mongols (the Chinggisid line)

Keep in mind

Huth's transliteration of Mongol names is idiosyncratic ('Porta Zeba' for Borte Chino, 'Porhan Galtona' for Burkhan Khaldun), and the OCR garbles some letters; the placing of Burkhan Khaldun by Baikal is the author's.

Thomsen: the Tatars of the stones were Mongols, not Turks

a well-known name that we meet here for the first time in history. In Chinese literature, Ta-ta appears at the beginning of the ninth century. This name belongs to the Mongols, or rather to one of their tribes, and not to any people of Turkic race.

Working translation

Original wording

nom bien connu que nous rencontrons ici pour la première fois dans l'histoire Dans la littérature chinoise, Ta-ta se présente au commencement du IXe siècle. C'est aux Mongols ou plutôt à une de leurs tribus que revient cette dénomination, et non à aucun peuple de race turque

Editorial context

In his notes Thomsen comments on 'Otuz Tatar' (Thirty Tatars) and 'Toquz Tatar' (Nine Tatars): the name meets us here 'for the first time in history', appears in Chinese books only in the ninth century, and belongs to the Mongols or one of their tribes, not to a Turkic people. He places them between the Oghuz (Uighurs) to the west and the Khitan to the east, near the Kerulen river. So the original Tatars were a real Mongolic group on the edge of the Turk empire.

Vilhelm Thomsen, commentary in Inscriptions de l'Orkhon dechiffrees (1896)

Strange talesLake Baikal1783

The lost ancient nation of Lake Baikal: no trace

My inquiries respected an ancient nation, supposed to have once inhabited the borders of the Baikal sea, in the interior of Tartary, and from which some persons conjecture, the learning, arts, and sciences of India, and even of Europe, to have been originally derived. If such a nation ever existed, the remembrance of it seems now to be buried in deepest oblivion.

Editorial context

Turner had read a European theory, fashionable in the 1700s, that a lost ancient people near Lake Baikal had taught arts and sciences to India and Europe. He asked the Manchu-born chamberlain Soopoon Choomboo, who had travelled along the Baikal, about it. The answer: the area was thinly peopled and he knew of no monument showing remote antiquity. A striking early test of a 'lost civilisation of Tartary' idea.

Samuel Turner (1800)
See the original page (p. 279)

About Mongols and Siberian peoples ('northern Tartars')

Keep in mind

The same page adds Soopoon Choomboo's answer: 'nor to his knowledge, was any monument existing, that bore marks of remote antiquity.' Turner's own view is only that the tradition, if real, is forgotten.
