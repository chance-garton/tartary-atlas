Strange talesDerbent1747

Forty Christian Tartar princes buried at Derbent

On the hills around Derbent the 1746 embassy saw many ancient graves and tombstones six to nine feet long, with inscriptions no one could read. Persians told them forty of these belonged to Christian Tartar princes who died for their faith, and that each stone was cut to the exact height of the dead man. The diarist warns that the Persians 'deal much in the marvellous'. (These are probably the Kirkhlyar, the 'Forty' cemetery linked to the early Arab conquest.)

…the Persians report, that of these are not less than 40 belonging to Christian Tartar princes, who have died fighting for their religion. There are many antient tomb-stones, from 6 to 9 feet long, … with inscriptions not intelligible to any of the present inhabitants. The Persians, who deal much in the marvellous, endeavoured to persuade us, that these stones were cut in length exactly to the stature of the deceased

Who 'Christian Tartar' princes (Persian tradition)

Heard itAnonymous journal of the Russian embassy to Persia of 1746, extract printed by Jonas Hanway (1753)

Keep in mind. Local Persian tradition, which the diarist himself doubts. The identification with the Kirkhlyar cemetery is ours, not the source's.

BuildingsCaucasus (south of Derbent)late 1254

Ruined walls from mountain to mountain: 'Alexander's barriers'

A day beyond Derbent Rubruck crossed a valley where the foundations of walls ran from one mountain to the next. He identifies them with the barriers of Alexander meant to hold back 'wild peoples', and explains these rationally as the shepherds of the wilderness kept out of the farmland and cities. He adds that he could learn nothing certain about the other barriers where Jews were said to be enclosed.

The next day we crossed a valley in which the foundations of walls could be seen running from one mountain to another, and over the mountain tops there was no road. These used to be the barriers of Alexander, holding back the wild peoples, that is, the shepherds of the wilderness, so that they could not come into the cultivated lands and the cities.

CitiesDerbentlate 1254

Derbent: the Iron Gate, its towers cut down by the Tartars

Returning south along the Caspian, Rubruck passed Derbent, the 'Iron Gate'. The town ran more than a mile between mountain and sea, with a strong castle on top, very strong walls without ditches and towers of large polished stones; but the Tartars had cut down the tops of the towers and the battlements to the height of the walls. The land within, he says, used to be like a paradise.

It has very strong walls without ditches, and towers of large polished stones; but the Tartars destroyed the tops of the towers and the battlements of the walls, levelling the towers with the wall. Within that city the land used to be like a paradise.

Daily lifeDaghestan coast north of DerbentJune 1670

Captured in Daghestan: dragged behind horses, shot with blunted arrows

After a shipwreck on the Caspian coast, Struys and his companions were stripped by one band and then seized by another, who took them for spies of the rebel Razin. He describes being tied to horses' tails and driven barefoot through thorns, then bound to a tree and struck with arrows whose points had been blunted to make the torment last. Russian slaves of the prince vouched that they were Dutch, which saved their lives (two pages later).

they tied us to the tails of their horses, making us follow backwards and barefoot through the brambles and thorns with which the road was covered … tie me to a tree, where they pierced me with arrows whose iron they had blunted

CitiesDerbentc. 1220

The Iron Gate city of Alexander razed by the Tartars

Sent west with 10,000 horsemen, the Mongol general Jebe ('Gebesagada') took towns before they knew he was coming, killing the armed men but, Sanudo says, sparing the people and leaving gold untouched. At the Caucasus he reached Alexandreta, built by Alexander, later called the Iron Gate, which blocks the way from deep Asia. The Tartars entered it suddenly and 'overthrew both citizens and place from the foundations'.

and he came to the great mountain Caucasus, where Alexander built Alexandreta, which was afterwards called the Iron Gates. This blocks the passage from deep Asia to the greater. This impregnable city the Tartars, entering the land suddenly, overthrew from its foundations, both citizens and place.

The nameDagestan1740s

Dagestan Tartars versus Lezgi Tartars

Cook admits writers were confused about the peoples of the eastern Caucasus and sets his own rule: 'Dagestan Tartars' for the hill people between Derbent and Circassia, 'Lezgi Tartars' for those south and south-east of Derbent to the Kura. It shows 'Tartar' being used simply as a label for Muslim mountaineers who spoke many languages.

The difference between the Dagestan and Lesgee Tartars, I think is not clear in authors; I therefore desire my readers to remember, that I call the Dagestan Tartars, those who inhabit the hills between Derbent and Circassia; and I call those who inhabit the hills south, and south-east of Derbent, all the way to the north banks of Cura, Lesgee Tartars.

Who Dagestani highlanders

Read itJohn Cook (published 1770)

CitiesDerbent1254

Derbent's stone towers, cut down by the Tartars

Rubruck passed through the Iron Gate (Derbent) on the Caspian. He credits Alexander the Great with building it, following medieval legend, and describes a narrow town with strong walls and towers of dressed stone. The Tartars had levelled the tops of the towers and the parapets, making them even with the walls. Rockhill's note says Muslim geographers credited the Persian king Khosrow Anushirvan.

The next day we came to the Iron Gate, which Alexander the Macedonian made … It has very strong walls without moats, and towers of great dressed (politis) stones ; but the Tartars have destroyed the tops of the towers and the parapets of the walls, making the towers even with the walls.

Who Tartars (Mongols)

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Keep in mind. The Alexander attribution is legend; the walls are Sasanian Persian work.

CitiesTarki, DagestanAugust 1722

Tarki, capital of 'Dagestan Tartary'

Peter the Great's army camped below Tarki, seat of the shamkhal. Bruce describes a town of over 3,000 houses on a hillside, without walls, with two-storey flat-roofed houses where women walked in the evening, each with its own garden and spring.

This city of Tarku, being the metropolis of Dagestan, contains above three thousand houses, and is very full of inhabitants ; the houses all two stories high, platformed at top, standing close to each other : the women walk upon them in the cool of the evenings, as the men do in the streets. Every house has a garden stored with all kinds of delicious fruits

Who Kumyks ('Dagestan Tartars')

BuildingsDerbentJanuary 1747

Towers on the hills credited to Alexander

Cook describes Derbent's huge walls of natural shell-stone and then notes towers on the hills to the west. The inhabitants told him Alexander the Great built them and ran them all the way to the Black Sea to hold back the Dagestan Tartars. This echoes the old legend of Alexander's wall against Gog and Magog.

Upon the tops of the hills, west from Derbent, are to be seen some towers, which the inhabitants say were built by Alexander the Great; and that he continued these towers from Derbent west to the Black Sea, in order to curb the Dagestan Tartars.

Who 'Dagestan Tartars' (Muslim highlanders)

Heard itJohn Cook (published 1770)

Keep in mind. Local tradition; the Derbent walls are Sasanian Persian work of the 6th century, not Alexander's.

A 'Tartarian or Circassian' village of four hundred houses

Marching south from the Terek, the embassy reached a large village of 300 to 400 houses called Andreofske Derevna, ruled by a prince named Alish under Russian protection. Cook is unsure whether to call it Tartar or Circassian. The prince, drunk from celebrating, showed the scars of many raids and boasted of horses stolen from Turkey and Georgia.

at seven o'clock at night we arrived, very cold, in a large Tartarian or Circassian village, called Andreofske Derevna, consisting of between three and four hundred houses. The prince of this village is called Alish, and is under the protection of Russia.

The nameDerbent (Caspian Gates)1823

Could anyone know the name 'Tatar' in the 6th century?

In a note, Frähn discusses a 16th-century Ottoman writer who claimed the Persian king Anushirvan (6th century) built the Caspian Gates for fear of 'Turks and Tatars'. Frähn replies that although Arab writers mention the Tatars earlier than some scholars thought, the name can hardly have been known that early in western Asia. It is a scholarly warning against reading 'Tatar' back into ancient history.

for although Arab writers mention the Tatars earlier than is noted in the excellent Mémoires sur l'Arménie (vol. II, p. 263), one can hardly assume that their name was known so early in western Asia.

CitiesTarki (Makhachkala), Dagestan1638

Tarku, an unwalled capital of a thousand houses

On the way home the embassy spent five weeks at Tarku (Tarki), capital of Dagestan, whose people Olearius calls Tartars. The town had no walls and about a thousand houses built in the Persian manner, set among rocks full of fossil shells. He found the local lords frightening: over dinner, he says, they talked only of robbing and selling people.

The City is not wall'd. It may contain a thousand houses, built according to the Persian way, but not so well. These Tartars, as also those of … and the others who live more Northerly, are called Kaitacks

Daily lifeDagestan1747

Muslim Tartars with stone crosses and Christian feast days

Cook says all the Tartars from Astrakhan to Derbent were Sunni Muslims, but he suspected many had once been Christians. They held yearly gatherings they could not explain, which he says fell on Greek Orthodox feast days, and he was told there were many stone crosses in the hills where they still met.

The Tartars from the borders of Astrachan to Derbent, are all Mahometans, of the sect of the Turks; but some things make me believe, that a majority of them had formerly professed the Christian religion. They have meetings at certain times of the year, where they divert themselves, without being able to account for the cause, any other way than that their ancestors did so before them

Who 'Tartars' between Astrakhan and Derbent (Kumyks and Dagestani peoples)

Heard itJohn Cook (published 1770)

Keep in mind. Cook's inference; the crosses are reported, not seen.

CitiesNorth Caucasus lowlands north of Derbent (Dagestan)January 1747

A Tartar town with whitewashed clay walls

The Russian embassy of 1746 passed a Tartar town under Russian protection, ruled by a prince called Alt Sheff, on a high river bank on the road north of Derbent. It had about 300 houses with clay walls whitened with lime. The diarist grumbles that its people were the most skilful thieves in the world.

…arrived at a tartar town under the Russian protection; though otherwise a kind of independent state. The prince of it was called Alt Sheff: it stands on a high bank on the north side of the river …, and contains about 300 houses; the walls of which are of clay whitened with lime. The inhabitants are the most acute thieves in the world,

Who Tartars of northern Dagestan (probably Kumyks)

Daily lifeDaghestanc. 1468

Plundered by the Kaitaks, 'a Tartar tribe'

After a storm wrecks one of their boats on the Caspian shore, Nikitin's party is seized and robbed by the Kaitaks near Tarki. The Shah of Shirvan then negotiates with the Kaitak prince, his brother-in-law, for their release. The footnote labels the Kaitaks a Tartar tribe of Daghestan, an example of how widely the label was stretched.

A storm having arisen at sea, the smaller boat was wrecked on shore. Then came the Kaitaks and made the whole party prisoners, and we came to Derbend

Who Kaitaks (called a Tartar tribe by the editor)

Saw itAfanasy Nikitin (translated by Count Wielhorski, 1857); tribe identified in editorial footnote

Keep in mind. The Kaitaks of Daghestan were a Caucasian people; 'Tartar tribe' is the 1857 note's label, not Nikitin's.
