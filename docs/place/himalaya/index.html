'Tartar cookery' on the road to Ladakh

Editorial contextMoorcroft saw his Lahouli porters cook a sheep that had died in the night: skinned, cut up and boiled whole in a big cauldron with all the innards, together with cakes of flour mixed with the blood. He calls it 'Tartar cookery' and admits he did not care to join in.

We had an opportunity here of witnessing Tartar cookery on a more sumptuous scale than usual, but it was not of a character to invite a participation in the meal. A sheep belonging to some of the carriers having died in the night, the carcase was skinned and cut up, and boiled in a large cauldron, together with all the viscera

William Moorcroft (edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)

The Himalaya as a 'Tartar bulwark'

Editorial contextStewart describes the mountain wall north of Bengal as a continuation of the Caucasus and calls it a 'stupendous Tartar bulwark' that the Mughals and other Muslim conquerors had always thought impassable. On the same page he says the Jesuit account of Tibet in Du Halde was compiled 'from Tartar relations'. Here 'Tartar' simply means everything beyond the mountains.

This stupendous Tartar bulwark had ever been held impassable by the Mogols, and all other Mussulman conquerors of India:

John Stewart (Philosophical Transactions, 1777)
