The nameCentral Asia (Tartary)1760s (theory)

Tartary as the first land after the Flood

Touching on the 'Atlantis' debate of his day, de Tott argues that the high plateau of Tartary must have been the first land inhabited in Asia, the source of the swarms that later poured into Europe as Goths, Ostrogoths and Visigoths. He says a Tartar tradition told him by Khan Krim Giray supports this, and on the next page traces the Crimean dynasty back to Genghis Khan.

this country, possessed at present by the Tartars, must have been the first discovered land in Asia, and the earliest inhabited; the source of that population which first sent forth those swarms which, constantly driven back by the wall of China, and the defiles of Thibet and of Caucasus, fell upon the northern parts of Asia, and overflowed our Europe, under the name of Goths, Ostrogoths, and Visigoths.

Legend or rumourBaron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

About Tartars (in general)

Keep in mind

A speculative 18th-century theory based on geography and one conversation, not on evidence; de Tott himself calls it a 'hypothesis'.

BuildingsTartary ('Lechune', unidentified)1544 (claimed)

The Tartar 'Rome': 27 royal tombs and 280 monasteries

Travelling on with the Tartars, Pinto describes Lechune, 'head of the false religion of this heathendom, as Rome is among us', with a sumptuous temple holding the tombs of twenty-seven kings or emperors 'of this Tartar monarchy' in chapels lined with silver, and nearby a walled enclosure of 280 monasteries with 42,000 priests. It is a vivid and entirely unverifiable picture of Tartar monumental building.

we came to a city called Lechune, which is the head of the false religion of this heathendom, as Rome is among us, in which there is a very sumptuous temple, with very remarkable buildings, in which twenty-seven kings or emperors of this Tartar monarchy are buried, in very rich chapel tombs

Working translation

Original wording

chegamos a hũa cidade que se chamaua Lechune, que he cabeça da falsa religião desta gentilidade, como o he Roma entre nos, na qual està hũ templo muyto sumptuoso, & de edificios muyto notaueis, em que estão sepultados vinte & sete Reys ou Emperadores desta Tartara Monarchia, em jazigos de capellas muyto ricas

Saw itFernão Mendes Pinto (Peregrinaçam, Lisbon 1614)

About Tartar 'monarchy' and its priests

Keep in mind

Pinto's Tartar episodes are widely regarded as fictional or built from hearsay; treat as claimed travel. Lechune may blend reports of Tibetan or Chinese Buddhist centres.

BuildingsTartary (unlocated, imaginary)Printed 1689

Lead domes, gilded cupolas and three classical orders

The book dresses its Tartar city in the European classical style of its own day: porticos on pillars and pilasters of the Doric, Ionic and Corinthian orders in the three wards, house roofs of lead shaped like domes with gilded copper cupolas, and at the centre a temple 'like the Pantheon at Rome'. It is a European architect's dream set in Tartary, not a description of anything built there.

The Roof of these Portico's is supported by Pillars without, and Pilasters within … The Doors and Windows observe the same Symmetry. The tops of their Houses are cover'd with Lead, in a Figure of a Duomo

Legend or rumour'Heliogenes de L'Epy' (English translation, 1689)

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Qing 'Imperial Tartary')c. 1689 to 1696

Tartars in huts, now made to build villages

Describing the lands between Beijing and the Russian frontier, Careri says the vast 'Imperial Tartary' owned by the emperor of China was uncultivated and wooded, and its 'poor Tartars' lived in huts without walled houses, like the ancient nomads with portable dwellings. But the present emperor was now 'civilising' them, beginning to have villages built and gathering them in, with forty tributary princes including the Khalkha.

those poor Tartars living there under huts, without walled houses, in the manner of the ancient Numidians or Hamaxobians, who, all given to the pastoral life, did not even have houses but went to live wherever it was most convenient with their portable huts. But the present Emperor of Tartary, now civilising them well, is beginning to have villages built for them and to gather them into these in communities

Working translation

Original wording

vivendovi que' poveri Tartari sotto capanne, senza case murate, a modo degli antichi Numidi, o Hamaxobiti, che tutti intesi alla vita pastorale, nè men case avevano, ma con loro portatili mapali, ove più concio era, andavano ad abitare. Ma l'Imperador odierno della Tartaria, bene al presente dirozzandogli comincia a far loro fabbricare Ville, e raccorgli entro a quelle in comunanza

Heard itGiovanni Francesco Gemelli Careri (Giro del Mondo, part IV, Naples 1700)

About Mongols under Qing rule ('poor Tartars'; Khalkha)

Keep in mind

Careri did not travel there; this comes from Jesuits at court (Grimaldi, Gerbillon, Pereira). The classical comparison is his own.

CitiesTartary (unlocated, imaginary)Claimed travels of the mid-1600s; printed 1689

Heliopolis: a round city in Tartary with streets like Amsterdam

From the top of a column like London's Monument, the narrator surveys Heliopolis. It is round, about three miles across, ringed by a moat fed by rivers, with no building over two storeys. It has three wards named after the Sun, the Council and the Schools, and wide, clean, straight streets like Amsterdam's, lined with trees and flushed by running water.

It is divided into three Quarters or Wards, of which the first in the heart of the City, derives its name from the Sun; the second from the Council; and the third from the Schools. The Streets are wide, clean and streight, like those of Amsterdam

Legend or rumour'Heliogenes de L'Epy' (English translation, 1689)

About The 'Heliopolitans', a fictional people

Keep in mind

Almost certainly fiction: the city is an ideal community in the manner of Campanella's City of the Sun, not a place any traveller recorded.

Strange talesNorthern China and Tartaryreported 1585

Mass weddings by lot 'toward Tartary'

Mendoza passes on a story that in the provinces toward Tartary, and in Tartary itself, governors set an age by which men and women must either enter religion or marry. At the appointed time all who are to marry gather in a designated city, where twelve elders record their names and wealth; if there are more men than women, they draw lots, and the surplus wait until next year. He introduces it with 'they say'.

They say that in the provinces toward Tartary, and in Tartary itself, a certain very strange manner of marrying is customary, which is that the viceroys or governors have set a time for both men and women, so that on reaching a certain age they are obliged either to enter religion or to marry.

Working translation

Original wording

Dizen que en las Prouincias hazia la Tartaria, y en la mesma Tartaria, se acostumbra vna cierta manera de casar muy estraña, y es que los Virreyes, o Gouernadores tienen señalado tiempo, assi a los hombres como a las mugeres paraque hasta llegar a tal edad tengan obligacion, o de meterse en Religion, o de casarse.

Legend or rumourJuan González de Mendoza (Historia del gran Reyno de la China, Rome 1585)

About Peoples of the northern provinces and 'Tartary itself'

Keep in mind

Hearsay introduced with 'they say'; the story echoes older European tales (such as Herodotus's Babylonian bride market) and has no clear Asian source.

The nameTartary (unlocated, imaginary)Printed 1689

Philosophers protected by the Great Kam

The book explains how its peaceful city survives among the Tartars: the Great Kam admires the citizens' honesty and lets them live in peace, and in return they send him a volunteer regiment of 300 young men. That service, the author says, is how they learn the 'Tartarian Language' needed for trade at their fair.

Now altho' they lye surrounded by the Tartarians, yet are they not at War with any body : For the Great Kam, who has always had a high esteem for them, by reason of their Integrity, permits them to live in Peace and Tranquillity one among another.

Legend or rumour'Heliogenes de L'Epy' (English translation, 1689)

About 'Tartarians' and their 'Great Kam'

Keep in mind

Fiction; the 'Great Kam' is the stock European name for a Tartar emperor, used here without any real ruler in view.

Strange talesTartary (unlocated, imaginary)Printed 1689

Why can't there be another Heliopolis, in Tartary?

In his preface the author answers readers who doubted his story. Many cities share a name, he argues (two Frankforts, two Lyons, two Chalons), so why deny that besides Heliopolis in Egypt there could be the one he describes in Tartary? He also compares his doubters to those who laughed at the idea of the Antipodes before Columbus.

Why must it be deny'd, but that besides Heliopolis in Egypt, there may be that other of which I have given the Relation in Tartary.

Legend or rumour'Heliogenes de L'Epy', preface (English translation, 1689)
Keep in mind

The protest of truth is a standard device of imaginary voyages.

Daily lifeTartary (inner Asia)c.1650, from European atlases

'Like wind and water': Katib Celebi on the Tatar character

Continuing from the European source, Katib Celebi describes the Tatars as steel-natured among mankind, restless as wind and water, with the strength of Ahriman and the might of demons. They roam the deserts like the wind; they neither eat bread nor build houses, their horses need neither shoes nor nails, and they cross rivers by swimming. It is the classic European literary picture of the steppe nomad, translated into Ottoman.

Qualities of the Tatars: this people are of steel nature among mankind, and like water and wind their nature is used to motion; they have the strength of Ahriman and the power of demons … they run like the wind; neither do they tire nor do their horses; the horses they ride need neither shoe nor nail.

Working translation

Original wording

واوصاف تاتار بوقوم بنی نوع انسان میانه‌ده پولاد نهادلر وآب وباد کبی طبیعتلری حرکته معتاد اهرمن قوت ودیو زورلو ... ایل کبی یلرلر نه کندولر یورولور نه اتلری درلر بندکلری آته نه نعل حاجت نه میخ

Read itKatib Celebi, Cihannuma (Muteferrika print, Istanbul 1732)

About Tatars (generic)

Keep in mind

A stereotype copied from European books, not observation; a few words at the line breaks are uncertain in the print.
