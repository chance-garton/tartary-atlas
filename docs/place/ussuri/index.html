The fish-skin Tartars of the Ussuri

On the Ussuri, Grosier says, live the 'Yupi-tatse' (Chinese for 'fish-skin Tartars'). They live almost entirely on fish and dress in fish skins, dyed three or four colours and stitched so finely with thin thongs that the seams look like silk. On the next pages he describes their bark canoes, hair hung with little mirrors, and a lady who drove a sledge pulled by a hundred dogs.

The river Ousouri, on the banks of which these people live, must supply them with abundance of fish, since they have no other food, and scarcely any clothes but what they make of their skins. They have the art of dressing these skins, and of dying them three or four colours ;

Who Yupi-tatse Tartars (Hezhe/Nanai, Tungusic fishing people)

Read itJean-Baptiste Grosier (English translation, 1788)

Keep in mind. From the Jesuit map-makers who surveyed Manchuria about 1709; Grosier calls them 'stupid and clownish', a prejudice he inherits.

The 'dog-using' and 'deer-using' tribes of the Amur

The gazetteer lists the northeastern peoples who brought tribute to the Qing post at Ningguta, measuring each by distance: the Fiyaka seven or eight hundred li beyond the three-river confluence, the Kiler three thousand li away on the sea coast, the Kuyala facing Korea across the Tumen. Beyond the 'dog-using' tribes lay a 'deer-using' (reindeer) tribe. These are the peoples European maps grouped in 'Eastern Tartary'.

Another seven or eight hundred li to the northeast are the Fiyaka. Three thousand li northeast of Ningguta are the Kiler, who border the great sea on the east... The Kuyala: their land lies on the north bank of the Tumen river, opposite Kyongwon in Korea... And the deer-using tribe lies beyond the dog-using tribes.
