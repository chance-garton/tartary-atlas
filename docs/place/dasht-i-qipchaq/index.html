The Dasht of Berke: 'a land of the Tatars alone'

Describing the steppe that Timur coveted, Ibn Arabshah calls it the Dasht of Berke (after the Golden Horde khan) and says it was a country belonging to the Tatars alone, teeming with herds and Turkish tribes, well guarded, well peopled and healthy in water and air. He had lived there himself in the 1410s and 1420s.

the Dasht of Berke. It was a country belonging to the Tatars alone, and teeming with every kind of livestock and with the tribes of the Turks; its borders guarded, its sides well peopled, its reaches wide, its water and air wholesome.

The namePontic-Caspian steppe (Sartaq's camp)1253

'Do not call our lord a Christian, nor us Tartars'

As Rubruck left Sartaq's camp, the secretary Coiac and other court scribes warned him not to say their lord was a Christian: he was a Moal. Rubruck explains that the Mongols treat 'Christian' as the name of a nation and are so proud that they want their own name, Moal, above every other, and will not be called Tartars either, because the Tartars had been a different people.

'Do not say that our lord is a Christian. He is not a Christian but a Moal'; for the name of Christianity seems to them the name of some nation. They are raised to such pride that, though they may perhaps believe something about Christ, they will not be called Christians, wishing to exalt their own name, that is Moal, above every name; nor will they be called Tartars. For the Tartars were another people, about whom I learned as follows.

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1253

How kumiss is made

Rubruck gives a step-by-step account of kumiss, fermented mare's milk: foals tied to a rope, mares milked, the milk churned in a skin with a club until it bubbles like new wine and turns pungent. He found it tasted of almond milk and 'intoxicates weak heads'. On the next page he says Batu received the milk of three thousand mares every day.

When they have got together a great quantity of milk, which is as sweet as cow's as long as it is fresh, they pour it into a big skin or bottle, and they set to churning it with a stick prepared for that purpose

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Houses built on wheels and covered in white felt

Rubruck opens his description of the Tartars with their homes. The sleeping-houses sit on wheels, are made of interlaced wooden laths that rise to a smoke-hole like a chimney, and are covered with white felt smeared with lime, white earth or powdered bone so that they gleam. He stresses that the Tartars have no fixed dwelling and divide the steppe from the Danube eastward into seasonal pastures.

The houses in which they sleep are set on wheels … [made of] interlaced pieces of wood, and they come together at the top in an opening like a chimney, and they cover this with white felt, which they coat with lime or earth

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Mounds, statues and brick towers over the dead

Crossing the steppe, Rubruck saw the tombs of the Comans: great mounds (kurgans) topped by a stone statue holding a cup, small pointed 'pyramids', tiled towers and stone houses, though there was no stone nearby. Just before, he notes that the burial places of Mongol nobles of Genghis's family were kept secret and guarded. Rockhill's note quotes Carpini on Mongol nobles buried with a horse and a mare.

raise a great tumulus over the dead, and set up a statue to him, its face to the east, and holding a cup in its hand at the height of the navel. They make also pyramids to the rich, that is to say, little pointed structures, and in some places I saw great tiled covered towers, and in others stone houses, though there were no stones thereabout.

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1517-1526

Drinking their horses' blood, prizing mare's milk

On long rides, Herberstein was told, Tartars open a vein in their horse and drink the blood, believing it does the animal good. They steer by the Pole Star, which they call the 'iron nail'. On the same page he describes their love of mare's milk and how chiefs share out the entrails of a slaughtered animal and lick the knife clean.

Moreover, if during a long ride they are troubled with hunger or thirst, it is a practice to lance the veins of the horses on which they sit, and relieve their craving by drawing their blood ; and they think that this is an advantage to the animals. As they nearly all wander on uncertain tracks, they are accustomed to direct their course by the observation of the stars

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1253

How kumiss is made, and what it tastes like

Rubruck explains the Mongols' summer drink, fermented mare's milk. Foals are tethered on a line so the mares let themselves be milked; the milk is churned in a skin with a club until it bubbles like new wine and turns sour. He found it prickled the tongue, left a taste of almonds and could make a weak head drunk; a clear black kind, cara-cosmos, was kept for the great.

they pour it into a skin or other vessel, where they beat and stir it very well … it begins to bubble like new wine, then to turn sour like leaven … it stings the tongue like rough wine when you drink it. When you have finished drinking, it leaves a taste of almonds on the tongue and greatly cheers the heart

Cuman mounds, stone statues and brick towers

Among the burial customs he saw on the steppe, Rubruck notes that the Cumans raised a great mound over the dead and set up a statue facing east holding a cup at the navel. For the rich they built small pointed 'pyramids', and in places he saw tall towers of baked brick and even stone houses, though no stone was found there. He also saw a newly dead man with the hides of sixteen horses hung on tall poles around him.

The Cumans raise a great mound over the dead man and set up a statue to him, its face turned to the east, holding a cup in its hand in front of the navel. For the rich they also build pyramids, that is, small pointed houses, and in some places I saw great towers of baked tiles, in others stone houses, although stones are not found there. I saw one man newly dead for whom they had hung the hides of sixteen horses, four towards each quarter of the world, between tall poles

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1253

Women whose headdresses look like helmets and lances

Married Mongol women wore the bocca (bogtak), a tall bark frame covered with silk and topped with a tuft of quills and peacock feathers. Rubruck says a group of ladies riding in the distance looked like soldiers in helmets with lances raised, and that women rode astride like men. He adds, less kindly, that the fattest women with the smallest noses were thought the most beautiful.

and one sees them from afar, they look like soldiers, helmets on head and lances erect. For this bocca looks like a helmet, and the tuft above it is like a lance. And all the women sit their horses astraddle like men.

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppec. 1436 to 1452

Nomads who sowed and reaped

Barbaro answers the obvious question of where so many people got their grain. Each February the horde announced where sowing would happen; volunteers went with seed, carts and families, ploughed and sowed, and later returned to harvest while the khan's camp circled nearby. He says the yields were huge.

About the mooneth of Februarie they make proclamacons throughout the Lordo, that he which woll sowe shall prepare his things necessarie against the mooneth of Marche, to sowe in such a place. … And there do they eare, sowe, and tarie, till they haue furnisshed that they came for, which doon they retorne to their Lordo.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

A house thirty feet wide, pulled by twenty-two oxen

Some Mongol houses were never taken down but rode on huge carts. Rubruck measured one cart himself and counted the oxen. The axle was as thick as a ship's mast and a driver stood in the doorway of the house. On the next pages he describes felt-covered chests on carts and a rich man's camp of one or two hundred carts.

I myself once measured the width between the wheel-tracks of a cart xx feet, and when the house was on the cart it projected beyond the wheels on either side v feet at least. I have myself counted to one cart xxii oxen drawing one house, eleven abreast across the width of the cart, and the other eleven before them.

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Kazakh horde, Great and Lesser Nogais: the peoples on the old map

The preface lists what the old Great Map showed in the south and east: the 'nomad Kazakh horde', the camps of the Great Nogais, the towns of Bukhara and Urgench, the Volga to Astrakhan and the Don to Azov, and the fields between Azov and Astrakhan 'where the Lesser Nogais roam'. The steppe is named by its nomad peoples, not by a single 'Tartary'.

and the nomad Kazakh horde, and the nomad camps of the Great Nogais, and the Bukharan and Urgench towns, and the river Volga to Astrakhan, and the river Don to Azov, and between Azov and Astrakhan to the Khvalyn [Caspian] and to the Azov and Black Seas, the fields and rivers where the Lesser Nogais roam.

Strange talesPontic-Caspian steppeabout 1220 (hearsay)

A plain without cities where the sun rises before a chop is cooked

Far to the north-west lies 'K'o-fu-ch'a', which Bretschneider identifies with the Kipchak steppe north of the Caspian. Yelü Chucai heard that it was endless plain, its people had no cities or towns, bred cattle and horses and made a drink from honey, and that summer nights were so short the sun rose again before a mutton chop could be cooked. Bretschneider shows the short-night detail was copied from a Tang-era account of a Siberian people.

To the north-west of Yin-du (Hindustan) is the realm of K'o fu-ch'a… The people there have no cities or towns. They breed much cattle and horses. They make a fermented beverage from honey. In that country the days (in summer) are long and the nights short. In little more than the time necessary to cook a mutton chop, the sun rises again.

Yelü Chucai's Si yu lu, in a Yuan-era abridgement (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Keep in mind

Hearsay, partly borrowed from the Tang history; the Mongols did not conquer the Kipchak steppe until 1236.

Cuman grave mounds, statues and brick towers on the steppe

Crossing the steppe, Rubruck describes the tombs of the Cumans, the Turkic people the Mongols had conquered there. They raised a mound over the dead with a statue facing east holding a cup; for the rich they built little pointed houses, and he saw great brick towers and stone houses in a land without stone. The same page notes that nobody knew where Chinggis Khan was buried.

As for the Cumans, they are accustomed to raise a mound over the grave of the dead man and set up a statue for him facing east … For the rich and great they put up pyramids, or little pointed houses, and in some places I saw great towers of brick, and in others houses built of stone, although no stone is found in those parts.

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe (Dasht-i Kipchak)c.1332

Nine animals for one stolen

Crossing the Kipchak steppe by hired cart (araba), Ibn Battuta noticed huge herds grazing with no herdsmen. He explains it by the harsh law on theft: a thief had to return the animal with nine more, and if he could not, his children were taken, or he himself killed. He also notes that the treeless steppe folk burned dung for fuel.

he is compelled to return it with nine others of equal value. But, if this is not in his power, his children are taken. If, however, he have no children, he is himself slaughtered just like a sheep.

Ibn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppeJune 1253

First taste of kumiss, and a moving city of carts

Rubruck says that in two months between Soldaia and Sartaq's camp they never slept under a roof and saw no village, nor any trace of a building, except great numbers of Cuman tombs. One evening his guide gave him kumiss; he broke into a sweat from horror and novelty, but found it very tasty. Next morning the carts of the commander Scatatai, loaded with houses, looked to him like a great city coming towards him.

That evening the lad who was guiding us gave us kumiss to drink, and at the first draught I sweated all over from horror and novelty, for I had never drunk it before. Yet it seemed to me very tasty, as indeed it is. In the morning we met Scatatai's carts loaded with houses, and it seemed to me that a great city was coming to meet me.

Felt houses thirty feet wide, pulled by 22 oxen

Rubruck describes the Mongol felt houses built on a frame of rods and carried whole on carts. He measured one cart's wheel track at 20 feet, with the house overhanging by at least 5 feet each side, and counted 22 oxen pulling one house, in two rows of eleven, with an axle as big as a ship's mast and a driver standing in the doorway.

'May you stay in one place like a Christian'

Herberstein reports that the roving Tartars thought staying put was a misfortune, and that their worst curse on a child was to wish it a fixed home like a Christian's. He is careful to add that Tartars who live in towns and cities live differently, so he is describing the herders, not all Tartars.

They never stay for any length of time in one spot, for they consider it a great calamity to be obliged to remain long in the same place ; hence, when they are angry with their children, and wish to utter a heavy imprecation against them, they are accustomed to say, " may you abide in one place continually like a Christian, and inhale your own stink !"

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1588-1591

No towns, only houses on wheels

Fletcher says the Tartars build no towns or standing buildings. They live in wheeled 'walking houses' which they park in rows to form streets and the shape of a town, and even the khan's capital is a moving 'town of wood'. They reportedly think fixed buildings unhealthy.

Townes they plant none, nor other standing buildings, but have walking houses, which the Latines call veii, built upon wheeles like a shepheards cottage. … And when they come to their stage or standing place, they plant their cart houses very orderly in a ranke ; and so make the forme of streetes and of a large towne.

Giles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

About Crimean and Nogai Tartars (Fletcher's general 'Tartar')

Keep in mind

CitiesPontic-Caspian steppe1253

A rich Tartar's camp looks like a big town

The Mongol camp could look like a settlement. Each wife of a great lord had her own large house with a train of smaller wagon-houses (Rubruck says Batu's wives each had more than 200), and the wives' courts were pitched in a row from west to east, a stone's throw apart. The result, he says, resembles a large town, though few men live in it.

the first wife has her little court set up to the west, then all the others do the same, each in her rank, so that the last is at the east, and the space between them is about a stone's throw: so that the court of one of these rich Tartars looks like a big town, although there will be very few men in it.

A round frame of sticks covered with white felt

Rubruck describes the Mongol dwelling (the yurt or ger): a circular frame of interlaced sticks meeting in a hoop at the top with a chimney collar, covered in felt. The felt is whitened with chalk, clay or bone powder and embroidered with vines, trees, birds and beasts. Rockhill's note adds Carpini's description of the same round felt tents.

converging into a little round hoop on the top, from which projects above a collar as a chimney, and this (framework) they cover over with white felt. Frequently they coat the felt with chalk, or white clay, or powdered bone, to make it appear whiter, and sometimes also (they make the felt) black.

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

The road to Cathay is safe, day and night

According to merchants who had used it, the road from Tana to Cathay was perfectly safe by day and by night. The one risk was death: if a merchant died on the way, the lord of the land took everything he carried, unless a brother or close companion was there to claim it. On the same page Pegolotti advises hiring an interpreter and two servants who speak Cuman, the Turkic language of the steppe, and taking flour and salt fish for the journey, since meat is plentiful everywhere.

The road from Tana to Cathay is perfectly safe, by day and by night, according to what the merchants say who have used it; except that if a merchant, going or coming, should die on the road, everything would belong to the lord of the country where he died, and the lord's officials would take it all

Why 'Golden Horde': the khan's gilded tent

On Abu'l-Fida's remark that the horde of the Tartar prince of Berke's country comes as far as Ukek, Reinaud explains that horde (Turkish ordu) means tent: the Chinggisid khans moved with the seasons and wherever they pitched their tents was called the horde. He suggests the Russians called the dynasty the Golden Horde because the khan's tent stood on wooden posts plated with gold, citing Ibn Battuta.

The word horde, in Arabic ordu, belongs to the Turkish language and means tent. The Tartar princes of the race of Chinggis Khan who ruled the Qipchaq steppe used to change their residence with the seasons, and the place where they pitched their tents was called the horde … probably because the tent the khan occupied rested on wooden posts covered with plates of gold

The 'valiant fool'

The bravest Tatar warriors, Barbaro says, earned the title 'Tulubagator', which he translates 'valiant fool', an honour like 'the wise' or 'the handsome' in Italy. The editor explains it as tulu bahadur; bahadur is the root of the Russian bogatyr, hero. He follows it with the story of a Tatar merchant who chased forty Circassians alone.

The hablemen are verie valiaunt and hardie, in such wise that some of them for their excellencie are called Tulubagator, which signifieth a valiaunt foole : being a name of no lesse reputacon amongst them than the sernames of wisedome or beaultie with vs

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

The Tartars of the West and their kings

Polo calls the Golden Horde the 'Tartars of the Ponent' (of the West). Their first king, 'Sain', conquered Russia, the Cumans, Alans, Circassians, Goths of Crimea and Khazaria. He lists the later khans down to Toqta, who reigned in Polo's time. Yule's note points out that 'Sain' (the Good) was really Batu's own surname, so Polo turned one khan into two.

Justice on the spot

In the camp, Barbaro says, quarrels were settled without violence or paperwork: the parties walked off together and asked the first respectable man they met to judge, and his verdict was accepted, with bystanders as witnesses. He also describes petitioners kneeling repeatedly as they approached the lord's pavilion.

Whan a difference groweth betwene partie and partie, and wordes multiplied (not aftre the maner of our quarters, for these do vse no violence), thei both or moo (if they be moo) arise and go what waie they thinke good : and to the first man of any estimacon that they meete they saie : Master, do vs right, for we here are in controversie

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Khan Uzbek turns Muslim, with an army counted in tens

Sanudo warns the Pope that the Sultan of Egypt, with gifts and treaties, had drawn 'the Emperor of the Tartars named Husbecho, who rules in Gazaria and other northern parts' to accept Islam. Trustworthy people told him Uzbek's cavalry was so vast that, his army being organised in tens, he could take just three of every ten and still field an almost unbelievable host.

the said Sultan so changed and drew the Emperor of the Tartars, named Uzbek, who rules in Gazaria and other northern parts, that he received the law of the faithless Muhammad ... For it is asserted by some trustworthy people that the said Uzbek has so numerous a multitude of horsemen that, taking three out of ten, since he has his army ordered by tens, from the threes alone he puts so large a force in the field that it seems almost incredible.

Felt idols on the doors of their wagons

Fletcher says that, unlike Turks, Tartars keep silk or cloth human figures fastened to the doors of their 'walking houses' as house guardians, made by religious women, and bow to a huge image of their khan when the army marches. He also calls them much given to witchcraft and omens.

Herein they differ from the Turkish religion, for that they have certeine idole puppets, made of silke or like stuffe, of the fashion of a man, which they fasten to the doore of their walking houses, to be as Janusses or keepers of their house. And these idols are made not by all, but by certeine religious women

Giles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

Islam arrives, idols linger

Barbaro says Islam had taken hold among the Tartars only about a hundred years before he wrote, in the time of the warlord Edigu. Before that, some were Muslims but each believed what he liked, and some worshipped idols of wood and rags carried on their carts. On another page a Tatar tells him there are still plenty of secret idolaters.

The faith of Macomett beganne to take place amonge the Tartariens about an C yeres past. In dede some of them were Macomettanes before, but everie man was at his libertie to believe what hym best liked ; so that some worshipped ymags of woode, and of ragges

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Kipchak: the boy born in a hollow tree

The legendary conqueror Oghuz Khan's officer died in battle, and his pregnant widow gave birth inside a hollow tree. The khan raised the boy and named him Kipchak, said to mean 'hollow tree' in old Turkish. The Kipchaks gave their name to the Dasht-i Kipchak, the steppe that later became Batu's Golden Horde.

she could find no other Place to retire to when her Time was come but an old Hollow-Tree; into which having crept when her Labour came on, she there brought forth a Son. … and gave him the Name of Kipzak, which signifies in the antient Turkish, an Hollow-Tree.

Abu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

The Qipchaq steppe: pasture, nomads and few towns

Mustawfi's short entry on the Dasht-i Qipchaq, the Golden Horde's steppe, says it has rich pasture but few houses, towns or villages, and that most people are nomads. He then lists its towns, including 'Saray Batu', the Horde capital on the Volga. Le Strange's footnote places the ruins of Saray above Astrakhan.

Dasht-i-Qipchaq. This is of the Sixth Clime, its plains bear excellent pasturage, stretching to the north of the Caspian Sea, but there are here few houses or towns or villages. Most of the inhabitants are nomads of the plain. Its most celebrated town is Khazar … then there is Burtas, also Suvar, Muhtal, Saray Batu and Sarir

Hamdallah Mustawfi (translated by G. Le Strange, 1919)

Berke, first Muslim khan of the Golden Horde

Batu's brother and successor Berke ('Burga'), Abu'l Ghazi says, met merchants from Bukhara on the road, was won over by their explanations of Islam, adopted it on the spot, and on his return ordered his subjects to follow. It is an early step in the Horde's move to Islam, the religion of the later Volga and Crimean Tatars.

he met upon the Road with several Merchants of Great Bucharia and falling into discourse with some of them, particularly upon the Articles of the Mahometan Faith, he was so pierced with the Explanations which these Men gave him of it, that he resolv'd off-hand to embrace that Religion

Abu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)
