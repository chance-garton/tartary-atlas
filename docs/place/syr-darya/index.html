BuildingsZhanadarya (Djan-deria), Syr Darya delta1820

Mud ruins the Kazakhs credited to the Nogais

It is clear these buildings, whatever they may have been, were built with sun-burnt bricks; near these ruins are small canals about 1 toise or 6 feet broad and two deep; a great quantity of broken pottery is also to be met with. … The Kirghiz could give us no account of their use or date, but supposing they must have been erected by the Nogais

Editorial context

Along a dried-up river channel Meyendorff saw long earth mounds with canals and broken pottery, the remains of buildings of sun-dried brick. The Kazakhs could not say when or why they were built but guessed they were the work of the Nogais, who had held the steppe before them. The best-known ruin, Djankend, was of fired brick and said to be the seat of an Oghuz chief.

Georg von Meyendorff (translated by William Monteith, 1840)

Daily lifeSyr Darya1820

Songs to the moon by the Syr Darya

The Kirghiz often pass half the night seated on a stone, gazing at the moon, and singing melancholy impromptu words to airs not less so. They also have historical songs, which celebrate the great actions of their heroes, but these are chaunted only by professional singers, and I greatly regret not having heard them.

Editorial context

Wintering among the reeds of the Syr Darya was the Kazakhs' great wish. Meyendorff describes men sitting half the night on a stone, gazing at the moon and singing sad improvised songs, and regrets never hearing the heroic songs that only professional bards performed.

Georg von Meyendorff (translated by William Monteith, 1840)

CitiesOld bed of the Jana Darya, Kyzylorda region, KazakhstanDecember 1820

Every unknown ruin 'comes from the Tatars'

These ruins, which were probably once a fortress, are called Kutschuk by the Kirghiz and are supposed, as they claim, to come from the Tatars; but their statement is not to be relied on at all: they make everything that is unknown to them in their steppe come from the Tatars, because these lived there immediately before them.

Working translation

Original wording

Diese Ruinen, die wahrscheinlich früher eine Festung gewesen sind, werden von den Kirgisen Kutschuk genannt, und sollen, wie sie behaupten, von den Tataren herstammen; aber auf ihre Aussage ist durchaus nicht zu bauen, sie lassen alles, was ihnen in ihrer Steppe unbekannt ist, von den Tataren herstammen, weil diese zunächst vor ihnen dort gehaust haben.

Editorial context

Riding through saxaul woods, the caravan passed ruins of 'an earlier town or fortress'. Eversmann saw walls of sun-dried bricks four to five inches thick mixed with some fired ones, two window openings with pointed arches in the largest building, three rings of walls now slumped into banks, and many unglazed potsherds. The Kazakhs called the place Kutschuk and said the Tatars built it, but he warns that they credit everything unknown in their steppe to the Tatars simply because the Tatars lived there just before them.

Eduard Eversmann

CitiesSyr Darya1246

Ruined cities on the Syr Darya

We found in that country innumerable ruined cities, overthrown villages, and many deserted towns. There is a great river in that country whose name I do not know, and on which stands a city called Ianckint, and also another called Barchin, and still another called Ornas

Editorial context

Riding east, Carpini crossed the old Khwarazmian lands the Mongols had conquered in 1219 to 1221 and found them full of ruins. He names Ianckint, Barchin and Ornas. Rockhill's footnote on the same page quotes Carpini's longer account of how the Mongols took Ornas by diverting the river and flooding the city, and on the page before Carpini describes skulls and bones lying on the steppe 'like cattle-dung'.

John of Pian de Carpine (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)
See the original page (p. 14)

BuildingsLower Syr Darya, east of the Aral Sea, KazakhstanNovember 1820

Domed steppe tombs built by hired Bukharans

They are mostly built of unfired bricks, sometimes also of rubble stone. All these tombs are very new and were built by Bukharans, whose labour the Kirghiz pay either in sheep or in grain … The whole thing is, moreover, a most wretched piece of work, and I mention it only because in Europe people imagine such things quite differently from what they really are.

Working translation

Original wording

Sie sind grösstentheils von ungebrannten Ziegeln aufgebaut, mitunter auch von Bruchsteinen. Alle diese Grabmähler sind sehr neu und von Bucharen aufgebaut, denen ihre Mühe von den Kirgisen entweder mit Schaafen oder mit Getraide bezahlt wird … Das Ganze ist übrigens ein höchst erbärmliches Machwerk, und ich führe es nur bloss deshalb an, weil man sich in Europa dergleichen Dinge ganz anders vorstellt, als sie wirklich sind.

Editorial context

Eversmann describes the typical larger Kazakh tomb of the southern steppe: a round building with a domed cupola topped by a small pillar, a projecting entrance with a pointed arch and four attached corner pillars. They were mostly of unfired brick, sometimes rubble, all quite new, and built by Bukharans whom the Kazakhs paid in sheep or grain. He mentions them only because Europeans imagined such things very differently; poorer graves where there is no stone were gabled vaults of clay. A footnote says Pallas drew the 'Tatar tombs' of southern Russia in just the same way.

Eduard Eversmann
See the original page (p. 45)

About Kazakhs (Kirghiz) as patrons; Bukharans as builders

Keep in mind

The dismissive judgement of the workmanship is Eversmann's taste; the facts about materials and builders are his observation and inquiry.

CitiesOtrar1218

Otrar: where the war began

One time the chief of this place ordered several envoys (of Chinghiz), and several hundreds of merchants (who were with them), to be put to death, and seized upon their goods. That was the cause of the army being directed against the Western people.

Editorial context

Yelü Chucai names Otrar ('O-ta-la'), ruling more than ten other towns, as the cause of the Mongol invasion of Central Asia. Its governor had Chinggis Khan's envoys and several hundred merchants with them killed and seized their goods. Bretschneider notes that Persian historians tell the same story.

Yelü Chucai's Si yu lu, in a Yuan-era abridgement (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Strange talesOtrar, Kazakhstan (Syr Darya)winter 1404 to 1405

Breath that froze on the beard

When a man breathed out, his breath froze on his moustache and beard … and if he spat phlegm from his mouth, for all its warmth it did not reach the ground except as a frozen pellet.

Working translation

Original wording

وكان الرجل اذا تنفس جمدت انفاسه على سباله ولحيته … وان لفظ من فيه نخامة لا تصل الى الارض مع ما فيها من الحرارة الا وهي بندقة جامدة

Editorial context

On Timur's last march toward China, Ibn Arabshah describes a winter so cold that a man's breath froze on his moustache and beard, and spittle turned to a frozen pellet before it hit the ground. It is a set piece of rhymed prose about the winter in which Timur died at Otrar.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

About Timur's army (Chaghatay Turks and Tatars)
