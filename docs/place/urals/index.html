Daily lifeKazakh steppe, about 800 versts east of the Ural (Yaik) riverJuly 1740

Kumiss in the tent of a Kazakh chief

This Tartar-chief was sitting on a carpet in his tent; he rose, and bid us welcome, and making us sit down with him, he took a large dish of kumees, which he drank, and made us do the same. We gave him some small presents, which he immediately divided amongst the company.

Editorial context

Travelling in Tartar dress with Kazakh guides, the factors reached the horde of the chief Jean Beek (Janibek Batyr). He sat on a carpet in his tent, rose to welcome them, drank a large dish of kumiss and made them drink too. The next page describes Kazakh felt tents, horse meat, drunkenness on fermented mare's milk, the dried cheese called 'crute', and their rule of never harming a guest while he is under protection.

British factors Reynold Hogg and George Thompson, their 1740 journal as printed by Jonas Hanway (1753)

BuildingsUy river, southern Urals (Chelyabinsk and Kostanay region)mid-18th century (reported)

Stone ruins that an old Tatar called tombs of a people who left for China

in various places there are ruins of stone buildings, some of which are still whole. About these, one very old man among the Tatars living in Iset province told Lieutenant-Colonel Rennenkampf, from the tradition of his ancestors, that the peoples who lived in these places in ancient times went away into the Chinese state. The buildings still visible are nothing other than the cemeteries of their notable people

Working translation

Original wording

въ разныхъ мѣстахъ находятся развалины каменныхъ строеній, изъ коихъ нѣкоторыя понынѣ еще въ цѣлости. О сихъ изъ живущихъ въ Исетской провинціи Татаръ одинъ престарѣлой человѣкъ Подполковнику Ренненкампфу по преданію предковъ своихъ объявлялъ, яко бы въ древнія времена жившіе въ сихъ мѣстахъ народы отошли въ Китайское государство. Оныя донынѣ видимыя строенія не что иное, какъ знатныхъ ихъ людей кладбища

Editorial context

Along the Uy frontier line Rychkov notes ruins of stone buildings, some still whole. An old Tatar of Iset province told Lieutenant-Colonel Rennenkampf, from his ancestors' tradition, that the peoples who once lived here had moved away to China, and that the standing buildings were nothing but cemeteries of their notable men. An early record of local explanations for steppe ruins.

Pyotr Rychkov, reporting an old Tatar's account to Rennenkampf (1762)

Daily lifeWestern Urals (between Yekaterinburg and Kazan)September 1761

Welcomed at the Tatar village of Birna

At last I reached Birna; this village is inhabited by Tartars. Several had come out a verst from their village to meet me … They had prepared a kind of dinner for me consisting of honey, butter and a few vegetables. Their houses are as clean as those of the Siberians are not. For the rest, they live in much the same way, except that they are Mahometans.

Working translation

Original wording

J'arrivai enfin à Birna; ce Village est habité par des Tartares. Plusieurs étoient venus au-devant de moi à un werst de leur Village … Ils m'avoient fait préparer une espece de dîner qui consistoit dans du miel, du beurre & quelques légumes. Leurs maisons sont aussi propres que celles des Sibériens le sont peu. Au reste, ils vivent à-peu-près de la même façon, excepté qu'ils sont Mahométans.

Editorial context

After a near-fatal sledge accident, Chappe reached Birna, a Tatar village. Villagers came out a verst to meet him with signs of friendship, led him to their headman (chosen, he says, for his merit without any election) and gave him a meal of honey, butter and vegetables. Their houses were as clean as Siberian ones were dirty; they lived much like their neighbours except that they were Muslims.

Jean Chappe d'Auteroche

Daily lifeWestern Urals (Birna)September 1761

Tatar women's sugar-loaf caps, and equal marriages

The dress of Tartar women differs little from that of the men: it is shorter, and the belt is worn over the robe. Their headdress is a cap, often shaped like a sugar loaf, covered with kopecks and glass beads … They share most of their husbands' work; the husbands treat them gently; a perfect equality reigns between them.

Working translation

Original wording

L'habillement des femmes Tartares differe peu de celui des hommes : il est plus court, & la ceinture est par-dessus la robe. Leur coeffure est un bonnet, souvent fait en pain de sucre; il est couvert de copecks & de grains de verre … Elles partagent la plupart des travaux de leurs maris : ceux-ci les traitent avec douceur : une parfaite égalité regne entre eux.

Editorial context

Chappe describes the dress of the Tatars of Birna: the men in wool tunics, long robes and boots with shaved heads, the women in shorter robes and tall sugar-loaf caps sewn with copper coins and glass beads. Wives shared most of their husbands' work and were treated gently, with 'perfect equality' between them, which he contrasts with the seclusion of Siberian Russian wives.

Jean Chappe d'Auteroche

Strange talesMiddle Urals (Chusovaya valley)1692

A hunting dog buried like a man, with its own little house

finally they laid him in the earth like a human being, put a piece of wood made for the purpose under his head so that he might lie softly, and afterwards built a separate little wooden house over his grave, six to seven feet high, standing on four posts

Working translation

Original wording

endlich bestätigten sie ihn gleich einen Menschen zur Erde, legten ihm ein darzu verfertigtes Holtz unter das Haupt, damit er sanft liegen möchte, und baueten nachmahls ein apartes Häuslein von Holtz, auff sein Grab, 6. biß 7. Fuß hoch, stehend auf vier Pfälen

Editorial context

Brand watched the Voguls mourn a large hunting dog that had died suddenly. They lamented its virtues, buried it like a person with a pillow of wood under its head, and built a small wooden house six or seven feet high on four posts over its grave. He adds that this is said to be an age-old custom among them.

Adam Brand (Neu-vermehrte Beschreibung, Lübeck 1734)

Daily lifeMiddle Urals (Chusovaya valley)1692

'We see so many poor Russians who believe in Christ'

as for well-being in this life, we see daily before our eyes so many poor Russians who, though they believe in Christ, can scarcely spare a piece of bread; as for eternal welfare, they must go on living and dying as their forefathers did, whether they had believed well or badly.

Working translation

Original wording

was das zeitliche Wohlseyn anlangt, sehen wir täglich vor unsern Augen so viel arme Russen, welche, ob sie wohl an Christum glauben, dennoch kaum ein Stuck Brodt erübrigen können, was aber die ewige Wohlfahrt anlangt, müsten sie fernerhin also leben und sterben, wie ihre Vor-Eltern, sie möchten gut oder übel geglaubt haben.

Editorial context

When the travellers urged the Voguls to give up their 'errors' and accept Christ, they gave a sharp reply: they saw every day many poor Russians who believed in Christ and could barely earn a crust of bread, and as for salvation, they would live and die as their ancestors had. Brand adds that they neither farm nor trade and live by hunting sable and elk.

Adam Brand (Neu-vermehrte Beschreibung, Lübeck 1734)

'Tartar houses' with a window of ice

who are the first Siberian Tartars, called Wogultski, and a heathen but very superstitious people … and they also live in such Tartar houses, which are however made with a fireplace; the smoke hole at the top of the roof is covered, as soon as the wood has burned to coals, with a cover of ice, through which the daylight falls in

Working translation

Original wording

welche die ersten Siberischen Tartarn so Wogultski genennet werden, und ein Heydnisches, aber dabey sehr superstitiöses Volck … wie sie denn auch in solchen tartarischen Häusern wohnen, die doch mit einem Camin gemacht, das Rauch-Loch, welches oben im Dach ist, wird so bald das Holtz zu Kohlen gebrennet ist, mit einer Decke von Eiß zu gedecket, wodurch das Tages-Licht hinein fället

Editorial context

Travelling up the Chusovaya toward the Urals, the embassy stayed in villages of the Voguls, whom Brand calls the first Siberian Tartars. He says they live in Tartar houses with a fireplace, and once the wood has burned down to coals the smoke hole in the roof is closed with a sheet of ice that lets in the daylight. They sit and sleep cross-legged on a broad bench running round the room.

Adam Brand (Neu-vermehrte Beschreibung, Lübeck 1734)

Vogul log rooms with an ice skylight, retold

Their dwellings are square wooden rooms such as the Russian peasants have. Instead of a stove they have a fireplace or hearth, on which they burn wood and cook their food. The smoke hole at the top of the roof, as soon as the [wood] has burned to coals, [is covered] with a lid of ice

Working translation

Original wording

Ihre Wohnungen sind viereckigte hölzerne Kammern, wie die Russischen Bauren haben. An statt des Ofens haben sie Camin oder Feuer-Herd, worauf sie Holz brennen und darbei ihre Speise kochen. Das Rauch-Loch, so oben im Tach ist, wird, so bald das … zu Kohlen gebrennet ist, mit einem Deckel von Eiß

Editorial context

The chapter on the 'Siberian Tartars' called Voguls repeats, almost word for word, Adam Brand's description of their houses, with one change: the compiler says they are square wooden rooms like those of Russian peasants rather than 'Tartar houses'. The smoke hole is still sealed with a lid of ice that lets in daylight.

Anonymous compiler (Der allerneueste Staat von Siberien, Nuremberg 1720)
