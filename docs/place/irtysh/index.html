Strange talesCamp of the Kalmyk taisha Ablai1650 to 1651

Yermak's armour and the Tatars' vow of silence

In 1651 the Tobolsk officer Ulian Remezov took Yermak's famous mail coat, reclaimed from Tatar princes, as a gift to the Kalmyk ruler Ablai, who kissed it and raised it to his head. Ablai then told his own version of Yermak's death: drowned at the ford, found and shot at, his blood flowing, his armour divided up, miracles from his armour and clothes, and a Tatar oath 'on pain of death' not to tell the Russians about him. Ablai said that as a sick child he was given earth from Yermak's grave to drink and was cured.

And Ablai began to tell stories about him, according to his own history: how he came to Siberia, and fled from Kuchum at the ford, and drowned, and was found, and was shot at, and blood flowed, and they divided up his armour and carried it off, and how there were miracles from the armour and the clothing, and how the Tatars made a vow on pain of death not to tell the Russians about him

Original wording

И нача Аблай повѣсти дѣяти о немъ по своей исторіи, какъ пріѣхалъ въ Сибирь, и отъ Кучюма на перекопѣ побѣжа, и утопе, и обрѣтенъ, и стрѣлянъ, и кровь течаше, и пансыри раздѣлиша и развезоша, и какъ отъ пансырей и отъ платья чюдесъ было, и какъ Татара смертной завѣтъ положиша, что про него Русакамъ невѣщати

BuildingsAblai-kit ruins, near Ust-Kamenogorsk (Oskemen), Kazakhstanbuilt 1650s; leaves sent to Peter I in 1720; described 1832

Ablai-kit: a walled Buddhist monastery whose books reached Peter the Great

The chief ruins of the steppe, Levshin says, are those of Ablai-kit on the left bank of the Irtysh, a stone-walled enclosure about a verst long. Inside stood two buildings, one a Buddhist temple where statues, paintings of burkhans (Buddhas) and many manuscripts were still to be seen early in the 18th century. Leaves of those manuscripts were sent to Peter the Great in 1720 and on to the Paris Academy. This is the same town the envoy Baikov saw being built for Ablai taisha by craftsmen from China in 1655 (W171).

Inside the enclosure there were two buildings; one served as a Lamaist temple, and at the beginning of the last century statues, painted images of burkhans and many writings could still be seen in it, some leaves of which were delivered in 1720 to the Emperor Peter the Great and sent by him to the Paris Academy of Inscriptions and Fine Arts.

Working translation

Original wording

Внутри ограды были два зданія; одно служило Ламайскимъ капищемъ, и въ началѣ прошедшаго столѣтія еще видны въ немъ были статуи, живописныя изображенія бурхановъ, и многія письмена, изъ коихъ нѣкоторые листы въ 1720 году были доставлены Императору Петру Великому, а имъ посланы въ Парижскую Академію надписей и изящныхъ искусствъ.

BuildingsUpper Irtysh, eastern Kazakhstan1650s to 1670s (reported 1675)

Brick halls in Kalmyk country, built by craftsmen from China

Following the upper Irtysh through Kalmyk country, Spafarii notes its few buildings. The Kalmyk prince Ablai had built two halls of fired brick in a strong place among stone mountains, and the craftsmen had been sent to him from China, because Mongol and Kalmyk princes exchanged envoys and gifts with the Chinese emperor, 'since now a Kalmyk and Tatar race rules in China'. On the same page he mentions the Kontaisha's clay town with two brick halls where Kalmyk lamas lived.

And on that stream Ablai taisha built 2 halls of fired brick in a strong place among stone mountains, and the craftsmen were sent to him from the Chinese state, because the Mongol and Kalmyk taishas often exchange envoys with the Chinese khan and take and give presents, since now a Kalmyk and Tatar race rules in China.

Original wording

BuildingsUpper Irtysh between Yamyshevo and Ust-Kamenogorsk, KazakhstanAugust 1734

Grave-robbers melt the gold of the old steppe graves

All along the Irtysh Gmelin saw very many graves, which he took for remains of former Kalmyk or Bukharan settlement, and nearly every one had been dug open. Local people treated them as a mine for 'grave silver': horse ornaments, big seals, bracelets, sometimes idols. The unopened ones near Ust-Kamenogorsk were ringed with upright field stones or slates and filled with stones and earth.

But those who open the graves in this country have the habit, to the uncommon loss of history, of generally melting down the gold and silver and throwing away the copper and iron.

Allein diejenigen, die hier zu Lande die Gräber eröffnen, haben die Gewohnheit, daß sie zu ungemeinem Verlust der Historie das Gold und Silber gemeiniglich schmelzen, das Kupfer und Eisen aber wegwerfen.

Saw itJohann Georg Gmelin (German original, Göttingen, 1751)

Daily lifeLake Yamysh, Irtysh (near Pavlodar)1670s (annual)

The salt-lake fair where Kalmyks, Bukharans and Tatars sold horses and captives

At the Yamysh salt lakes, where the 'Siberian kingdom' ended, 30 or 40 river boats came every year from Tobolsk, Tomsk and other towns to load natural salt. While the Russians gathered it, a fair sprang up: many thousands of Kalmyks, Bukharans and Tatars came to sell horses, captives and Chinese goods. After two or three weeks everyone left and the place stood empty again, and Spafarii suggests a fort should be built there.

And at the time when the Russian people gather salt from the lake, a fair takes place. And many thousands of people come, Kalmyks and Bukharans and Tatars, and trade with the Russian people. And they sell horses, and captives, and other Chinese goods. And they hold that fair for 2 or 3 weeks

Original wording

Strange talesTobolsk and the Irtyshrecorded 1670 and in the 1730s

The Tatar tale of Chinese who fled when the forest grew

Müller reports a story still told orally by the Tobolsk Tatars: Siberia was once a bare steppe where the Chinese lived, but when forest began to grow everywhere the people took fright, 'as the land became as if horned', and moved away to their present home. He says it was also written down in 1670 on the orders of the Tobolsk governor Pyotr Godunov. Müller calls it a false tale that no reasonable person can believe, and notes that forest could easily grow over abandoned sites in 450 years without any miracle.

They say that at first all Siberia was a bare steppe without forest, and when at that time the Chinese lived there, forest little by little began to grow in various places, at which the people took fright, because the land became as if horned, and moved to their present dwelling. This tale can still be heard by word of mouth from the Tobolsk Tatars.

Working translation

Original wording

Сказываютъ, якобы сперва вся Сибирь была голая степь безъ лѣсу, а какъ въ оное время Китайцы тамъ жительство свое имѣли, то помалу по разнымъ мѣстамъ началъ лѣсъ расти, чего народъ испужавшись, понеже земля подобно какъ рогатая стала, на нынѣшнее жилище переселился. Сію повѣсть еще и нынѣ у Тобольскихъ Татаръ изустно слышать можно.

BuildingsSouthern Siberia (Yenisei, Irtysh and Tobol steppes)1711 to 1721

The 'pyramids' of Tartar kings on old maps

Strahlenberg describes the burial mounds of southern Siberia: some are earth heaps as high as houses, lying so thick that they look like a ridge of hills; others are ringed with rough or squared stones, and some are built entirely of stone. He explains that the 'pyramids' drawn on old European maps of Great Tartary, labelled 'Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings', must mean these tombs, which are not really pyramids.

Some are only raised up of Earth, as high as Houses, … that, at a Distance, they appear like a Ridge of Hills. … In some Places these Tombs are entirely built of Stone. Hence we find in the ancient Maps of Tartary the Greater, a Number of Pyramids, with these Words, in Latin; viz. The Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings,

Saw itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Daily lifeIshim river mouth, Irtysh1580s (song reported c. 1700)

A Tatar lament for five slain Cossacks

After a hand-to-hand fight at the mouth of the Ishim in which five of Yermak's Cossacks died, the chronicle says the Tatars still sang about it at their gatherings, weeping, with a Tatar refrain that it translates. The song was known as 'the Tsaritsa's lament'. It is a rare glimpse of Tatar oral memory of the war.

and about those five men the Tatars sing with weeping at their gatherings, with the refrain: 'yanym, yanym, bit kazak, bit kazak', that is: 'warriors, warriors, five, five men we defeated and destroyed.' And this song is called the Tsaritsa's Lament.

Original wording

и о тѣхъ пяти человѣкахъ Татара поютъ съ плачемъ при бесѣдахъ въ пѣсняхъ припѣваючи: янымъ, янымъ, битъ казакъ, битъ казакъ, сирѣчь: воины, воины, пять пять человѣкъ побѣдиша и разориша. И сія пѣсня и словетъ царицынъ плачь.

BuildingsUst-Kamenogorskmid-17th century; ruins seen by Russians in the 18th century

Ablai-kit: a Kalmyk monastery with a printing press

Near the upper Irtysh stand the ruins of Ablai-kit, a Buddhist monastery that the Kalmyk prince Ablai founded in the mid-17th century, with a Mongol-Tibetan library and a woodblock press. Manuscripts from it were sent to Paris under Peter the Great. Humboldt notes that half-torn leaves of Tibetan books could still be found scattered in the steppe grass around the ruins.

which a Kalmyk prince, Ablai, had founded in the middle of the 17th century and endowed with a Mongol-Tibetan library and a woodblock printing press. … Even today one finds half-torn leaves of Tibetan books scattered in the steppe grass around the ruins of Ablai-kit.

Strange talesIrtysh steppe between Semey (Semipalatinsk) and YamyshevoNovember 1723

A Russian envoy guesses that ancient Russians lay under the mounds

Continuing the argument about the robbed mounds, Unkovsky told Darzha that in pagan times, over seven hundred years earlier, Russians had buried their dead with war-gear under great earth mounds, so perhaps some of 'our peoples' had once lived here. He blamed the digging on Kalmyks, Ostyaks or Kazakhs, and said Russian law punished grave-robbers with death. Darzha, doubtful, replied that his people had only had writing for a hundred years and could not know. A striking early example of a claim about who once lived on the steppe, made on the spot and without evidence.

Among us people are buried with their wealth, and especially in ancient times, before the Christian law, more than seven hundred years ago, they were buried with all their war-gear and great mounds of earth were heaped over them; and it may be that in ancient times some of our peoples lived in these places … [Darzha] said that among them writing began only a hundred years ago.

Working translation

Original wording

У насъ погребаются люди съ богатствомъ, а паче что въ древнія времена еще до христіанского закона, лѣтъ слишкомъ за семь сотъ погребалися со всѣмъ военнымъ уборствомъ, и насыпали на нихъ великіе бугры земли, и можетъ быть, что въ древнія времена нѣкоторые изъ нашихъ народовъ въ сихъ мѣстахъ жили … сказалъ, что у насъ де только сто лѣтъ какъ грамота началась

Strange talesWestern Siberia (Irtysh)1584 onward

Miracles at the enemy's grave

After Yermak drowned in the Irtysh, his body was first abused on Kuchum's orders, but then, Coxe says, the Tatars came to revere him: they buried him with pagan rites, and stories spread that touching his body cured all illness and that a flame hovered over his tomb. A footnote adds that a Kalmyk prince, Allai, was said to have been cured by drinking earth from the grave.

Many miraculous stories were soon spread abroad, and met with implicit belief. The touch of his body was supposed to have been an instantaneous cure for all disorders; and even his clothes and arms were said to be endowed with the same efficacy. A flame of fire was represented as sometimes hovering about his tomb

Legend or rumourWilliam Coxe (compiling Müller's Siberian history)

About Siberian Tatars; later a Kalmyk prince

Keep in mind

Legend preserved in Russian Siberian chronicles; Coxe calls these 'idle fables'.

Strange talesDemyanka River (Irtysh)1582 or 1583 (the chronicle's year 7090)

A golden 'Russian god' in a cup

Yermak's men besieged Prince Demyan's big hill-town on the lower Irtysh, held by 2000 Tatars, Voguls and Ostyaks. A Chuvash who had been Kuchum's captive told them the defenders prayed to 'a Russian god, cast in gold, sitting in a cup'; they poured water in the cup and drank it, called it Christ, and said it had come from Vladimir's baptism of Rus. Sent to steal it, he reported that it stood on a table surrounded by burning fat and sulphur fumes. The Cossacks never found it.

One of them, a Chuvash who had been with Kuchum, a Russian captive, said: they pray, he said, to a Russian god, and that Russian god is cast of gold, sits in a cup, and they pour water into that cup and drink it, and call him Christ, and they say he was brought from Vladimir's baptism

BuildingsAblaikit ruins, near Ust-Kamenogorsk, East Kazakhstan1826 (temple built 1654)

A Zunghar brick temple quarried for Kazakh tombs

Meyer reached the ruins of the 'Ablakit palaces' about 70 versts from Ust-Kamenogorsk. He credits the building to the Zunghar prince Ablai in 1654 (following Pallas) and reports that the local Kazakhs had carried off its large fired bricks to build tombs for their sultans, leaving only foundations and the outer wall. On the same page he notes that the builders carried the enclosing wall up to the highest crags with piled granite blocks.

A description of this soongorian [Zunghar] temple, now completely destroyed, which was built in 1654 by the soongorian prince Ablai, can be found in Pallas's Travels … The Kirghiz have broken away the fine, large, solid bricks to build tombs for their dead sultans and other rich people, so that now only the foundations and the wall enclosing the whole site still stand.

Daily lifeUpper Irtysh1720s

Printed scrolls from Kalmyk temples

Strahlenberg corrects another author: the idols and manuscripts sent to Europe did not come from Samarkand or the Caspian but from pagan temples and tombs in the Kalmyk lands along the upper Irtysh. The writings are in Tangut (Tibetan), Mongol and Kalmyk script, printed rather than written, in gold or silver letters on varnished cotton or silk paper. In a footnote he says he saw a wooden printing block for Kalmyk letters in the governor's house at Tobolsk in 1723.

…were brought from the Desarts of the Kalmucks, on each Side of the River Irtisch, upwards, out of the Pagan Temples and Tombs; And as to the Manuscripts, … they consist of Tanguthian, Mungalian and Kalmuckian Characters, not upon Parchment, but upon a thick Paper, made of Cotton or Silk, done over with black and blew Varnish; Upon which the Characters or Letters are not wrote, but printed,

Saw itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Daily lifeIrtysh River (Aremzian volost)1582 or 1583 (the chronicle's year 7090)

Tribute sworn on a bloody sabre

Yermak sent the fifty-leader Bogdan Briazga down the Irtysh to bring the Tatar and Ostyak districts under the tsar and collect yasak (fur tribute). At the first district he stormed a strong little town, hanged the best 'mergens' (warriors) by the leg and shot them, collected tribute 'by the sabre', laid the bloody sabre on the table and made the people kiss it as an oath to pay every year. The terror, says the chronicle, silenced the whole district.

And coming to the first, the Aremzian district, he took the strong little town by battle, and hanged many of the best mergens by the leg and shot them, and collected the yasak by the sabre, and laid it, bloody, on the table, and ordered them to kiss it in faith to the sovereign tsar, that they would serve and pay yasak every year

Log huts with windows made of ice

Travelling up the Irtysh in midwinter, Bell's party passes many Tatar villages and sleeps in their huts. He describes one- or two-room log houses chinked with moss, a broad bench for sitting and sleeping, and, in place of glass, a pane of ice cut to fit the window. The next page adds that these Tatars are very clean and use open hearths rather than Russian stoves.

These houses consist generally of one or two rooms, according to the ability of the landlord. … The walls are built of wood and moss, consisting of large beams, laid one above another, with a layer of moss between every two beams. All the roofs are raised. A square-hole is cut out for a window, and to supply the want of glass, a piece of ice

BuildingsBaraba steppe, between the Irtysh and the Ob1690s

Summer huts, winter log cabins, and fields of barley

The Ides section describes the Baraba people between Tara and the Ob. They live in villages, in light huts in summer and good wooden cabins in winter, 'like the Tartars of Siberia', and they plough and sow oats, barley and buckwheat. The same page says they drink a spirit distilled from mare's milk called kumis and carry a wooden idol, their 'Saitan', on the hunt.

They are a cunning and warlike people, who live in wooden cabins like the Tartars of Siberia … They live in kinds of villages, in light huts in summer and in good wooden cabins in winter. Ploughing is practised among them, and they sow oats, barley, buckwheat and so on.

Strange talesIrtysh River near the Vagai1585

Yermak's body that bled for six weeks

Following the Remezov chronicle, whose author wanted Yermak made a saint, Müller tells how Tatars found the drowned Cossack leader by his armour. When a murza tried to strip it, blood ran from the corpse's mouth and nose as from a living man, so the Tatars laid the body naked on a scaffold and everyone who passed shot an arrow into it, and each time fresh blood flowed. Birds would not touch it, and after six weeks troubling dreams led the Tatars to bury him.

At once much blood ran from the dead man's mouth and nose, just as from a living person … For this purpose they laid the body quite naked on a scaffold, and everyone who came up or passed by shot an arrow at it, and each time fresh blood ran from the wound.
