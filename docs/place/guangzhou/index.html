CitiesGuangzhou (Canton)24 November 1650

The fall and sack of Canton, 1650

Nieuhof tells how the Tartar army took Canton after a siege that, he says, lasted a whole year, helped by a bribed Chinese governor who opened a gate. Two Dutch gunners married to Chinese women had helped the defenders. The sack that followed lasted weeks. He arrived five years later, so this is what the townspeople told him.

It was upon the 24. of November, in the year 1650. when the Tartars upon this advantage rushed with their whole Army into the City … The whole Tartar Army being got into the City, the place was soon turned to a Map of misery … there was heard no other cry in the Streets, but Strike, Kill, and Destroy the Rebellious Barbarians

Who Tartars (Qing Manchu army)

Heard itJohan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

The nameGuangzhou1556

Tartar prisoners in Canton, called 'Tatos'

Here Cruz speaks as an eyewitness: in Canton he saw many Tartar captives whose only 'captivity' was serving as soldiers far from home. They wore red caps as a badge but otherwise dressed like the Chinese, and received a fixed royal pay. The Chinese, he says, call them 'Tatos' because they cannot pronounce the letter R. Chinese Dada (Tatar) is real; the missing-R explanation is his.

In the city of Canton I saw many Tartar captives, whose only captivity is to serve as men-at-arms in other parts far from Tartary. They wear red caps as a badge; otherwise they wear the dress of the Chinese among whom they live … The Chinese call these people Tatos, because they cannot pronounce this letter r.

Strange talesGuangzhou1556

Painted giants who guard the passes of Tartary

Describing officials' houses in Canton, Cruz notes huge gates painted with two giants holding staves. He saw four such figures in a temple, said to be portraits from life of men the king keeps to guard the weakest passes of Tartary, big-limbed and twelve or thirteen palms tall. What he saw were painted door gods; the frontier story is what he was told.

on the front they have some very large doors, like the gates of a city, with two giants painted on them holding staves in their hands. I saw four in a pagoda (which is a temple of idols), portrayed from life of some whom they say the King keeps to guard for him the passes [that are weakest in Tartary; they are very big-limbed, perhaps twelve or thirteen palms tall].

Daily lifeRoad from Beijing to Canton1793

Under the bamboo: the Chinese cry, the Tartar is silent

Travelling south, Barrow saw beatings with the bamboo almost daily. He contrasts the two peoples: a Chinese cries out and thanks the official on his knees, while a Tartar bears it silently and then grumbles, disputing a Chinese official's right to flog him.

In travelling through the country, a day seldom escaped without our witnessing the application of the Pan-tsé, or bamboo… A Chinese suffering under this punishment cries out in the most piteous manner; a Tartar bears it in silence.

Who Manchus and Chinese

Saw itJohn Barrow

Keep in mind. A generalisation from what he happened to see.

Daily lifeGuangdong, on the river north of CantonMarch 1656

A Tartar archer hits a palm-sized mark three times

While the embassy camped ashore, the Tartar escort put on a display of arms. One bowman shot three arrows in a row through a target no bigger than a hand at thirty-five paces and was paid a small coin. A small eyewitness scene that matches the Manchus' fame as archers.

The Tartars in the mean time, to show some pastime, exercised their Arms before the Tent of the Embassadours, which was performed by them with much dexterity. Amongst the rest, there was one with a Bow and Arrow, who was so rare a Marks-man, that he shot thrice together through the white, no broader then the Palm of a Hand, at the distance of thirty five Paces

Who Tartars (Manchu soldiers escorting the embassy)

Saw itJohan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)
