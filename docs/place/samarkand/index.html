BuildingsSamarkandc.1399 to 1404

A marble mosque for Samarkand, and a builder dragged to death

Timur had seen a mosque of white marble in India and wanted one like it in Samarkand. Ibn Arabshah says he ordered solid marble to be quarried and put a man named Muhammad Jald in charge, who raised four minarets and outdid the master builders. When Timur came back from campaign and looked at the mosque, he had Muhammad Jald thrown on his face, his feet tied, and dragged until he was torn to pieces.

… and that blocks of solid marble be cut for it; and he entrusted the work to a man called Muhammad Jald … When he returned from his journey and inspected what had been done in his absence, he went to the mosque to look at it; the moment his eye fell on it, he gave orders about Muhammad Jald, and they threw him on his face, tied his feet, and kept dragging him on his face until they had cut him to pieces.

BuildingsSamarkand1404

A tent palace on thirty-six poles and five hundred cords

Outside Samarkand Timur held his feasts in a vast camp. Clavijo measured the great pavilion by eye: square, three lances high, a hundred paces across, raised on twelve painted poles each as thick as a man's chest, with cart-wheel winches to lift them, porticoes on twenty-four more poles, and silk hangings inside. It shows that the tent tradition of the steppe survived as palace architecture beside Timur's brick buildings.

there was a great and lofty pavilion, which was like a tent, only square, and three lances high. It was a hundred paces broad, and had four corners, and the ceiling was round, like a vault. It was pitched against twelve poles, each as large round as a man, measured round the chest.

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

BuildingsSamarkandc.1500 (built 1420s)

Ulugh Beg's three-storey observatory

Among the works of Timur's grandson Ulugh Beg, Babur singles out the observatory on the hill of Kohik: three storeys high, fitted with instruments, where the astronomical tables still in use in his day were made. He places it among the seven or eight observatories ever built, alongside Hulagu's at Maragha.

Another remarkable edifice is the observatory, erected on the skirts of the hill of Kohik, which is provided with an astronomical apparatus, and is three stories in height. By means of this observatory, Ulugh Beg Mirza composed the Zich-Korkani (or Korkani Astronomical Tables), which are followed at the present time, scarcely any other being used.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

CitiesSamarkand1844

Samarkand, 'Ornament of the Face of the Earth'

Wolff describes Samarkand, Timur's old capital, as a clay-walled town of 40,000 people with a palace, marble houses, mosques, colleges and Timur's tomb. He adds stories that Chinese once lived there making paper from silk, and that the city took its name from a conqueror called Samar.

It has a wall of clay, and forty thousand inhabitants; a beautiful palace, and many houses of marble; many mosques and colleges. It was formerly inhabited by Chinese, who manufactured paper of silk; and it once had the name of Bokhara-Tsheen, but received the present name from the conqueror Samar, after Christ 643. Oologh Beyk erected there an observatory.

Joseph Wolff

BuildingsSamarkand1399

Foreign masons build Timur's great mosque at Samarkand

Back from India, Timur ordered a congregational mosque for Samarkand. Yazdi says the foundations were laid at an astrologically chosen moment and that the work was done by masons brought from foreign countries, 200 from Azerbaijan, Persia and India inside alone. On the next page he adds 500 men cutting stone in the mountains, 95 elephants hauling blocks, and a finished hall of 480 hewn-stone pillars, a carved marble roof, four minarets and brass doors. Earlier (p. 67) he records that Timur kept the captured masons of Delhi for this very building.

The masons, brought from foreign countrys, as mention'd before, gave the greatest proofs of their art and skill, as well in the solidity and beauty of the angles, as in the strength of the foundations of this noble edifice. In the inside of the mosque were employ'd two hundred masons from Azerbijana, Persia and India:

Sharaf al-Din Ali Yazdi (English of 1723, via Pétis de la Croix)

The nameSamarkandc. 1569

Samarkand, 'the head of Tartary' on the maps

Describing the Mogores (Mughals), who he says now rule Sind and have made many wars on Cambay, Cruz says the head of their kingdom is 'the great Samarkand', which the maps of his day label 'the head of Tartary'. He then uses Josephus to make these people Scythians descended from Japheth, son of Noah, through Magog. This is book learning, not observation: it shows 'Tartary' working as a map label in the 1560s.

The head of their kingdom is called the great Samarkand, which on the maps is called the head of Tartary. These are counted among the Scythians, as Josephus testifies in the first book of the Antiquities, who according to him descend from Japheth son of Noah through Magog.

CitiesSamarkand1404

A tent city with streets, butchers and bath-houses

When Timur called his army together for his festivals, some twenty thousand men pitched their tents in their assigned places without confusion. Clavijo describes the result as a moving town: streets of stalls selling cooked sheep, fruit, barley and bread, and even tents for hot baths with boilers. Compare Rubruck's description of Batu's ordu 150 years earlier (W043).

In this horde there are always butchers and cooks who sell cooked sheep, and others who sell fruit and barley, and bakers who sell bread. Every division of the horde is provided with all that the troops require, and they are arranged in streets. There are even baths and bath men in the horde

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

CitiesSamarkandabout 1812 to 1822

Samarkand: Timur's capital in ruins

Relying on the Indian traveller Mir Izzatullah and other reports, Fraser says Samarkand had been almost deserted until Shah Murad of Bukhara repopulated it. It had a mud wall and citadel but stone public buildings, and Timur's tomb still stood under a tall dome whose gold covering Shah Murad had removed.

This venerable city, often the seat of empire, once the favourite capital of the great Timoor, is now little better than a mass of ruins … In this place may yet be seen the Mausoleum of the Ameer Timoor ; it is a large building with a very lofty dome, which once was covered with gold ; but that was taken off by Shah Muraud.

James Baillie Fraser, drawing on Mir Izzatullah

CitiesSamarkandabout 1220 to 1222

Samarkand, 'a delicious place'

Yelü Chucai calls Samarkand 'Sün-sz'-kan', explains that the name means 'fat' because the land is so fertile, and describes gold and copper coins without holes. For miles around the city he saw orchards, flower gardens, aqueducts, springs and pools, melons as big as a horse's head, grape wine, and white cotton clothes, since white was lucky there and black the colour of mourning.

Around the city, to an extent of several tens of li, there are everywhere orchards, groves, flower gardens, aqueducts, running springs, square basins, and round ponds, in uninterrupted succession ; indeed, Sün-sz'-kan is a delicious place ! The water-melons there are as large as a horse's head.

Yelü Chucai's Si yu lu, in a Yuan-era abridgement (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

BuildingsDamascus to Samarkand1401

The craftsmen of Damascus marched off to Samarkand

When Timur took Damascus in 1401, Ibn Arabshah says he carried off its learned men and craftsmen, 'everyone skilled in any art': weavers, tailors, stone-cutters, carpenters, cap-makers, farriers, tent-makers, painters and decorators, bow-makers and falconers. They were shared out among the army commanders, who were ordered to deliver them to Samarkand. Ibn Arabshah, then a boy, was himself taken from Damascus to Samarkand.

He took from Damascus the men of learning and the craftsmen, everyone skilled and outstanding in any art: weavers and tailors, stone-cutters and carpenters, cap-makers and farriers, tent-makers, painters and bow-makers, and falconers; in short, the people of every craft whatever … He divided these groups among the heads of the army and ordered them to deliver them to Samarkand.

'Were the name Moghul written in gold'

After his defeat by the Uzbek Shaybani Khan, Babur complains bitterly that the Mongol troops sent to help him did not fight but turned on his own men, unhorsing and stripping them, and he says this was their usual practice. He sums up with a couplet that shows how sharply a Timurid Turk could separate himself from the Mongols, though he was one by descent.

If the Moghul race were a race of angels, it is a bad race ; And were the name Moghul written in gold, it would be odious.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

The nameSamarkand1404

The Zagatays: Tartars who stayed and gave their name

Clavijo explains Timur's people. An 'emperor of Tartary' divided his lands among four sons, and Zagatay (Chagatai) received Samarkand; when the locals killed him, his followers who stayed were called Zagatays. Timur descends from these 'Zagatay Tartars', and many Samarkand people now call themselves Zagatays to share in the famous name. Markham's note corrects the details of the story.

Timour Beg, and the other Zagatays who follow him, are descended from these Zagatay Tartars; and many people of the land of Samarcand have now taken this name of Zagatay, that they may share in the great name which the Zagatays have now attained.

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

Keep in mind

Local tradition as told to the envoys; Markham notes that Chagatai was not killed by his subjects.

CitiesSamarkandWinter 1221-1222

Samarkand after the conquest: a quarter of its people left

Samarkand, taken by the Mongols in 1220, was laid out along canals that carried river water down every street to every house. The diarist says it had held over 100,000 families before the Sultan of Khwarazm fell but only a quarter remained, that Muslim landowners could not dispose of their fields and had to manage them jointly with Khitan, Chinese and Tangut (Ho si) men, and that Chinese workmen lived everywhere. Changchun lodged in the Sultan's new palace on a mound about a hundred feet high, which the governor (probably Yelu Chucai, says Bretschneider) had left because robbers made that quarter unsafe.

the city of Samarkand had a population of more than a hundred thousand families, but after the occupation only the fourth part remained behind. Most of the fields and gardens belong to the Mohammedans, but they are not allowed to dispose of them. They are obliged to manage their properties in conjunction with K'i-tan (i.e., Karakhitai), Chinese, and men from Ho si. Chinese workmen are living everywhere.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Muslim townspeople under Mongol rule, with Khitan, Chinese and Tangut overseers

Keep in mind

The population figures are what the diarist was told. Bretschneider notes it cannot be proved that the city Chinggis took in 1220 stood on the site of modern Samarkand rather than the Afrasiab ruins to the north.

Timur's tomb, stripped of its gold

Izzet Ullah visited the Gur-i Amir, the tomb of Timur (Tamerlane), whose court history calls his army 'the Tartars'. He saw the great dome, the famous dark green stone slab and the inscribed plate, and was told the gilding had been stripped off by the Bukharan ruler Shah Murad. The poor attendants asked him whether any descendants of Timur still lived in India who might help them.

the seat of the sepulchre of Amir Timur ; a lofty building with an immense cupola : within it is a remarkable slab of a brilliant green stone, and above it is a plate with an inscription, with the name of Timur engraved on it. Formerly the dome was gilt, but Shah Murad Be stripped off the gilding, and took it away ;

Craftsmen carried off to build Samarkand

Clavijo explains how Timur filled Samarkand: he brought captives from every land he conquered, above all skilled workers. Silk weavers, bow-makers, glass and pottery makers came from Damascus; archers, masons and silversmiths from Turkey. So many arrived, some 150,000 of many nations, that many lived under trees and in caves outside the city. The page before says the villages around the city were peopled with captives too.

From Damascus he brought weavers of silk, and men who made bows, glass, and earthenware, so that, of those articles, Samarcand produces the best in the world. From Turkey he brought archers, masons, and silversmiths. He also brought men skilled in making engines of war

CitiesSamarkand1820 to 1821 (from informants)

Samarkand's marble and Timur's jasper tomb

Meyendorff was told that Samarkand's mosques and squares were finer than Bukhara's, built of white marble from nearby quarries, with fronts of glazed tiles, and that Timur's tomb of jasper still stood though Ulugh Beg's observatory had vanished. He adds that each new Khan of Bukhara was raised three times on a bluish stone called the Kok-tash.

The mosques and squares are handsomer than those at Bokhara, they are all built of white marble of which there are quarries at a trifling distance from this ancient residence of Timour. The fronts of the houses are covered with varnished tiles like those of Bokhara … The tomb of Timour, built of Jasper is still to be seen at Samercand, but the Observatory of Olong-Bey no longer exists

Georg von Meyendorff (translated by William Monteith, 1840)

CitiesSamarkand1813

Samarkand's colleges, once haunted by lions and wolves

Timur's old capital had shrunk. Izzet Ullah found it defended only by a mud wall and an earth citadel, Ulugh Beg's observatory reduced to a mound of rubbish, and the great 'Madressa Khanum' (the Bibi Khanum complex) in ruins. He says that before Shah Murad repopulated it, the city had been so run down that lions and wolves haunted the colleges.

Samarkand is defended by a mud wall, as well as a citadel of earth… There are many large colleges in the city ; the largest is the Madressa Khanum, but it is now in ruins… Before the reign of Shah Murad Be, Samarkand was in an entirely dilapidated condition, and the colleges were haunted by lions and wolves.

Mir Izzet Ullah (translated by H. H. Wilson)

CitiesSamarkandc. 1580 (looking back to c. 1400)

Samarkand, 'once the metropolis of all Tartary', now in ruins

Guagnini calls Samarkand a very large city that was once the capital of all Tartary but now lies disfigured by ruins, with many traces of antiquity, and the burial place of Tamerlane, 'that king of the Tartars' who, he repeats, carried the captured Ottoman sultan Bayezid round Asia in a cage bound with golden chains. For modern readers interested in 'lost Tartarian cities', this is a sixteenth-century writer naming a real capital and explaining its ruins by Timur's fallen empire.

Going eastward beyond Turkmenia, Samarkand is a very large city, which was once the metropolis of all Tartary but now lies disfigured by ruins, together with many traces of antiquity. In it is buried Tamerlane, that king of the Tartars, who once carried the Turkish emperor Bayezid … captive, bound in golden chains, in a cage like a bird through all Asia.

BuildingsSamarkandc.1497 to 1500 (describing buildings of c.1400)

Timur's stone mosque, built by stonecutters from India

Babur, who took Samarkand more than once, describes the buildings of his ancestor Timur: a four-storey palace, the Gok-serai, in the citadel, and a grand mosque of stone near the Iron Gate, whose Qur'an inscription could be read almost a kos away. He notes that the stonecutters were brought from Hindustan. Erskine's footnote adds that the historian Sharaf al-Din counted 200 masons from Azerbaijan, Fars and India.

There are many other magnificent edifices. One of these is the grand mosque, which is situated near the Iron-gate, within the walls of the city, and is built of stone. A number of stone-cutters were brought from Hindustan to work on it.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

CitiesSamarkand1832 (reported)

Samarkand, Timur's capital, shrunk to a provincial town

Burnes could not safely visit Samarkand, so he describes it from report: once Timur's capital, now a town of 8000 to 10,000 people, with gardens and fields where streets and mosques had been. Three colleges survived, including Ulugh Beg's with enamelled bricks, and Timur's tomb under a lofty dome decorated with agate. Its possession still made a Bukhara king legitimate.

The city has now declined from its grandeur to a provincial town of 8000, or at most 10,000, inhabitants, and gardens and fields occupy the place of its streets and mosques; but it is still regarded with high veneration by the people.

Alexander Burnes (1834)

Daily lifeSamarkand1404

The best drinker is called Bahadur

At Timur's feasts, cup-bearers made guests drain full cups in one pull, and refusing was an insult to the lord. The heaviest drinker earned the title bahadur, 'valiant'. Timur even sent the envoys a jug of wine beforehand so they would arrive in a jovial mood. The food was roast horse, sheep and rice.

They call the man who drinks the most wine Bahadur, which is as much as to say "a valorous man" ; and he who does not drink, is made to do so, although he does not wish it.

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

Daily lifeSamarkandc.1488

The veiled bride and the child who unveils her

Visiting his uncle in Samarkand at the age of five, Babur found the new bride still veiled 'according to the custom of the Turks', and was told to take the veil off. The translators' note explains the custom: a Turki bride stayed veiled for a time after marriage until a child of the family pulled the veil off and ran, which was thought to bring the child luck in marriage.

she still kept her face covered with a veil, according to the custom of the Turks. He directed me, and I took off her veil.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

CitiesSamarkandc.1500

Samarkand: every trade in its own bazaar

Babur calls Samarkand 'a wonderfully elegant city'. Its markets were arranged so each trade had its own bazaar, its bakers and cooks were excellent, and it made the best paper in the world and a crimson velvet exported everywhere. He also measured the city walls himself at 10,600 paces round.

Samarkand is a wonderfully elegant city. One of its distinguishing peculiarities is, that each trade has its own bazar ; so that different trades are not mixed together in the same place. The established customs and regulations are good. The bakers' shops are excellent, and the cooks are skilful. The best paper in the world comes from Samarkand.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

The Uzbek turning charge

Describing how he lost the battle, Babur explains the Uzbek method: they always tried to turn the enemy's flank (the tulghama), and charged front and rear together, shooting arrows at full gallop, chiefs and common men mixed together, retreating just as fast if driven back. Babur escaped by swimming his armoured horse across the Kohik river.

In battle, the great reliance of the Uzbeks is on the Tulghmeh (or turning the enemy's flank). They never engage without using the Tulghmeh. Another of their practices is to advance and charge in front and rear, discharging their arrows at full gallop, pell-mell, chiefs and common soldiers, and, if repulsed, they in like manner retire full gallop.

Babur (translated by John Leyden and William Erskine, 1826)

Samarkand: an earth-walled city a little bigger than Seville

Clavijo compares Samarkand to his own Seville: an earthen wall, a city a little larger, but with suburbs and gardens stretching for leagues and more people outside the walls than within. Timur's palaces stood among the gardens. Elsewhere he watches Timur drive a vaulted market street through the city in twenty days, pulling down houses in its path.

The city of Samarcand is situated in a plain, and surrounded by an earthen wall. It is a little larger than the city of Seville, but, outside the city, there are a great number of houses, joined together in many parts, so as to form suburbs.

Ruy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

A great khan at Samarkand, heir of Tamerlane

Perry reports that the lands between Siberia and the Caspian hold many hordes of Tartars, each with its own chief, but many recognise one principal khan at Samarkand said to descend from Tamerlane. He adds that their religion is close to Islam, except that they eat horse meat, which the Turks and Crimean Tartars refuse. By 1700 Samarkand had no such overlord; this is hearsay.

Many of them acknowledge one principal Cham as their Chief, who has his Residence at Samarcand, … and derives his Pedigree, as 'tis said, from the Great Tamerlane.

Captain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)
