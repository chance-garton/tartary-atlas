My Tatar servant understood the Yakuts

Sarychev argues that the Yakuts descend from 'an ancient race of Tartars' who never became Muslim. His proof is practical: one of his own attendants was a Tatar, and he understood most of what the Yakuts said without difficulty. Modern linguists agree that Sakha is a Turkic language, related to Tatar.

The Jakuts are probably derived from an ancient race of Tartars not yet converted to Mahometanism. This appears evident from a similarity of their features, their mode of life, and still more their language, which approaches so nearly to the Tartar dialect, that one of my attendants, who was a Tartar, understood most of what they said without any difficulty.

Saw itGavriil Sarychev (English translation, 1806)

The nameYakutskLegend set about 300 years before 1787

The Yakuts' own story: fleeing from Lake Baikal

The Yakuts told Sauer they call themselves Sakha and came from the south. Their chief Omogai Bey led his tribe and cattle from the plains west of Lake Baikal to escape a stronger horde, fought the Buryats, and followed the Lena north to the meadows around today's Yakutsk. A related people near Krasnoyarsk, which Sauer calls Mongols, also called themselves Sakha and spoke the same language.

The nation known among the Russians by the name of Yakuti call themselves Socha, and say that they came originally from the south. … The Toyon (i.e. Chief) Omogai Bey, with all his tribe and cattle, left the fertile plains situated to the west of the lake Baical, or Baighal, to make way for a more powerful horde

Heard itMartin Sauer (1802), relating Yakut tradition

Keep in mind

Oral tradition recorded through interpreters. The Sakha language is Turkic, so calling the Krasnoyarsk kin 'Mongals' is Sauer's loose label.

The nameYakutsk1829

Yakut words are 'pure Turkish'

In Yakutsk Erman compared the Yakuts with the Tatars of western Siberia in looks and language. Their words for God, iron and other metals and fishing gear were 'all pure Turkish', and they named their milk spirits as the Turkish Tatars did. So he rejects the idea that the Yakuts owed their culture to Mongol neighbours.

It cannot by any means be maintained, that the Yakuts have received the better part of their civilisation, at some time or other, from Mongolian neighbours; their names for the Deity, for iron, and the other metals, (excepting the tin of the Buraets), for their fishing gear, &c. are all pure Turkish.

Saw itAdolph Erman (translated by W. D. Cooley, 1848)

CitiesYakutsk1620-1632

How the Russians built their fort among the Yakuts

Sauer gives a short history of the conquest: Cossacks first heard of the Yakuts in 1620; a chief named Millach went over to the Russians and gave them forty archers to defeat his rival Tygin. Tribute was collected from 1630, and in 1632 the first ostrog (fort) was built on a hill, later moved to where Yakutsk stands. The Russian town grew out of a local feud.

Millach is the first chief reported to have gone over to the Russians. … He supplied the Russians with food, and gave them 40 archers to subdue Tygin and his tribe, which was accomplished on Tygin's falling in the field. Tribute was collected in 1630, and in 1632 the first ostrog was built among them on the mountain Tshebedal, but afterwards removed to the place where the town now stands.

Heard itMartin Sauer (1802)

Horsehair for the spirit of the mountain

Crossing a mountain, Wrangel's lead Yakut driver pulled a few hairs from his horse's mane and tied them to a tree as an offering to the spirit of the mountain. Travellers on foot planted a stick instead. Even baptised Yakuts kept the custom, and the drivers sang songs praising the landscape, which his sergeant explained was meant to flatter the spirit.

Our leading postilion got off his horse, plucked a few hairs from the mane, and fastened them to the tree with much solemnity of manner. He told us that this was a customary offering to the spirit of the mountain, to obtain his protection during the journey, and that foot-passengers placed a stick in the ground with the same intention.

Saw itFerdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840)

The nameYakutsk1822

Yakuts are Tartars: the Kazan test

Arriving in Yakutsk in October 1822, Cochrane states that the Yakuts are 'evidently of Tartar origin, as their language is understood by the Tartars of Kazan'. He praises them as ingenious smiths who make their own knives, guns and kettles, living mostly on horse-flesh, but also describes them with the harsh prejudice of his day.

They are evidently of Tartar origin, as their language is understood by the Tartars of Kazan. Their complexion is a light copper colour; they are generally of low stature, with more regular and pleasing features, than the Tongousi

Heard itJohn Dundas Cochrane (1824)

About Yakuts (Sakha); Kazan Tatars

Keep in mind

Aley the shaman, father of twelve sons

The Yakut founding story continues with Aley, a runaway from the Buryats who became Omogai's overseer and was thought to have supernatural powers. He declared himself a shaman, prophesied that Omogai's daughter would bear no children, and chose the chief's adopted girl instead. After Omogai's death most of the tribe followed Aley, who is said to have had twelve sons; the eldest founded a named tribe.

He observed, that all his tribe esteemed Aley to adoration; for they supposed him to possess supernatural powers, and attributed his continual good fortune and success to the immediate influence of spirits. … Aley now avowed himself a Shaman, and assumed the powers of divination.

Legend or rumourMartin Sauer (1802), relating Yakut tradition

CitiesYakutsk1785-1786

Yakutsk: a decaying wooden fort and ice windows

Sarychev describes Yakutsk as the oldest town in the region, grown up around a wooden ostrog of 1647 whose towers still stood but were falling into decay. There were a few stone churches, but all the houses were wooden, 'in the old Russian taste', with Yakut huts scattered between them. For windows they used mica or bladders in summer and slabs of ice in winter.

The ostrog, or wooden fortress, was erected in the year 1647, and soon afterwards the building of the town commenced. This old edifice, with its towers, is still standing, but in some places is fallen very much to decay. … All the dwelling-houses are built of wood, in the old Russian taste, with here and there Jakutish huts interspersed between them.

Saw itGavriil Sarychev (English translation, 1806)

About Russians, with Yakut (Sakha) huts among them

Keep in mind

Sarychev's date of 1647 differs from Sauer's (W008) story of a first fort in 1632 that was later moved.

Daily lifeYakutsk to Okhotsk roadlate 1780s

A priest flogs his Tartar guides

In a footnote Sauer tells why he calls the expedition's priest 'more than savage'. On the road to Okhotsk the priest lost some food, assumed his two Tartar guides had stolen it, and had them tied to trees and flogged so badly that one died. Runaway exiles turned out to be the thieves. The priest said no harm was done because the guides were not Christians.

On a mere supposition that his two Tartar guides had taken it, he tied each of them up by an arm to a tree, and had them flogged to such a degree, that one of them died, and the other never recovered the use of his arm: it was afterwards known, that some runaway exiles hid in the woods were the thieves.

Heard itMartin Sauer (1802), footnote

About 'Tartar' guides (on this road, most likely Yakuts)

Keep in mind

Sauer was not present; he tells it to show the priest's character. The same priest burned the Aleuts' carved masks, as Sauer reports on this page.
