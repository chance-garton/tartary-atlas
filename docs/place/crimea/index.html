BuildingsCrimea1803

Brick houses on the plain, flat turf roofs in the mountains

Reuilly contrasts two kinds of settled Tatar house. Plains Tatars live in small Turkish-style houses of stone or, lacking stone, brick, and burn dried cakes of turf and dung. Mountain Tatars build on steep slopes with flat turf roofs, packed so closely that each row of roofs forms a street for the row above.

they inhabit small houses built after the Turkish fashion, and make use of bricks, when they cannot procure stone… their houses, which are built on the steep sides of the mountains, are roofed with turf in a flat manner… and they are built so close together, that these flat roofs seem like a street for the row of houses which stands next above them

Cave homes at Inkerman, later reused by Tatars

Webster examined the rock-cut chambers and chapel at Inkerman, near Sevastopol. From the altar, carved cross and style he judged them medieval Christian dwellings, and he noticed rafter marks showing some were part built, with a few 'reinhabited, or reconstructed by the Tartars'. He credits the builders to Christians, not Tatars, and says the Tatars reused them.

Some seem to have been partly excavated and partly built, which is proved by the marks of the rafters in the rocks, as also by these being, in a few instances, reinhabited, or reconstructed by the Tartars … the inference is natural that these excavations were the dwellings of the Christians.

Daily lifeCrimea1760s (describing an older custom)

A bride's dowry measured in a pyramid of millet

In a footnote de Tott describes an old Tartar wedding custom: a dish was set on the veiled bride's head and millet poured over it until the heap reached the dish. Small brides fared badly, so by his day families simply counted measures of millet. He then speculates that European nuptial crowns and sugar-plums come from this rite.

they placed a flat dish… on the head of the new married woman; they stretched a veil over her head… after this they poured millet on the trencher, which, spreading all around her, formed a cone… her dowry was not complete until the pyramid of millet reached up to the trencher, the veil still allowing her to breath. This custom was not favourable to little persons

A Tatar curse: 'may you stay in one place like a Christian'

In his chapter on Tatar customs Guagnini says they never stay long in one place and think doing so a great misfortune. When angry they curse their children with it: may you be stuck in one place forever like a Christian and smell your own stink. He adds that they measure the growth of their realm by how many provinces they have laid waste, since they live in the open fields.

(to) stay, and when angry they call down on their children this grievous evil, saying: 'O may you cling forever to one place, like a Christian, and smell your own stink.' … The more provinces they have laid waste, the larger, they say, they have made their realms (for they are used to living in the fields under the open sky).

The nameCrimea1794 to 1800

Nogais: 'unmixed descendants' of Genghis Khan's army

Pallas divides the Crimean Tatars into three classes. The Nogais, as their faces show, are 'unmixed descendants' of the Mongol tribe that formed the bulk of Genghis Khan's invading army. The steppe Tatars keep some Mongol features and build with unbaked clay bricks; the nobles and the mountain people are mixed with Turks and other nations.

All these Nagays are, as their features evince, the unmixed descendants of the Mongolian tribe, who formed the bulk of the army of Tshingis-Khan, which invaded Russia and the Crimea.

Tartar law: the victim's family carries out the sentence

A Jew's slave murdered his master and then converted to Islam hoping for pardon. De Tott explains that under Tartar law the injured family must execute the criminal themselves. Khan Maksud Giray refused to spare the convert, saying he would hand over his own brother if guilty; in the end the Jews were allowed to stone the murderer according to the law of Moses.

by the Tartar law the criminal must perish by the hand of the injured person, or by his heirs. The nameCrimea1774

'The Tatar peoples' in the 1774 treaty: free, and ruled by Genghis's line

The 1911 editor prints Article 3 of the Kucuk Kaynarca treaty between Russia and the Ottomans. It lists the 'Tatar peoples' by name, the Crimeans and the Nogay hordes of Budjak, Kuban, Yedisan, Jamboyluk and Yedichkul, and declares them independent, governed by khans of 'the line of Genghis' chosen by the Tatars themselves. Here 'Tatar' is a legal and diplomatic name for real, named peoples north of the Black Sea, and their rulers claim descent from the Mongol conqueror.

The tribes of Crimea, Budjak, Kuban, Yedisan, Jamboyluk and Yedichkul, and all the Tatar peoples without exception, shall be recognised and accepted by the two states as free and entirely independent, subject to no foreign state. Their own khans, elected and installed from the line of Genghis by the common vote and agreement of all the Tatar peoples …

How Bahcesaray was founded, 'as the Tatar historians say'

As the Tatars multiplied and the old site grew too small, they settled at Bahcesaray and spread over the whole peninsula, while the 'Genoese infidels' stayed in their seaside fortresses. He names Jochi Khan and 'Artogmuz Khan' as the conquerors behind its first building.

According to the Tatar historians, the first conqueror of Crimea settled at the Eski Salachik yurt; Tatars came to Crimea from the cities of Mahan, Ahlat, Kerman and Yesu and from the lands of Kazan, Astrakhan and Sarai, and as they multiplied they no longer fitted into the old yurt, and settled at this Bahcesaray site.

Felt huts that ride on carts

The Nogais, Pallas says, live in small felt huts four to five and a half arshines across that cannot be taken apart; two men lift the whole hut onto a two-wheeled cart to move it. The next page adds that the huts have a smoke-hole cover worked by a cord and are lined with reed mats, and that the Nogais must buy hazel poles from the Crimean mountain Tatars because the steppe has no timber. A footnote compares them to the ancient 'wagon-dwellers' of the Sea of Azov.

The Nagays, as well as their kinsmen near the Lines of Caucasus, and the Akhtouba, live in small huts constructed of felt, the largest of which are from about four to five and a half arshines in diameter, and, like those of the Kundure Tartars, cannot be taken to pieces, but are placed by two men upon a carriage, and thus removed from one place to another.

Bakhchysarai kept for the Tatars by imperial decree

The old Tatar capital, the authors explain, kept its eastern look because Catherine II granted the Tatars sole possession of the town. Walking its narrow streets of mosques, shops and cemeteries felt like the old quarters of Constantinople. On the same page they give a potted history: Batu Khan's Tatars founded the kingdom in the 1200s, and Bakhchysarai became the capital in the early 1500s.

Bagtche Serai has completely retained its national character in consequence of an ukase of Catherine II., empowering the Tatars to retain exclusive possession of their own capital. You would fancy yourself in the heart of the East, in walking through the narrow streets of the town, the mosques, shops, and cemeteries of which so much resemble those of the old quarters of Constantinople.

Daily lifeCrimea1803

Coffee-houses, hawking and a singer with a guitar

Noble Tatars love hawking and hare-hunting; in town, men spend whole days cross-legged in coffee-houses, smoking and talking. Sometimes a singer recites stories or verses to a guitar-like instrument while the listeners nod in approval. Reuilly finds Tatar song 'grave, harsh, and discordant' and describes a dance of shaking limbs with the feet barely leaving the ground.

Hawking and hunting the hare, form the greatest delight of the noble Tartars. In the towns their amusements are reduced to meeting in the coffee-houses… Sometimes a singer recites a story or chaunts verses, accompanying himself with a bad instrument, in the shape of a guitar; the assembly listens with the greatest attention, and expresses the pleasure it receives by slight motions of the head.

Strange talesCrimea1803

Broken ribs cured by drinking gruel

Reuilly watched a Tatar remedy: a servant of Atai Myrza who broke two ribs falling from his horse was made to drink water-gruel until his belly swelled, under threat of the bastinado, so the ribs would push back into place. Reuilly will not vouch for the cure but admits the man was better by the time he left.

A domestic of Atai Myrza having fallen from his horse, and had two of his ribs broken, his master made him drink water gruel till his belly was prodigiously inflated; and when he refused to take more, he was threatened with the bastinado and drenched with the gruel… his master assuring me that the ribs would get into their places spontaneously.

How the Nogais came to the Crimea

In his history chapter Reuilly says that when Mengli Giray became khan under Ottoman protection (1478), few Tatars actually lived in the Crimea; the khan settled thousands of Nogais captured in wars on the Volga, and his successors peopled the Kuban and the lands between the Don and Dniester the same way.

When the sovereignty of the peninsula passed to Menguely Guerai, there were not many Tartars amongst its inhabitants; but the wars which he carried on with them on the banks of the Volga, afforded him an opportunity to bring with him into the Crimea, several thousand Nogays, who were obliged to establish themselves in that country.
