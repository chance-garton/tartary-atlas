Daily lifeBalkhc. 1809

Kumiss: drunk all summer, never sold

Elphinstone describes how the Uzbeks make kimmiz (kumiss) by leaving mare's milk overnight in a skin and then beating and rolling it until morning. He notes that it can only be had in the last two months of summer, that people who can afford it stay drunk for much of that time, and that it is never sold. Nearby he adds that Uzbeks love horse-flesh but usually have to make do with beef.

The liquor thus made is of a whitish colour and a sourish taste: it is only to be had in plenty during the two last months of summer, and those who can afford it are generally drunk for the greater part of that period; but kimmiz is not sold, and those only can enjoy it who have mares enough to make it in the house.

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

BuildingsBalkhc. 1809

The round felt tent used 'over all Tartary'

Elphinstone contrasts two ways of living among the Uzbeks. Their houses, villages and towns look just like Afghan ones, but their tent is the round lattice tent covered with felt (the yurt), which he calls the kirgah and says is used across all of Tartary. On the same page he adds that it is warmer than the Afghan black tent and hardly harder to carry.

Their houses, villages, and towns, exactly resemble those of the Afghauns, but their tents are widely different The Uzbek tent is of the kind called Kirgah, which appears to be in use over all Tartary, in part of Persia, and even in part of China. It is round, and formed of lattice work … covered with black or grey felts.

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

Strange talesBalkhJune 1832 (as told)

A goat that walked the rooftops from Balkh to Mazar

Mohan Lal records two local marvels. An old mud fort outside Balkh was said to grow taller at night (he wanted to check, but the caravan left). And the city had once been so vast that its roofs touched all the way to Mazar, so that a goat climbed onto a roof in Balkh and came down the next day in Mazar, some 20 kilometres away.

Without the city of Balk is an old mud fort, called Chihal Gazi, which, people say, in the night increases in its height. I wished to stop and verify the fact, but our caravan started at the very eve. … a goat climbed up one of the roofs in Balk, and descended next day at Mazar, whither his master followed him also.

Legend or rumourMohan Lal (1846 edition)

Keep in mind

CitiesBalkh, AfghanistanSeptember 1222

Balkh, empty but for barking dogs

Travelling by night after crossing the Amu Darya on the way to Chinggis's camp, the party passed Balkh, a very large city whose people had recently revolted and fled, and all they heard inside was the barking of dogs. Bretschneider's note adds, from Rashid al-Din, that Balkh fell to Chinggis in 1221 and most of its people were massacred.

travelling during the night, we passed Ban-li a very large city, the inhabitants of which had revolted not long ago, and fled. We heard the barking of the dogs in the city.

Saw itLi Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

CitiesBalkhFebruary 1825

Balkh, 'mother of cities', in ruins

Approaching Balkh, once capital of Bactria, Moorcroft passed ruins said to be the elephant stables of the old kings of Turkestan, then entered through a broken gate into streets of rubbish, broken bricks and crumbling clay walls. On the next pages he says only one covered bazaar was still inhabited, the population was under a thousand families, and there were no relics of antiquity apart from one tiled shrine.

A short distance in advance brought us to the ruins of some buildings, said to have been the elephant stables of the kings of Turkistan: similar remains accompanied the road to the wall of the modern town, which we entered by a mean gateway, the folding-doors of which were off their hinges and lying in the mud, no insignificant type of neglect and decay.

Saw itWilliam Moorcroft (edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)

BuildingsBalkh1333 (describing damage from 1220-1221)

Balkh's great mosque, broken open by Genghis Khan for treasure

Balkh lay in ruins, never rebuilt since Genghis Khan destroyed it; only the outline of the city could be traced. Its mosque had been one of the finest in the world, and Genghis had three of its pillars torn down because he was told treasure was hidden beneath them. Ibn Battuta then tells the legend behind that rumour: a governor's wife built the mosque from the price of a jewelled robe and buried a third of the money under a pillar for repairs.

It has not been rebuilt since its destruction by the cursed Jengiz Khan. … Its mosque was one of the largest and handsomest in the world. Its pillars were incomparable : three of which were destroyed by Jengiz Khan, because it had been told him, that the wealth of the mosque lay concealed

Saw itIbn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)

Strange talesBalkh, Afghanistanearly 7th century

A Turkish prince speared in a dream for raiding a monastery

The New Monastery at Balkh was so rich that rulers coveted it. A son of the Turkish Yabghu khan (named on the previous page) led his whole horde to plunder it and camped nearby. That night he dreamed that the guardian god Vaisravana drove a long halberd through his chest; he woke in pain, sent for the monks to beg forgiveness, and died before the messenger returned.

[He gathered] his tribes and led his troops to fall on the monastery by surprise, meaning to seize its treasures. He camped in the open not far from here. That night he dreamed he saw the god Vaisravana, who said: 'What power have you, that you dare to destroy this monastery?' and drove a long halberd through him from chest to back. The khan woke in terror, with a pain in his heart.

CitiesBalkh1832

Balkh: slaughtered by Genghis, built of frail brick

Burnes lists Balkh's fate: its people 'butchered in cold blood by Jenghis Khan', then ruled by the house of Timur, the Mughals, Nadir Shah, the Afghans and Bukhara. In 1832 it had under 2000 people. Looking at its crumbling remains he doubts it was ever a substantial city, since its buildings rested only on brick foundations.

Its inhabitants were butchered in cold blood by Jenghis Khan; and under the house of Timour it became a province of the Mogul empire. … and from the frail materials of which its buildings are constructed, the foundations being only brick, I doubt if Balkh ever were a substantial city.

MixedAlexander Burnes (1834)

CitiesBalkhJune 1832

Balkh, 'mother of cities', a mass of ruins and dust

Mohan Lal found the ancient city of Balkh reduced to ruins and dust among orchards, with a broad bazaar roofed crudely with rafters, hay and mud, whose shops opened only on Tuesdays. An Uzbek customs officer searched their baggage. Locals called Balkh the mother of cities, founded by a son of Noah.

This was formerly a very large and populous city, but at present nothing is to be seen except a mass of ruins and dust. Orchards are scattered in every spot … The bazar of Balk, though broad, is irregularly roofed with rafters, hay, and mud.

Saw itMohan Lal (1846 edition)

BuildingsBalkhearly 18th century

Balkh: stone and brick houses and a marble castle of the Uzbek khan

Describing the Uzbek khanate of Balkh, Deguignes calls Balkh the most important town held by the 'Mahometan Tartars': large and populous, mostly built of stone or brick, with earth ramparts faced by a wall, a trading hub between Bukhara and India, and a khan's castle 'in the oriental style' built almost entirely of marble from nearby quarries. He also calls Balkh's Uzbeks the most civilised of the Muslim Tartars.

The town of Balkh, at present the most considerable of all the towns the Mahometan Tartars possess, is large and well peopled; most of its buildings are built of stone or brick … The Khan's castle is a large building in the oriental style, almost entirely built of marble, of which there are very fine quarries in the neighbouring mountains.

CitiesBalkhc. 1809

Balkh: a great ruin with one corner lived in

Balkh, the old capital of the Uzbek-populated province south of the Oxus, is called by Asians the oldest city in the world and the 'mother of towns' (the scan is garbled here). By Elphinstone's day it had shrunk to a small inhabited corner inside a vast walled ruin, with a citadel held by the Afghan governor. The rich countryside was still watered by eighteen canals.

Its ruins still cover a great extent, and are surrounded with a wall, but only one corner is inhabited.

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)
