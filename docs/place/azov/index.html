The nomad Horde sows its grain

Otherwise it was the custom that in the month of February the day on which sowing should take place in March was proclaimed in the camp. On that day those who wished to grow grain drove with their wagons and seed to the fields, usually one or two days' journey from the court camp, and tilled them. The harvest was plentiful, for the soil gave fifty-fold of the grain

Working translation

Original wording

Sonst war es der Gebrauch, dass im Monathe Februar im Lager der Tag, an welchem im März gesäet werden sollte, ausgerufen ward. An diesem Tage fuhren die, so Getreide bauen wollten, mit ihren Wagen und Samen auf die gewöhnlich vom Hoflager Eine oder zwey Tagreisen entlegenen Felder und bestellten dieselben. Die Ernte war ergiebig, denn der Boden gab das Fünfzigfache des Kornes

Editorial context

From the Venetian Josafa Barbaro, who lived at Tana, Hammer reports that Ulu Muhammad kept his camp moving for eleven years without letting the Horde sow in March and wait for the harvest, so it suffered grain shortage and lived on meat and milk alone. Normally, in February the camp announced the sowing day; those who wanted to grow grain drove out with wagons and seed to fields a day or two from the camp. The harvest was rich: fifty-fold for grain and a hundred-fold for millet. Nomads, in other words, also farmed.

Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall (1840), following Josafa Barbaro's Viaggio alla Tana

Burial mounds with stone crosses, credited to the Alans

These have been Christen men, and were chased awaie and destroied by the Tartares. In that region are hills, ryvers, and plaines : wheare are to be seene an infinite nombre of little hills forced in signe or steede of sepultures, and on the toppe of everie of them a great stone with an hole : wherein standeth a crosse of one peece made of an other stone.

Editorial context

Describing the land around Tana, Barbaro says the countless mounds on the steppe are graves, each topped with a pierced stone holding a stone cross. He credits them to the Alans, a Christian people destroyed by the Tartars, not to the Tartars themselves.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Keep in mind

The crosses may be later Christian additions or Barbaro's reading of stone markers; most steppe kurgans are far older than the Alans.

'He that is afraid builds towers'

In this armie are many artisanes, as clothiers, smythes, armorers, and of all other craftes and things that they neede. … For (saving that they are not walled about) they seeme verie great and faire cities. … I saied vnto a Tartarien marchant … howe thinkest thou, is not this a faire thinge ? But he, smiling, againe answered, he that is afearde buyldeth towres

Editorial context

Barbaro insists the horde was not a band of vagabonds: it carried clothiers, smiths and armourers, and its camps looked like great fair cities, only without walls. When he showed a Tatar merchant the fine tower over Tana's gate, the man smiled and said towers are built by the fearful. Barbaro thought he was right.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Venetian treasure-hunters dig a kurgan

we founde in the botome certein vessels of stone, some of them with asshes, some with coles, some emptie and some full of fishe back bones. We founde also v or vi beadestones as bigge as oranges made of bricke and covered with glasse … We founde also halfe the handle of a little ewer of sylver, made with an adders hedde on the toppe.

Editorial context

Tipped off by a story that a Tatar woman in Cairo had told of Alan treasure, Barbaro and six partners hired 120 men and dug into a great mound. Instead of gold they found layers of ash, millet husks and fish scales, then stone vessels, glass-covered brick beads and part of a silver ewer. The dig was so big that locals renamed the mound 'the cave of the Franks'.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Keep in mind

'Whoever is afraid builds towers'

I answer no: for, except that they are not surrounded by walls, such encampments look like very large and very beautiful cities. Being one day at Tana, speaking of this, over whose gate there was a rather fine tower, and having beside me a Tartar merchant who was looking at the tower, I asked him: Does this seem a fine thing to you? And he, looking at me and smiling, said: Pah, whoever is afraid builds a tower.

Working translation

Original wording

Rispondo di nò: conciosia, ch'eccetto il nõ esser circondati di mura, tali alloggiamenti paiono grossissime, & bellissime città. Ritrouandomi, a questo proposito, vn giorno alla Tana, sopra la porta dellaquale era vna torre assai bella, & essendo appresso di me vn Tartaro mercante, ilquale guardaua la torre, gli domãdai: Ti pare vna bella cosa questa? Et egli, guardandomi, & sorridendo, disse: poh, c'ha paura, fa torre

Editorial context

Giosafat Barbaro, a Venetian merchant who lived at Tana on the Don for sixteen years, watched a whole horde migrate past the town. Asked whether these people wander like gypsies, he says no: apart from having no walls, their camps look like very large and beautiful cities. When he asked a Tatar merchant if Tana's fine gate tower was not beautiful, the man laughed: 'Pah, whoever is afraid builds towers.'

Giosafat Barbaro (Viaggio alla Tana, written 1487), in Ramusio's Italian edition

Tall carts carrying round felt houses

This people carie with them innumerable cartes of twoo wheeles higher than ours be, which are closed with mattes made of reades, and parte covered with felte, parte with clothe … Some of these cartes carie their houses vpon them which are made on this wise. They take a cercle of tymber, whose dyameter is a pase and an halfe, crossed within foorthe with other halfe cercles

Editorial context

Barbaro watched the whole horde pass Tana and describes its vehicles: two-wheeled carts taller than Italian ones, closed with reed mats and covered with felt or cloth depending on rank. Some carried a house built on a timber ring with half-hoops above, filled with reed mats and covered in felt, which was lifted off when they camped.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Tartary begins at Tana on the Don

From Constantinople to Tana or Tartary is a thousand miles, going always towards the east, by sea.

Working translation

Original wording

De Constantinopoli usque Tanan vel Tartariam sunt milliaria mille, semper eundo versùs orientem, per mare.

Editorial context

In his closing table of distances Jordanus says it is a thousand miles by sea, always eastwards, from Constantinople to 'Tana or Tartary'. Tana was the Italian trading post at the mouth of the Don, in Golden Horde territory, so for a traveller of 1330 'Tartary' started at the Sea of Azov.

Jordanus Catalani of Séverac, O.P. (Latin text, ed. Coquebert de Montbret, Recueil de voyages IV, Paris 1839)

Baltracan, the plant that fed travellers

The Tartars have a plant in their country which they call Baltracan, the want of which would cause them great suffering, and prevent them from going from place to place, especially across those great deserts and solitudes, where they find nothing to eat except this plant, which supports them and gives them vigour.

Editorial context

In a letter to the Bishop of Padua, Barbaro describes 'Baltracan', a wild plant the Tartars depended on when crossing the steppe. When it sprouted, merchants set out saying 'Let us go, for the Baltracan has grown', and no one chased a runaway slave because he could live on it. He says he ate it himself and later found it in Albania and near Padua.

Giosafat Barbaro, letter of 1491 (translated 1873)
