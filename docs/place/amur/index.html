BuildingsAmur River17th century (written 1703)

Russian forts and towns built across 'Great Tartary'

Fontaney explains how the Muscovites had pushed from Moscow through Siberia along the Irtysh, Ob, Yenisei and Angara to the Selenga and the Amur ('Sangalien-oula' to the Tartars, the Black Dragon river to the Chinese), building forts and towns as they went. The nearest to China were Selenginsk, Nerchinsk and Albazin. In this corner of 'Tartary' the builders were Russians.

The Muscovites were not content with making these discoveries; they built forts and towns at intervals on all these rivers to secure possession of them. The nearest to China were Selinga, Nipchou and Yacsa.

Original wording

Les Moscovites ne se contenterent pas de faire ces découvertes; ils bâtirent de distance en distance des forts & des villes sur toutes ces rivieres, pour s'en assurer la possession. Les plus proches de la Chine étoient Selinga, Nipchou & Yacsa.

Jean de Fontaney, letter to Père de La Chaise (1703)

The 'Water Tatars' re-spelled out of existence

Quoting the History of the Yuan on the Hailan and 'Water Tatar' circuits of Manchuria, the editors change 水達達 (Shui Dada, 'Water Tatars') to 碩達勒達, explained as Manchu for 'a hidden place', and note that the old form has been 'now corrected'. The Qing court thus removed the Tatar name from the history of its own homeland.

Hailan prefecture, Shuo Dalda and other circuits. The History of the Yuan: Hailan (see the Jin Upper Capital entry above) prefecture, Shuo Dalda (Manchu for 'a hidden, sheltered place'; formerly written Shui Dada, 'Water Tatars'; now corrected) and other circuits: the land is broad and the people live scattered.

海蘭府碩達勒達等路元史海蘭(詳前金上京條)府碩達勒達(滿洲語隠避處也舊作水達達)(今改正)等路土地廣濶人民散居

Agui, Yu Minzhong and the Qianlong commission, Researches on Manchu Origins (Siku quanshu Wenyuange copy, Kanseki Repository transcription)

'Water Tatars' in Manchuria

Bretschneider summarises the Ming imperial geography of 1461 on the Jurchen homeland: after the Mongols overthrew the Jin, the Yuan set up military posts there, and the country was inhabited by Jurchens and 'Water Tatars', who gave their name to an administrative circuit. His footnote compares Carpini's 'Su-Mongal' or Water Mongols, who Carpini said called themselves Tartars after a river.

The country was inhabited by the Nü-chi and the Shui Ta-ta (Water Tatars), and formed the circuits of Ho-lan-fu and Shui Ta-ta.

E. Bretschneider, Mediaeval Researches vol. 2 (1888, reissued 1910), summarising the Ming Geography of 1461

About Jurchens (Nu-chi) and 'Water Tatars' (Shui Ta-ta)

Keep in mind

A 19th-century summary in English of a Ming source, not the Yutang jiahua itself.

Daily lifeAmur River1787

Fleeing to China: 6,000 Yakuts in one year

Sauer reports that Yakuts and Tungus unhappy under Russian rule were slipping away to the upper Amur valleys under Chinese protection, where they had reportedly built strong places. Russian officials told him more than 6,000 Yakuts moved to China in 1787 with all their goods, and his footnote remarks that such migrations reduced 'the number of Tartars tributary to Russia'. Elsewhere he blames heavy exactions by Russian officials for the Yakuts' decline.

In the year 1787, there migrated to China, from the districts of Olekma, Yakutsk, and the Vilui, more than 6000 Yakuti, with all their possessions

Martin Sauer (1802), citing Russian officials Evers and Kyshkin

About Yakuts and Tungus (Evenks)

Keep in mind

The figure is second-hand, from two Russian assessors.

Daily lifeLower Amur and Ussuri17th to 18th centuries (compiled 1784)

The 'dog-using' and 'deer-using' tribes of the Amur

The gazetteer lists the northeastern peoples who brought tribute to the Qing post at Ningguta, measuring each by distance: the Fiyaka seven or eight hundred li beyond the three-river confluence, the Kiler three thousand li away on the sea coast, the Kuyala facing Korea across the Tumen. Beyond the 'dog-using' tribes lay a 'deer-using' (reindeer) tribe. These are the peoples European maps grouped in 'Eastern Tartary'.

Another seven or eight hundred li to the northeast are the Fiyaka. Three thousand li northeast of Ningguta are the Kiler, who border the great sea on the east... The Kuyala: their land lies on the north bank of the Tumen river, opposite Kyongwon in Korea... And the deer-using tribe lies beyond the dog-using tribes.

Original wording

又東北行七八百里曰費雅喀自寕古塔東北行三千里曰奇勒爾東濵大海…庫雅喇其地在圖們江北岸與朝鮮之慶逺相對…而使鹿部又在使犬諸部之外

Agui, Liu Jinzhi and the Qianlong commission, Imperially Commissioned Gazetteer of Shengjing (1784 recension; Siku quanshu Wenyuange copy, Kanseki Repository transcription)

Kamchatka's people pushed out by the 'East Tartar kingdoms'

Steller argues that the Itelmens once lived outside China in the Mongol steppe along the Amur and gradually withdrew north and east along the Sea of Okhotsk as the 'East Tartar kingdoms' rose. In a footnote on the same page he links the ending -men in their own name, Itelmen, to the Turkic and Tatar word men ('I'). It is a scholar's hypothesis based on language, looks and customs, not a tradition of the people themselves.

As for the positive arguments that these peoples formerly lived outside China in the Mongol steppe below the Amur, and, when the East Tartar kingdoms were set up, withdrew little by little ever further along the Penzhina Sea [Sea of Okhotsk]

Working translation

Original wording

Was die argumenta affirmativa anbelanget, daß diese Völker ehedem ausserhalb China in der mungalischen Steppe unter dem Amur gewohnet, und sich bey Aufrichtung derer ostlich-tartarischen Reiche nach und nach immer weiter an der penschinischen See retiriret

Georg Wilhelm Steller (ed. J. B. Scherer, 1774)

About Itelmens (Kamchadals), Mongols

Keep in mind
