'Unknown peoples': nobody knew who the Tatars were

The chronicler's first notice of the Tatars is an admission of ignorance. Nobody knew who they were, where they came from, what language they spoke or what faith they held. People called them Tatars, Taurmens or Pechenegs, and learned men linked them to the prophecy of Pseudo-Methodius about peoples locked away in the desert who would come out at the end of time.

there came unknown peoples, whom no one clearly knows: who they are and whence they came, and what their language is, and of what tribe they are, and what their faith is; and they call them Tatars, and others say Taurmens, and others Pechenegs, and others say that these are the ones of whom Methodius, bishop of Patara, bears witness: that they came out of the Etrian desert

The Dasht of Berke: 'a land of the Tatars alone'

Describing the steppe that Timur coveted, Ibn Arabshah calls it the Dasht of Berke (after the Golden Horde khan) and says it was a country belonging to the Tatars alone, teeming with herds and Turkish tribes, well guarded, well peopled and healthy in water and air. He had lived there himself in the 1410s and 1420s.

the Dasht of Berke. It was a country belonging to the Tatars alone, and teeming with every kind of livestock and with the tribes of the Turks; its borders guarded, its sides well peopled, its reaches wide, its water and air wholesome.

'Do not call our lord a Christian, nor us Tartars'

As Rubruck left Sartaq's camp, the secretary Coiac and other court scribes warned him not to say their lord was a Christian: he was a Moal. Rubruck explains that the Mongols treat 'Christian' as the name of a nation and are so proud that they want their own name, Moal, above every other, and will not be called Tartars either, because the Tartars had been a different people.

'Do not say that our lord is a Christian. He is not a Christian but a Moal'; for the name of Christianity seems to them the name of some nation. They are raised to such pride that, though they may perhaps believe something about Christ, they will not be called Christians, wishing to exalt their own name, that is Moal, above every name; nor will they be called Tartars. For the Tartars were another people, about whom I learned as follows.

Daily lifePontic steppe1223

'Swift to battle and swift to flee'

Closing its Kalka story, the Nikon compiler sums up the newcomers: nobody knew where these Tatars came from or where they went. They were quick to fight and quick to run, light and not weighed down by baggage; they fled and then turned back again, the feigned retreat that western writers also noticed. The last phrase, 'like demons', shows the chronicler's fear.

This was the first coming of the Tatars on Rus; they were swift to battle and likewise to flight, being light and not burdened with anything heavy; they flee and then return again, and they are evildoers like demons.

How kumiss is made

Rubruck gives a step-by-step account of kumiss, fermented mare's milk: foals tied to a rope, mares milked, the milk churned in a skin with a club until it bubbles like new wine and turns pungent. He found it tasted of almond milk and 'intoxicates weak heads'. On the next page he says Batu received the milk of three thousand mares every day.

When they have got together a great quantity of milk, which is as sweet as cow's as long as it is fresh, they pour it into a big skin or bottle, and they set to churning it with a stick prepared for that purpose

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Houses built on wheels and covered in white felt

Rubruck opens his description of the Tartars with their homes. The sleeping-houses sit on wheels, are made of interlaced wooden laths that rise to a smoke-hole like a chimney, and are covered with white felt smeared with lime, white earth or powdered bone so that they gleam. He stresses that the Tartars have no fixed dwelling and divide the steppe from the Danube eastward into seasonal pastures.

The houses in which they sleep are set on wheels … [made of] interlaced pieces of wood, and they come together at the top in an opening like a chimney, and they cover this with white felt, which they coat with lime or earth

Mounds, statues and brick towers over the dead

Crossing the steppe, Rubruck saw the tombs of the Comans: great mounds (kurgans) topped by a stone statue holding a cup, small pointed 'pyramids', tiled towers and stone houses, though there was no stone nearby. Just before, he notes that the burial places of Mongol nobles of Genghis's family were kept secret and guarded. Rockhill's note quotes Carpini on Mongol nobles buried with a horse and a mare.

raise a great tumulus over the dead, and set up a statue to him, its face to the east, and holding a cup in its hand at the height of the navel. They make also pyramids to the rich, that is to say, little pointed structures, and in some places I saw great tiled covered towers, and in others stone houses, though there were no stones thereabout.

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Drinking their horses' blood, prizing mare's milk

On long rides, Herberstein was told, Tartars open a vein in their horse and drink the blood, believing it does the animal good. They steer by the Pole Star, which they call the 'iron nail'. On the same page he describes their love of mare's milk and how chiefs share out the entrails of a slaughtered animal and lick the knife clean.

Moreover, if during a long ride they are troubled with hunger or thirst, it is a practice to lance the veins of the horses on which they sit, and relieve their craving by drawing their blood ; and they think that this is an advantage to the animals. As they nearly all wander on uncertain tracks, they are accustomed to direct their course by the observation of the stars

Heard itSigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

About Steppe Tartars (Crimean, Nogai)

Keep in mind

How kumiss is made, and what it tastes like

Rubruck explains the Mongols' summer drink, fermented mare's milk. Foals are tethered on a line so the mares let themselves be milked; the milk is churned in a skin with a club until it bubbles like new wine and turns sour. He found it prickled the tongue, left a taste of almonds and could make a weak head drunk; a clear black kind, cara-cosmos, was kept for the great.

they pour it into a skin or other vessel, where they beat and stir it very well … it begins to bubble like new wine, then to turn sour like leaven … it stings the tongue like rough wine when you drink it. When you have finished drinking, it leaves a taste of almonds on the tongue and greatly cheers the heart

Lors que l'on a acheué de boire, cela laisse sur la langue vn goust d'amande, & resioüit grandement le cœur

Women whose headdresses look like helmets and lances

Married Mongol women wore the bocca (bogtak), a tall bark frame covered with silk and topped with a tuft of quills and peacock feathers. Rubruck says a group of ladies riding in the distance looked like soldiers in helmets with lances raised, and that women rode astride like men. He adds, less kindly, that the fattest women with the smallest noses were thought the most beautiful.

and one sees them from afar, they look like soldiers, helmets on head and lances erect. For this bocca looks like a helmet, and the tuft above it is like a lance. And all the women sit their horses astraddle like men.

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppec. 1436 to 1452

Nomads who sowed and reaped

Barbaro answers the obvious question of where so many people got their grain. Each February the horde announced where sowing would happen; volunteers went with seed, carts and families, ploughed and sowed, and later returned to harvest while the khan's camp circled nearby. He says the yields were huge.

About the mooneth of Februarie they make proclamacons throughout the Lordo, that he which woll sowe shall prepare his things necessarie against the mooneth of Marche, to sowe in such a place. … And there do they eare, sowe, and tarie, till they haue furnisshed that they came for, which doon they retorne to their Lordo.

Saw itGiosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

A house thirty feet wide, pulled by twenty-two oxen

Some Mongol houses were never taken down but rode on huge carts. Rubruck measured one cart himself and counted the oxen. The axle was as thick as a ship's mast and a driver stood in the doorway of the house. On the next pages he describes felt-covered chests on carts and a rich man's camp of one or two hundred carts.

I myself once measured the width between the wheel-tracks of a cart xx feet, and when the house was on the cart it projected beyond the wheels on either side v feet at least. I have myself counted to one cart xxii oxen drawing one house, eleven abreast across the width of the cart, and the other eleven before them.

A riddle: 'like a white tent, with no door'

The Codex preserves Cuman riddles, the oldest recorded Turkic riddles. This one runs: 'Like a white tent, it has no mouth. That is the egg.' Kuun reads 'akkoy' as 'ak ev', literally 'white house', and renders it 'the tent of a new bride'. The riddle draws on the everyday sight of a tent, the home of the people who told it.

Like a white tent (ak ev), it has no mouth. That is the egg.

Horse meat cooked under the saddle

Chevalier repeats the famous story that Tartars on the march softened horse meat by riding on it under the saddle and ate it with no sauce but the horse's sweat. He adds that they chose worn-out, sick or lame horses for this, not the best ones. At home they ate millet porridge and boiled horse meat.

when they goe to war and are upon their March, they make it ready, or rather mortify, or just heat it, under their Saddles, and eat it afterwards in this condition without any other sauce then the froth upon the Flesh, made by the sweating of the Horse

Heard itPierre Chevalier (translated by Edward Brown, 1672)

About Crimean Tatars on campaign

Keep in mind

The saddle-meat story goes back to medieval writers and is a stock European image of steppe warriors; Chevalier does not say he saw it.

The namePontic steppe and Moldavialater 1400s (Chalkokondyles), annotated 1785

Were the Tatars Herodotus's Scythians?

In an appendix Schlözer prints what the Athenian historian Laonikos Chalkokondyles, writing in the later 1400s, said about the north. Like other learned Byzantines he gave new peoples old classical names and called the Tatars Scythians. Schlözer points out the result: taking them for Herodotus's Scythians of Moldavia, Chalkokondyles had them migrate from Europe into Asia instead of the other way round. He adds that the Polish writer Miechowita mocked this error and distinguished four kinds of Tatars: Chagatai east of the Caspian, the Kipchak Horde between the Don and the Yaik, the Crimeans, and the Lithuanian Tatars.

The good Chalkokondyles really seems to take the Tatars of his time, who lived from the Danube to the Altai under several Mongol rulers, for the Scythians of Herodotus in Moldavia: consequently they must of course have migrated from Europe to Asia, not the other way round!

Strange talesPontic-Caspian steppeabout 1220 (hearsay)

A plain without cities where the sun rises before a chop is cooked

Far to the north-west lies 'K'o-fu-ch'a', which Bretschneider identifies with the Kipchak steppe north of the Caspian. Yelü Chucai heard that it was endless plain, its people had no cities or towns, bred cattle and horses and made a drink from honey, and that summer nights were so short the sun rose again before a mutton chop could be cooked. Bretschneider shows the short-night detail was copied from a Tang-era account of a Siberian people.

To the north-west of Yin-du (Hindustan) is the realm of K'o fu-ch'a… The people there have no cities or towns. They breed much cattle and horses. They make a fermented beverage from honey. In that country the days (in summer) are long and the nights short. In little more than the time necessary to cook a mutton chop, the sun rises again.

Legend or rumourYelü Chucai's Si yu lu, in a Yuan-era abridgement (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Kipchaks (the steppe later ruled by the Golden Horde)

Keep in mind

Hearsay, partly borrowed from the Tang history; the Mongols did not conquer the Kipchak steppe until 1236.

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe (Dasht-i Kipchak)c.1332

Nine animals for one stolen

Crossing the Kipchak steppe by hired cart (araba), Ibn Battuta noticed huge herds grazing with no herdsmen. He explains it by the harsh law on theft: a thief had to return the animal with nine more, and if he could not, his children were taken, or he himself killed. He also notes that the treeless steppe folk burned dung for fuel.

he is compelled to return it with nine others of equal value. But, if this is not in his power, his children are taken. If, however, he have no children, he is himself slaughtered just like a sheep.

Heard itIbn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)

About Golden Horde (Kipchak steppe)

Keep in mind

The sentence begins on the previous page (n102): a man 'convicted of having stolen a beast'. The law is what he was told, not a case he saw.

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppeJune 1253

First taste of kumiss, and a moving city of carts

Rubruck says that in two months between Soldaia and Sartaq's camp they never slept under a roof and saw no village, nor any trace of a building, except great numbers of Cuman tombs. One evening his guide gave him kumiss; he broke into a sweat from horror and novelty, but found it very tasty. Next morning the carts of the commander Scatatai, loaded with houses, looked to him like a great city coming towards him.

That evening the lad who was guiding us gave us kumiss to drink, and at the first draught I sweated all over from horror and novelty, for I had never drunk it before. Yet it seemed to me very tasty, as indeed it is. In the morning we met Scatatai's carts loaded with houses, and it seemed to me that a great city was coming to meet me.
