BuildingsSelitrennoye, Astrakhan Oblast1770s (earlier visit, quoted in the 1793 journey)

Glazed tiles and mosaic on a khan's tomb

Pallas describes the finest building at the Selitrennoye ruins as a khan's family mausoleum with a prayer house above, its foundation walls two fathoms high. The brickwork was the most elegant he had seen among any Tartar ruins, set with glazed tiles in green, yellow, white and blue, with stucco flowers, shell-work and mosaic panels. He adds that whole cargoes of its bricks had been carried off to build Astrakhan.

In the whole brickwork, which consists of beautiful broad squares, disposed in the most regular manner, there is a degree of taste and elegance of which I have no where seen an instance among the ruins of the Tartars. The outside of the walls is not only embellished in all the interstices between the bricks with glazed earthen ornaments, of a green, yellow, white, and blue colour,

Peter Simon Pallas, quoting his own earlier Travels (vol. 3), in the English translation (1812 edition)

CitiesSarai, lower Volga (Akhtuba channel), Astrakhan and Volgograd regions, Russia13th to 14th centuries (ruins described by 18th and 19th century travellers)

Sarai, the Horde's capital, and its ruins on the Akhtuba

In his survey of the towns of Kipchak, Hammer places Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, on the Akhtuba channel of the Volga, where ruins stretched forty versts into the steppe and at least ten versts along the river. He takes from the Persian historian Wassaf that Batu founded it, and from coins that there were several Sarais: the old and the new (the second only an extension of the first), with Gülistan apparently a suburb, and a third called Sali Serai. Elsewhere (p. 140 to 141) he says Batu built Sarai in his last years, as he had earlier built Kazan.

Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans of the Golden Horde, whose ruins [lie] forty versts into the steppe from the bank of the Volga and at least ten versts along the Akhtuba … There were, as the coins show, three Sarais: the old and the new, the latter being only an extension of the old, just as Gülistan, which often appears on coins, seems to have been only a suburb of the residence Sarai

CitiesSarai (Selitrennoye, Astrakhan oblast)c.1320

Sarai: a new city of merchants and the Turkish slave trade

Abu'l-Fida's entry on Seray (Sarai), capital of 'the states of Berke', says the present ruler is Uzbek, that the city lies in a plain on the Volga north-west of the Caspian, and that merchants crowd there and do a great trade in Turkish slaves. He calls it a modern city, founded by a descendant of Chinggis Khan named Sain Batu, and adds day-length information from a man of the city.

The prince now reigning is called Uzbek. Sarai lies in a plain, north-west of the Caspian Sea … It is a place much visited by merchants, and there is a great trade there in Turkish slaves. The town is of recent origin; its founding is attributed to a descendant of Chinggis Khan called Sain Batu.

Sarai, 'the new town that Baatu is making'

On his way home Rubruck asked about his lost books and vestments. Coiac, an official of Batu's son Sartach, said he had left them near Sarai, a town Batu was then building on the Volga. It is a rare contemporary glimpse of the founding of the Golden Horde's capital, and Rubruck later travelled south toward it.

I left your things at my father's, who stays near Sarai, the new town that Baatu is making on the Etilia

Coiac, an official of Sartach, as reported by William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

Sarai, an Islamic city built by Khan Berke

Ibn Arabshah names Sarai as the throne city of the Dasht and calls it 'a city of Islamic building, of marvellous structure'. Khan Berke, he says, built it when he became a Muslim and made it his royal seat. Just before this he laments that today, from Khwarazm to Crimea, nothing stirs of those peoples, and only gazelles and camels are left.

The throne of the Dasht is Sarai, a city of Islamic building and marvellous structure … Sultan Berke, may God have mercy on him, built it when he became a Muslim, and took it as the seat of kingship and chose it.

CitiesAstrakhan and Sarai, lower Volga, RussiaWinter 1395 to 1396

Walls of ice at Astrakhan, and Timur's razing of Sarai

After defeating Tokhtamysh, Timur resolved to level Astrakhan. Its people used the hard winter to build walls of ice blocks cemented by pouring water over them, but Timur marched through the deep snow, emptied the town, razed it and had its headman (kelanter) Mahmudi drowned under the ice that was meant to protect him. He then went on to Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, and laid it waste, as revenge for the Horde's earlier destruction of a city of the Chagatai realm. The loot of both cities was divided among the army.

In spite of the severe winter, which still gave the inhabitants of Astrakhan the advantage of fortifying themselves with walls of ice, joined together by pouring water over them, and in spite of the deep snow, he marched on Astrakhan, emptied the town of its inhabitants and had it razed … From Astrakhan Timur moved to Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans, and laid it waste

BuildingsTsarev (Tsarevy Pody), Volgograd Oblast1793

Mortar harder than the bricks

At Tsarevy Pody ('the royal residence') on the Akhtuba, Pallas examined a flat vaulted tomb mound inside a sandstone wall. The vaults were of six layers of square bricks joined with a grey cement so hard that the bricks broke before the mortar did. He analysed it as lime, powdered charcoal and ground sandstone, and wondered whether sour milk, plentiful among a pastoral people, had been mixed in.

It has, however, acquired such a solid consistence, that it is easier to break the well-burnt bricks, than to separate the mortar. This grey cement appears to be a mixture of unslacked lime, pulverized charcoal, and pounded sandstone, instead of the sand used for building.

Peter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

BuildingsSelitrennoye1773

Glazed tiles and bricks carted off to Astrakhan

Pallas says he had never seen such splendour in any Tatar ruin: brick walls laid with great regularity, the joints inlaid with green, yellow, white and blue glazed ornaments, and a facade with mosaic tiles. He blames time and plunder for the damage, adding that whole boatloads of bricks had been shipped to Astrakhan, and that to get a thousand whole bricks the workers smashed two thousand because the mortar was so hard.

The outer face of the walls is not only inlaid, panel by panel in all the joints, with glazed clay ornaments of green, yellow, white and blue colour in triangular and other figures… But time, and still more the greed of the rabble, long ago turned these memorable remains of antiquity into heaps of stones.

In one line the book marks the ruins of the Golden Horde's capital: 90 versts along the Akhtuba from Tsaritsyn lies 'the Golden Horde, stone Tatar mosques'. By 1600 the city was a field of ruins, and Muscovite clerks named both the builders (the Horde) and the material (stone).

And along the river Akhtuba, 90 versts from Tsaritsyn, is the Golden Horde, stone Tatar mosques.

CitiesSarai; Astrakhan; Bolgar; Tana (Azov); Kaffa (Feodosia)c. 1405-1410

Sarai, Astrakhan, Bolgar and Kaffa: the cities of Great Tartary

Schiltberger lists the towns he visited 'that belong to Great Tartary': Urgench, Haitzicherchen (Hajji Tarkhan, old Astrakhan), Sarai 'the residence of the kings of the Tartars', Bolgar, Ibissibur, Tana on the Don, Solkhat in Crimea, and Kaffa with its double walls and its Italian, Greek, Armenian and Jewish quarters. So the Horde of his day had real cities as well as camps.

Item, a city called Haitzicherchen, which is a large city, and in a good country. Another city called Sarei ; there, is the residence of the kings of the Tartars. There is also a city called Bolar, in which are different kinds of beasts.

Johann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

About Golden Horde Tartars and their subject towns

Keep in mind

He says he was in these places but gives almost no description of Sarai itself; editor Bruun's notes (p.173) add that its ruins lie on the Akhtuba.

CitiesSarai (lower Volga, Akhtuba)13th century (reported 1843)

Sarai, the lost capital of the Golden Horde

In Saratov a local official showed Haxthausen coins and ornaments dug from the 'supposed ruins' of Sarai. He then retells its history: Batu founded the city, the khans built magnificent palaces, and Russian grand dukes came to kneel there. On the next page he marvels that nobody could yet say for certain where this great capital had stood.

Batu Khan, the Grand Khan of the Golden Horde, who ruled over the half of two hemispheres, founded in the thirteenth century a powerful city, Sarai, and fixed his residence there. He and his successors built palaces of fabulous magnificence. Princes, among others the Grand Dukes of Russia, were obliged to come and bend the knee before their lord and swear fealty to him

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Khans of the Golden Horde (Mongols and Tatars)

Keep in mind

The history is Haxthausen's summary of received accounts, not something he saw; 'palaces of fabulous magnificence' is his phrase, not a description of surviving remains.

Daily lifeÖzbeg Khan's camp, North Caucasus steppe; Sarai1333

Ibn Battuta at Özbeg Khan's court: unveiled, honoured wives

Hammer retells Ibn Battuta's visit. Every Friday Özbeg sat under a golden vault called the golden alcove, on a silver-plated, jewelled throne, with his sons on either side and his daughter before him. Whenever one of his wives came in he rose, took her by the hand and led her to her seat; all were unveiled. Each wife had her own house and household, and Ibn Battuta marvelled at the freedom and standing of women among the Tatars, so unlike Arab and Persian harem seclusion.

Whenever one of his wives came in, he stood up, took her by the hand and led her to her place; they were all unveiled. … Özbeg's wives were highly honoured. Each had her own house and her own household; in general Ibn Battuta wonders at the freedom and esteem which women enjoyed among the Tatars (quite unlike the compulsion of the Arab and Persian harem).

BuildingsTsarev, lower Volga (probably the Golden Horde capital, New Sarai)August 1669

Ruins of an imperial city, its stones carried off to build Astrakhan

Near Tsaritsyn Struys passed the ruins of Czaresgorod, the 'Imperial City', which he says Tamerlane destroyed. It had been built of hard stone, some remains of which could still be seen, but most of it had been carried off to Astrakhan to build that city. This matches the known reuse of brick and stone from the ruined Golden Horde capital, though Struys does not say who built it.

we passed near the ruins of Czaresgorod, or Imperial City, which was destroyed by Tamerlane during the war we spoke of above. It was built of hard stones, of which some remains are still seen, but most were carried to Astrakhan and served to build it.

The nameSarai (lower Volga)1237 to 1240s

Batu's 'Mongol' army had very few Mongols

In footnotes to his account of Batu's conquest of Russia and sack of Kiev in 1240, Klaproth points out that Batu's army contained very few Mongols and that he ruled as prince of the Kipchak Tartars. The khans of Kipchak called themselves Tartar sultans and lived at Sarai on the Akhtuba; after their empire split, the khans of Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea kept the title.

Batu-chan had very few Mongols in his army, and had become prince of the Kipdschak Tartars… The chans of Kipdschak styled themselves Tartar sultans, and resided in the city of Sarai or Saratschik on the river Achtuba; an arm of the Wolga, till the division of their extensive dominions.

Julius Klaproth (his own footnotes, English translation, 1814)

CitiesSarai (Selitrennoye, Astrakhan oblast)14th century to 1395

Sarai: sixty-three years from building to ruin

Ibn Arabshah says Sarai drew so many scholars, men of letters and wits in a short time that no other city could match it, not even the great mosque of Cairo and its villages. He puts the whole life of the city, from its building to its ruin, at sixty-three years, and calls it one of the greatest and most populous of cities.

So Sarai, through these masters, became a gathering place of learning and a mine of good fortune … Between the building of Sarai and its ruin there were sixty-three years; and it was one of the greatest of cities in layout and the most crowded with people.

CitiesTsarev (Tsarevy Pody), Volgograd Oblast1793

A royal valley of tombs, not houses

Local tradition said the khan's palace had stood at Tsarevy Pody. Pallas disagreed: the mounds and brick vaults were, in his view, tombs of Mongol Tartar princes, mosques and walled cemeteries. A wandering people like the Golden Horde, he argues, would no more live in houses than the Kalmyk khans who refused the houses built for them at Yenotaevsk. (Most historians now identify Tsarev with New Sarai, a real city of the Horde, so here the naturalist's judgement went too far.)

In my opinion, the ruins are not the remains of dwelling-houses, but partly of mosques, and partly of vaulted chapels, … A wandering nation, such as were called the golden horde of these countries, could no more be induced to reside in houses, than the Khans and Princes of the Kalmuks along the banks of the Volga;

Peter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

About the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

This is Pallas's interpretation; later excavation showed the Golden Horde capitals had real houses, workshops and streets.

CitiesSelitrennoye, Astrakhan OblastAugust 1823

The ruins of a great Tatar city on the Akhtuba

On the way home the missionaries stopped at the 'saltpetre town', a Russian cordon post among ruins that stretched far along the Akhtuba, a navigable arm of the Volga. Zwick could see the city had once been large and splendid but could not learn its old name. Just before, he says these Tartars were subdued by Russia 'in 1562' and their lands laid waste from Kazan to Astrakhan (Kazan actually fell in 1552 and Astrakhan in 1556).

At noon we arrived at Selitrenoi Gorodok, (which the Tartars call Tschigit) where we stayed till noon the following day. This ancient Tartarian city must, as it is evident from the remains, have been remarkable for its size and splendour; its ancient name I have not been able to learn. … The ruins of the city extend far and wide along the shore of the river.

Heinrich August Zwick (English edition, 1831)

About Golden Horde Tatars

Keep in mind

Modern archaeologists identify the Selitrennoye ruins as Sarai (often called Sarai-Batu), a capital of the Golden Horde; Zwick did not know this.

Daily lifeLower Volga (Sarai region)1666

Horsemeat, mare's milk and 'no lies' among the Sarai Tatars

On the Tatars around old Sarai, Evliya writes that they love horseflesh above all, some boiling it and some eating it raw with the blood running, and drink boza, kumiss (mare's milk) and camel's milk. He claims they know no backbiting, slander or lies, since they fear no one. All these Tatars are desert-dwellers who move with their camel-carts, and they became Muslims (Hanafi and Shafi'i) after Chinggis Khan.

What all of them love most is horse meat: many tribes cook horse meat, and many tribes eat the meat raw with the blood running. Instead of water they drink boza, mare's-milk kumiss and camel's milk … and all the Tatars are desert-dwellers; they mount their camel-carts and migrate.

Daily lifeGolden Horde (Crimea or Sarai)c.1330 to 1340

The Lord's Prayer in the language of the Horde

The German Franciscan part of the Codex contains Christian texts turned into Cuman for preaching. This is the start of the Lord's Prayer: 'Our father who art in heaven, blessed be thy kingdom, thy will be done as in heaven so on earth, give us this day our daily bread'. Elsewhere in the Codex the word for God is 'Tengri', the old Turkic name of the sky god, as Kuun's word index shows.

Our father who art in heaven. Blessed be thy kingdom (khanate). Thy will be done, as in heaven so on earth. Give us today our daily bread.

Original wording

Atamis kim köctä sen. Algiszle bulsun senig hanlechin. bulsû senig tilemegin nezikkim kocta alley ierda. kundegi ötmackimisni bisga bugun bergil.

Codex Cumanicus, German (Franciscan) part, c.1330 to 1340, ed. Geza Kuun 1880

BuildingsSelitrennoye, Astrakhan OblastAugust 1823

Russian towers built from Tatar rubble

The few towers still standing among the ruins were not Tatar work at all, Zwick decided, but early Russian forts. His reasons are practical: the towers are held together with clay, while the Tatar buildings used a hard mortar, and they were patched together from broken pieces of fine older buildings, with glazed tiles stuck in any which way.

In the first place, they are built with clay cement, whereas the Tartars used a durable mortar; in the next place, they are evidently piled up from the ruins of handsome buildings, as may be seen by the glazed tiles which are stuck on at random, without any respect to the glazed side.

Heinrich August Zwick (English edition, 1831)
