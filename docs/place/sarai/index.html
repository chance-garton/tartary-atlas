Glazed tiles and mosaic on a khan's tomb

Pallas describes the finest building at the Selitrennoye ruins as a khan's family mausoleum with a prayer house above, its foundation walls two fathoms high. The brickwork was the most elegant he had seen among any Tartar ruins, set with glazed tiles in green, yellow, white and blue, with stucco flowers, shell-work and mosaic panels. He adds that whole cargoes of its bricks had been carried off to build Astrakhan.

In the whole brickwork, which consists of beautiful broad squares, disposed in the most regular manner, there is a degree of taste and elegance of which I have no where seen an instance among the ruins of the Tartars. Sarai: a new city of merchants and the Turkish slave trade

Abu'l-Fida's entry on Seray (Sarai), capital of 'the states of Berke', says the present ruler is Uzbek, that the city lies in a plain on the Volga north-west of the Caspian, and that merchants crowd there and do a great trade in Turkish slaves. He calls it a modern city, founded by a descendant of Chinggis Khan named Sain Batu, and adds day-length information from a man of the city.

The prince now reigning is called Uzbek. Sarai lies in a plain, north-west of the Caspian Sea … It is a place much visited by merchants, and there is a great trade there in Turkish slaves. The town is of recent origin; its founding is attributed to a descendant of Chinggis Khan called Sain Batu.

BuildingsTsarev (Tsarevy Pody), Volgograd Oblast1793

Mortar harder than the bricks

At Tsarevy Pody ('the royal residence') on the Akhtuba, Pallas examined a flat vaulted tomb mound inside a sandstone wall. The vaults were of six layers of square bricks joined with a grey cement so hard that the bricks broke before the mortar did. He analysed it as lime, powdered charcoal and ground sandstone, and wondered whether sour milk, plentiful among a pastoral people, had been mixed in.

It has, however, acquired such a solid consistence, that it is easier to break the well-burnt bricks, than to separate the mortar. This grey cement appears to be a mixture of unslacked lime, pulverized charcoal, and pounded sandstone, instead of the sand used for building.

Who Mongol Tartar princes of the Golden Horde

Saw itPeter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

Keep in mind. The sour-milk idea on the next page is his own guess.

BuildingsSelitrennoye1773

Glazed tiles and bricks carted off to Astrakhan

Pallas says he had never seen such splendour in any Tatar ruin: brick walls laid with great regularity, the joints inlaid with green, yellow, white and blue glazed ornaments, and a facade with mosaic tiles. He blames time and plunder for the damage, adding that whole boatloads of bricks had been shipped to Astrakhan, and that to get a thousand whole bricks the workers smashed two thousand because the mortar was so hard.

The outer face of the walls is not only inlaid, panel by panel in all the joints, with glazed clay ornaments of green, yellow, white and blue colour in triangular and other figures… But time, and still more the greed of the rabble, long ago turned these memorable remains of antiquity into heaps of stones.

Sarai, Astrakhan, Bolgar and Kaffa: the cities of Great Tartary

Schiltberger lists the towns he visited 'that belong to Great Tartary': Urgench, Haitzicherchen (Hajji Tarkhan, old Astrakhan), Sarai 'the residence of the kings of the Tartars', Bolgar, Ibissibur, Tana on the Don, Solkhat in Crimea, and Kaffa with its double walls and its Italian, Greek, Armenian and Jewish quarters. Sarai, the lost capital of the Golden Horde

In Saratov a local official showed Haxthausen coins and ornaments dug from the 'supposed ruins' of Sarai. He then retells its history: Batu founded the city, the khans built magnificent palaces, and Russian grand dukes came to kneel there. On the next page he marvels that nobody could yet say for certain where this great capital had stood.

Batu Khan, the Grand Khan of the Golden Horde, who ruled over the half of two hemispheres, founded in the thirteenth century a powerful city, Sarai, and fixed his residence there. He and his successors built palaces of fabulous magnificence. Princes, among others the Grand Dukes of Russia, were obliged to come and bend the knee before their lord and swear fealty to him

Ruins of an imperial city, its stones carried off to build Astrakhan

Near Tsaritsyn Struys passed the ruins of Czaresgorod, the 'Imperial City', which he says Tamerlane destroyed. It had been built of hard stone, some remains of which could still be seen, but most of it had been carried off to Astrakhan to build that city. This matches the known reuse of brick and stone from the ruined Golden Horde capital, though Struys does not say who built it.

we passed near the ruins of Czaresgorod, or Imperial City, which was destroyed by Tamerlane during the war we spoke of above. It was built of hard stones, of which some remains are still seen, but most were carried to Astrakhan and served to build it.

Batu's 'Mongol' army had very few Mongols

In footnotes to his account of Batu's conquest of Russia and sack of Kiev in 1240, Klaproth points out that Batu's army contained very few Mongols and that he ruled as prince of the Kipchak Tartars. The khans of Kipchak called themselves Tartar sultans and lived at Sarai on the Akhtuba; after their empire split, the khans of Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea kept the title.

Batu-chan had very few Mongols in his army, and had become prince of the Kipdschak Tartars… The chans of Kipdschak styled themselves Tartar sultans, and resided in the city of Sarai or Saratschik on the river Achtuba; an arm of the Wolga, till the division of their extensive dominions.

A royal valley of tombs, not houses

Local tradition said the khan's palace had stood at Tsarevy Pody. Pallas disagreed: the mounds and brick vaults were, in his view, tombs of Mongol Tartar princes, mosques and walled cemeteries. A wandering people like the Golden Horde, he argues, would no more live in houses than the Kalmyk khans who refused the houses built for them at Yenotaevsk. (Most historians now identify Tsarev with New Sarai, a real city of the Horde, so here the naturalist's judgement went too far.)

In my opinion, the ruins are not the remains of dwelling-houses, but partly of mosques, and partly of vaulted chapels, … A wandering nation, such as were called the golden horde of these countries, could no more be induced to reside in houses, than the Khans and Princes of the Kalmuks along the banks of the Volga;

Horsemeat, mare's milk and 'no lies' among the Sarai Tatars

On the Tatars around old Sarai, Evliya writes that they love horseflesh above all, some boiling it and some eating it raw with the blood running, and drink boza, kumiss (mare's milk) and camel's milk. He claims they know no backbiting, slander or lies, since they fear no one. All these Tatars are desert-dwellers who move with their camel-carts, and they became Muslims (Hanafi and Shafi'i) after Chinggis Khan.

What all of them love most is horse meat: many tribes cook horse meat, and many tribes eat the meat raw with the blood running. Instead of water they drink boza, mare's-milk kumiss and camel's milk … and all the Tatars are desert-dwellers; they mount their camel-carts and migrate.

BuildingsSelitrennoye, Astrakhan OblastAugust 1823

Russian towers built from Tatar rubble

The few towers still standing among the ruins were not Tatar work at all, Zwick decided, but early Russian forts. His reasons are practical: the towers are held together with clay, while the Tatar buildings used a hard mortar, and they were patched together from broken pieces of fine older buildings, with glazed tiles stuck in any which way.

In the first place, they are built with clay cement, whereas the Tartars used a durable mortar; in the next place, they are evidently piled up from the ruins of handsome buildings, as may be seen by the glazed tiles which are stuck on at random, without any respect to the glazed side.

Who Golden Horde Tatars; early Russian settlers

Saw itHeinrich August Zwick (English edition, 1831)

Keep in mind. The judgement that the towers are Russian is Zwick's own reading of the masonry.

A friar goes to Sarai to learn the language

Kuun cites the Franciscan Pascal of Vitoria, who in 1337 went to the 'ancient city of Sarai on the bank of the Volga' to learn Turkish among the Cumans and studied it there for a whole year. Sarai was the capital of the Golden Horde, and the story shows missionaries living in the Horde's own city.

Friar Pascal of Vitoria in 1337, as he himself says, hurried to the Cumans in the ancient city of Sarai on the bank of the Volga in order to learn the Turkish language, and there worked as hard as he could at the study of the language for one whole year.

Sarai, once the Horde's capital, now a heap of stones

A footnote to Nikitin's route explains that Sarai, on the Akhtuba branch of the Volga, had been the capital of the Golden Horde, where Russian grand dukes went for two centuries to pay tribute. By the nineteenth century it was reduced to ruins. The note is a useful reminder that the Horde did have a real capital city, and that it was well known to Russian historians.

Sarai, now a heap of stones and the abode of loathsome reptiles, still observable on the borders of the Akhtouba, an arm of the Volga, forty-six miles from the sea, was at that time the capital of the great or Golden Orda, the famous residence of the great conqueror Baty, the place where, during two centuries, the Grand-Dukes of Russia had to pay their allegiance and their tribute
