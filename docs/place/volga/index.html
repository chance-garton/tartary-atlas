A Kalmyk prince with a wooden castle and a piano

Not every Kalmyk lord lived in a tent. Prince Sered-Dscheh, a colonel in the Russian army, had a large wooden castle on the left bank of the Volga, fortified with Russian-style outworks after the Napoleonic wars and furnished with mahogany, mirrors, clocks, a billiard table and a piano. Even so, he held court out on the steppe for part of the summer.

He resides on the left bank of the Volga, in a large wooden castle, thirty paces in front, which he fortified with Russian outworks when he returned from the French war … His castle is well fitted up in the interior, and contains a number of saloons, with mahogany furniture, lustres, mirrors, a billiard table, piano-forte, a number of clocks, &c.

The nameVolga region, Kazan, Astrakhan, Crimea13th to 15th century (argued 1828)

How Mongol conquerors became the Turkic 'Tatars' of Kazan and Crimea

Bichurin explains how the name moved: the Mongol lords on the Volga were few among Turkic subjects, married into them, took their language, customs and Islam, and so disappeared, reappearing under the Russian name 'Tatars' as a seemingly new people ruling Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea. A footnote adds that the Tatars do not call themselves by that name but Uzbeks, Nogais and so on.

The conquerors, taking from the Turkestanis through marriage their manners and customs, their language and religion, little by little were completely lost, so that at last the very name of the Tatans disappeared, and under the Russian name of Tatars they became as it were a new people, which afterwards ruled in the tsardoms of Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea.

Batu's camp looked like a great city

Arriving at Batu's camp on the Volga, Rubruck was amazed: the ordu spread out like a city for three or four leagues, with every family knowing its place. He explains that orda means 'middle', because the court stands in the middle of the people, with its doors opening south. This is the moving 'city' of the steppe, the ancestor of the word 'horde'.

When I saw the ordu of Baatu, I was astonished, for it seemed like a great city stretched out about his dwelling, with people scattered all about for three or four leagues.

A Jewish king ruling Muslims, Christians and pagans

The author describes Atil's four gates and a king who is Jewish, with judges from each faith: Muslim, Jewish, Christian and pagan. Most inhabitants were Muslims and Christians, and the leading citizens were Muslim merchants. On the same page he notes that their language resembles that of the Turks, glossed by the translator as 'Tartars'.

The king of this country is a Jew : he has in his train four thousand Mussulmans, and Khozrians (Christians), and Idolaters ; but his principal people are Jews … The principal persons of Atel are Mussulmans and merchants: their language is like that of the Turks (or Tartars), and is not understood by any other nation.

Sarai, the Horde's capital, and its ruins on the Akhtuba

In his survey of the towns of Kipchak, Hammer places Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, on the Akhtuba channel of the Volga, where ruins stretched forty versts into the steppe and at least ten versts along the river. He takes from the Persian historian Wassaf that Batu founded it, and from coins that there were several Sarais: the old and the new (the second only an extension of the first), with Gülistan apparently a suburb, and a third called Sali Serai. Elsewhere (p. 140 to 141) he says Batu built Sarai in his last years, as he had earlier built Kazan.

Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans of the Golden Horde, whose ruins [lie] forty versts into the steppe from the bank of the Volga and at least ten versts along the Akhtuba … There were, as the coins show, three Sarais: the old and the new, the latter being only an extension of the old, just as Gülistan, which often appears on coins, seems to have been only a suburb of the residence Sarai

Bow to the fire and the idols

When Mikhail of Chernigov came to Batu's camp, Batu ordered him to bow to fire and to 'their idols'. Mikhail refused, mocked the idols as deaf, and was killed. The episode matches western reports that visitors had to pass between fires and honour Mongol felt images, and it made Mikhail a Russian saint.

How a Kalmyk kibitka is put up

Camped in bitter cold above Astrakhan, Bruce's detachment was lent sixty kibitkas by the Kalmyk prince Dasan. He describes them closely: 24 feet across, folding lattice walls six feet high, a round wooden crown raised on lances with holes for the rafters, and a felt covering laid from the bottom up. He found them warmer and more weatherproof than a house.

they are all round, the sides being made of a kind of checkered wicker-work, and the cross sticks neatly jointed for folding together or extending … they raise with their lances a round board, three feet diameter, with a hole in the middle of it … the large hole serves for their chimney, the small holes receive the ends of so many strait rafters

Daily lifeBolgar, Tatarstan (middle Volga)late 9th century

Muslim farmers of the Volga Bulghars

The Bulghars (written Bulkar) lived on the Itil (Volga), between the Khazars and the Slavs; their king was a Muslim. Ibn Rusta says the Khazars and the Rus came to trade with them in sable, ermine and squirrel, and that they farmed wheat, barley and millet, and that most of them were Muslims with mosques and schools. Their land around Bolgar later became part of the Golden Horde and then the Kazan khanate.

They are a people who have crops and ploughing; they sow every kind of grain, wheat, barley, millet and so on. Most of them profess the religion of Islam; in their settlements there are mosques and Quran schools, and they have muezzins and imams.

A ruined brick city 'built by Tamerlane', quarried for Astrakhan

Sailing down the Volga, Bruce passed the ruins of a great city called Czarefgorod. He reports the local belief that Tamerlane built it, says its palace and walls were brick, and notes that the Russians had been carting the bricks away for years to build Astrakhan's walls, churches and monasteries. It was most likely one of the Golden Horde's Volga towns.

About forty miles from this, and at a small distance from the river-side, are to be seen the ruins of a great city, formerly called Czaresgorod, built, as is related, by Tamerlane : its palace and walls were all of brick, and have served the city of Astrachan with materials, these many years, for building their walls, churches, and monasteries.

But it is certainly a saltpetre kettle belonging to the Crown.

How a Kalmyk felt tent is put together

Pallas gives a step-by-step account of the Kalmyk tent, keyed to an engraved plate: folding lattice walls of willow rods tied into a circle, a wooden crown ring lifted on long roof rods, the frame painted red, and a large cut felt thrown over the roof like a raincoat. In summer the sides stay open; in cold weather they are wrapped in felt or reed mats.

Then the frame, whose parts are usually painted red, is finished. Over its roof a large felt, cut to fit, is laid round like a raincoat and fastened with ropes slung over it. The sides stay open in summer, but when it is cold pieces of felt or reed mats, or both, are laid around them.

BuildingsAkhtuba River1773

Tatar tents that ride on two-wheeled carts

Along the Akhtuba Pallas saw the camps of the Kundurov Tatars, Nogais from the Kuban who had stayed behind when many Kalmyks fled east in 1771. Unlike Kalmyk tents, their round felt-and-reed huts could not be taken apart and were small enough to set whole onto a tall two-wheeled cart (arba), which they covered like a parasol. He compares them to the wagon-dwelling 'Hamaxobii' of the ancient geographers and quotes Horace on the Scythians whose carts drag their wandering homes.

Their yurts are very different in shape and construction from those used by the Kalmyks and other Asian nomads. They cannot be taken apart piece by piece, but are all the more lightly built and only so large that they can be set over a big cart.

BuildingsAtil (Khazar capital, lower Volga)10th century

Atil: a capital of tents with one brick palace

The Khazar capital on the Volga is described as mostly tents. Only a few market halls and bathhouses were of clay, and the king's residence was the only burnt-brick building in the country, because nobody else was allowed to build in brick. It is a precise picture of a steppe capital before the Mongols, on the same river where Sarai and Astrakhan later stood.

Here are many tents ; and in this country there are but a few edifices of clay, such as bazars (market-places) and bathing houses. … it is constructed of burnt bricks ; and this is the only building of such materials in all the country : they will not allow any body but the king to erect such a dwelling.

Daily lifeDon and Volga steppe (Astrakhan campaign)1569

Crimean guides who led a Turkish army to starve

Asked how the Muscovites had inflicted a great defeat on the Turks when a river diversion was attempted, Possevino's informant said it was not the Muscovites but the Crimean Tatars who did it. Though allies of the Turks, they feared a heavier yoke if the scheme succeeded, so they offered themselves as guides and led the Turkish troops through vast forests and foodless country until nearly all died of hunger and toil.

... the Muscovite had inflicted [so great a defeat on the Turks], when he had ordered the Don to be diverted into the Borysthenes: he answered that this was done not by the Muscovites but by the Precop Tartars, though allies of the Turks, who resented the scheme because they foresaw a heavier yoke hanging over them. So, offering themselves as guides to the Turkish forces, they led them round through huge forests and places without supplies, so that almost all of them perished of hunger and toil.
