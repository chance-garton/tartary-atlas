CitiesSoltaniyeh, Iran1673

Sultaniyeh, a royal city founded by Hulagu's descendants

Passing Sultaniyeh, Chardin saw fine public buildings, about 3,000 houses and vast ruins of churches, mosques and towers stretching west. Some said no one knew its founder; others said Arghun Khan, grandson of Hulagu, laid its foundations and his son finished it and named it 'the Royal City'. He adds on the same page that it was later wrecked by Tamerlane and by other Turkish and Tartar princes.

Others on the other side affirm, that the foundations of it were laid when the Sun was in Leo, by the order, and in the Reign of Ergon Can, the Son of Abkei-Can, and Grand-child of Halacou-Can; and that because it could not be finish'd in his days, his Son Jangou-Sultan compleated the work and call'd it Sultania, or the Royal City.

Who Mongol Ilkhans (Tartar khans of Persia)

Saw itJean Chardin (English translation, London, 1691)

Keep in mind. Chardin gives competing traditions about the founder; he saw the ruins himself.

Strange talesDamghan1404 (towers seen); event a few years earlier

Towers built of the heads of 'white Tartars'

Outside Damghan the envoys saw towers of mud and human heads. They were told these were the heads of 'white Tartars', nomads from between Turkey and Syria whom Timur had deported to Damghan; when they tried to ride home, plundering as they went, his army killed them, and Timur ordered every white Tartar found to be put to death. Locals said lights burned on the towers at night.

These towers, which are made of heads, are built of the heads of a race of people called "white Tartars," natives of a country between Turkey and Syria. … The people of the city say, that they often see lights burning on the top of these towers in the night.

Who 'White Tartars', nomads deported by Timur

Saw itRuy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)

Keep in mind. The towers were seen; the story, the figure of 60,000 dead and the lights at night were told to the envoys.

Daily lifeChina and Persiac. 1290s

The 'genuine Tartars' are going native

After praising Tartar warfare, Polo adds a striking remark: in his day the Mongols were changing. Those settled in China had taken up the religion and ways of the Chinese, and those in the Middle East had adopted Muslim customs. It is an eyewitness observation that the conquerors were being absorbed by the settled peoples they ruled.

But I must add also that in these days they are greatly degenerated ; for those who are settled in Cathay have taken up the practices of the Idolaters of the country, and have abandoned their own institutions ; whilst those who have settled in the Levant have adopted the customs of the Saracens.

Who Mongols settled in China and in the Ilkhanate

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Keep in mind. 'Idolaters' means Buddhists and followers of Chinese religion; 'Saracens' means Muslims.

BuildingsCaucasus and Persia (Mongol army lands)1247

They always live in tents, not villages or cities

In a chapter on the Tartars' outward appearance and way of life, the text says plainly that they always live in tents and never in villages or cities, because they are country herdsmen with vast flocks of sheep and goats, herds of cattle, camels and horses. They winter on the plains and follow the good pasture into the mountains in summer. The same chapter describes their bull-like singing of 'Alai alai'.

They always live in tents, and not in villages or cities. For they are country herdsmen of livestock, and abound in flocks of sheep and goats and in herds of cattle, camels and horses. In winter they are accustomed to stay on the plain; in summer they follow the richness of the pastures in the mountains.

Daily lifeSojas, Zanjan province, Iran1291 and after

A khan's grave hidden in a sacred mountain

Mustawfi records that the Ilkhan Arghun was buried on the mountain of Sujas and that, following Mongol custom, the grave was concealed and the whole mountain made a forbidden sanctuary (qurugh). His daughter later revealed the grave and founded a dervish lodge there, a Muslim practice laid over a steppe one.

The sepulchre of Arghun Khan was made in the mountain of Sujas, and according to Mongol custom, they concealed the place, making the whole mountain an inviolable Sanctuary (Qurugh), so that people could not without difficulty pass that way. Arghun's daughter, Uljay Khatun, however, made manifest her father's grave, founding a Darvish-house, and settling a community here.

Who Mongols (Ilkhan Arghun and his daughter)

Saw itHamdallah Mustawfi (translated by G. Le Strange, 1919)

Strange talesAlamut region, Iran1256

The Assassins' paradise

Describing the Ismaili state the Mongols destroyed in 1256, Chang De repeats the story that its soldiers were all assassins, trained by being drugged, shown music and women in a hidden chamber, and told they would enjoy such bliss after death. It is the Chinese version of the 'Old Man of the Mountain' legend that Marco Polo tells, and it is hearsay.

The soldiers of that country are all assassins. Their custom is that when they see a brave, strong man, they lure him with gain and make him kill his own father or elder brother with his own hand; then he is enrolled as a soldier. They make him drunk, carry him into a cave chamber and entertain him with music and beautiful women, indulging his desires for several days, then put him back where he was. When he wakes, they ask what he saw and teach him that if he can become an assassin, at death he will enjoy bliss like that.

Original wording

其國兵皆刺客俗見男子勇壯者以利誘之令手刄父兄然後充兵醉酒扶入窟室娱以音樂羙女縱其慾數日復置故處既醒問其所見教之能爲刺客死則享福如此

Who Ismailis (Mulahida), conquered by Hulegu's Mongols

Legend or rumourLiu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)

Keep in mind. A hostile legend about the Ismailis, told by their conquerors; not an eyewitness account.

BuildingsSoltaniyeh, Iranc.1290 to 1316

A Mongol capital and a tomb of cut stone

Mustawfi credits the founding of Sultaniyyah to the Mongol Ilkhan Arghun and its completion to his son Uljaytu, who named it after himself. He gives the circuit of the walls built and planned, and describes a castle of cut stone at the centre that is Uljaytu's tomb. Elsewhere on the page he says the new capital drew settlers of all nations and that only Tabriz had as many great buildings.

The city was founded by Arghun Khan, grandson of Hulagu the Mongol, and his son Uljaytu Sultan completed it, naming it Sultaniyyah after himself … The walls which Arghun built were 12,000 paces in circuit … In the centre stands a Castle built of cut stone, which is the tomb of Uljaytu Sultan

Who Mongols (Ilkhans Arghun and Uljaytu)

Saw itHamdallah Mustawfi (translated by G. Le Strange, 1919)

Keep in mind. Mustawfi served the dynasty and knew the city well; the 'castle' is the great domed mausoleum still standing today.

Daily lifeIsfahan, Iran, and Khiva, Uzbekistan1630s (told about 1670s)

A captive Tartar prince who galloped home from Isfahan

Chardin tells the court story of 'Abouel-Kazi', a prince of Khwarazm captured in a Persian victory over the Uzbeks. Shah Safi treated him as royalty in Isfahan for ten years, until he feigned illness, went to a spa, and rode off with twenty Uzbek horsemen sent to fetch him. He became khan and stayed a friend of Persia. This is Abu'l-Ghazi Bahadur, khan of Khiva, later author of a famous history of the Turks.

To whom the Yusbeck Prince thus briskly answer'd; I am highly oblig'd to the King of Persia, but I owe more my own Country and Relations that call me back. 'Tis my Father that sends me these Men, to facilitate my return to my Native soil

Who Uzbeks of Khwarazm (Khiva)

Heard itJean Chardin, 'The Coronation of Solyman III' (appended to the 1691 English edition)

Keep in mind. Court gossip retold decades later; details such as the role of the prince's father may not match other accounts.

BuildingsGorgan plain, northeastern Iran1822

How a Turkmen tent is built

Fraser says no one had yet described exactly how the 'portable wooden houses' of the Turkmens are made, and does so: a folding lattice of thin laths pinned with rawhide that opens out like a child's toy, curved roof poles, black felt (numud) coverings, a reed screen outside and a broad woven band holding it all together.

The frame is curiously constructed of light wood, disposed in laths of about an inch broad, by three quarters thick, crossing one another diagonally … and pinned at each crossing with thongs of raw hide, so as to be moveable ; … over which is thrown a covering of black numud, leaving in the center a large hole to give vent to the smoke, and light to the dwelling

Who Goklen and Yomut Turkmens

Saw itJames Baillie Fraser

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

A horse on a pole for the dead lord

Riccoldo describes Tartar funerals: cooked meat, clothes and money buried with the dead, and for a great lord a horse ridden to exhaustion, killed, stuffed with green grass and hung up on a stake, told to be ready whenever its master rises again. He adds that they once buried up to twenty living slaves with a lord until Christians shamed them out of it, and that Tartars even paid bishops to be buried in Christian cemeteries.

and the horse falls, and he guts it and empties everything from the horse's belly and fills it with green grass, and then drives a great stake in from behind and makes the stake come out at the mouth, and so leaves the horse impaled, and hangs it up and orders it to be ready whenever its lord wishes to rise

et equus cadit, et ipse exenterat eum et euacuat omnia de ventre equi, et implet herba viridi, et postea infigit palum magnum per posteriora, et facit palum exire vsque ad os, et ita dimittit equum impalatum, et suspendit eum et mandat ei, quod sit paratus, quandocumque wlt dominus surgere

Who Tartars (Mongols of the Ilkhanate)

Daily lifeGorgan valley, Iran (Turkmen country)1832

Turkmen roadside grave mounds

Riding with a sayyid near the Caspian, Burnes asked about mounds of earth with a pole in the centre. They were 'yoozka', marking where someone had died or been laid out, and Turkmen said a blessing as they passed. Burnes remarks that they were not graves but barrows raised in honour of the dead, and suspects the custom was 'Tatar'.

They are not graves, but mounds or barrows raised in honour of the dead. I entertain an impression that the usage is Tatar; but I had no opportunity of further investigation.

Who Turkmen

Saw itAlexander Burnes (1834)

Keep in mind. Burnes's 'Tatar' origin is an untested hunch, as he admits.

Strange talesIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

A shaman who seemed to fly

Riccoldo says the Tartars honour above all men certain 'boxitae' (bakhshis), priests of idols from India, wise and grave, who practise magic with the help of demons, show many marvels and foretell the future. One of their leaders was said to fly, but, Riccoldo reports, it was found that he only walked just above the ground without touching it and sat on nothing solid.

For one of the greater among them was said to fly, but according to the truth it was found that he did not fly, but walked close to the ground without touching it, and when he seemed to sit he was held up by nothing solid.

Nam et vnus maior inter eos dicebatur volare, sed secundum veritatem repertum est, quod non volauit, sed ambulabat iuxta terram et eam non tangebat, et quando videbatur sedere, nulla re solida sustentabatur.

Who Tartars and their 'baxitae' (Buddhist or shamanic priests)

CitiesRey, near Tehran, Iran1673

Rey, 'the Market of the Universe', brought to nothing

Near Saveh Chardin passed the scattered traces of Rey, once, the Persian histories said, the greatest city in Asia, with incredible numbers of streets, baths and mosques. He blames its fall on sectarian wars together with Tartar incursions, and notes that a later prince tried to rebuild it after making peace with 'Cazan-Can, King of Persia, of the Race of the Tartars' (the Ilkhan Ghazan), but failed.

These Wars, together with the Incursions of the Tartars, destroy'd the Potent Rey, and reduc'd it to nothing before the end of the sixth Age of the Mahumetan Epoche.

Who Tartars (Mongols) and warring Muslim factions

Read itJean Chardin (English translation, London, 1691)

Keep in mind. Taken from Persian and Arabic histories; the huge figures for Rey on the previous page Chardin himself doubts.

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

Execution called 'sleep', and a blank space for the lord's name

Two sharp details of Mongol court manners. When writing to a superior, a Tartar leaves a gap where the lord's name belongs and writes the name out in the margin. When a man is condemned, the lord says 'cut him and make him sleep', because they do not call death death, and the condemned simply holds out his arms to be bound.

When they write to their superiors they keep such reverence that they do not write their superior's name in its place, but leave an empty space and afterwards write the name outside, in the margin, in another place.

Working translation

Original wording

Cum scribunt suis maioribus, tantam reuerenciam habent et seruant, quod non scribant nomen sui maioris in suo loco, sed dimittunt spacium vacuum et postea scribunt nomen de foris in margine in alio loco.

Who Tartars (Mongols of the Ilkhanate)

Saw itRiccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1873)

'The Tatars are exceedingly numerous'

When a large Mongol army under Chormaghun crossed the Oxus, Sultan Jalal al-Din of Khwarazm, though on bad terms with the caliph and the rulers of Anatolia and Syria, sent envoys to each of them. Rashid writes 'Mughul' (Mongol) in his own voice, but gives the substance of the warning as 'the Tatars are exceedingly numerous'. The same army is Mongol in the narrative and Tatar in the alarm.

He sent an envoy to each of them and gave news of the Mongols' coming; the substance of the message was that the Tatars are exceedingly numerous, and this time more than ever before.

Strange talesIran (Ilkhanate)c. 1280s

A 'magician' who dug up a jewel and won the Ilkhan's trust

In a footnote from the Syriac chronicler Bar Hebraeus, d'Ohsson tells of Abd al-Rahman, a slave's son who escaped the sack of Baghdad, worked as a carpenter in Mosul, and then claimed spirits had taught him magic. Taken to the Mongol ruler Abaqa, he asked to be brought to the treasure castle of Tala, paced the ground, pointed to a spot, and there a ring with a precious stone was dug up. From then on he was believed to command demons, and under Ahmad he ran the whole kingdom.

he began to measure the ground this way and that; then he pointed to a spot where they should dig, and himself stood some distance away. Digging, they found a ring set with a stone of great value; it was taken to Abaqa. This having confirmed the words of the so-called magician, everything he said was believed

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1260 to 1300

Why the Tartars became Muslims

Riccoldo explains the Ilkhanate's conversion to Islam from a missionary's frustrated point of view: the Tartars had neither law nor prophet, found Islam an easy law, and Muslims gave them rich gifts to convert, so that now 'for the most part' they have become Saracens. He adds that many still like Christians but find Christianity too hard and are offered no presents.

they took up the law and became Saracens, above all because the Tartars had neither law nor prophet. So now for the greater part the Tartars have become Saracens. For the Saracens buy them and give them very great gifts so that they will become Saracens.

Working translation

Original wording

acceperunt legem, et facti sunt Sarraceni, maxime quia ipsi Tartari nec legem nec prophetam habebant. Vnde modo pro magna parte Tartari facti sunt Sarraceni. Nam et Sarraceni eos emunt et dant eis maxima dona, vt ipsi efficiantur Sarraceni.

Who Tartars (Mongols of the Ilkhanate)

Saw itRiccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Keep in mind. A partisan explanation; the Ilkhan Ghazan converted in 1295, within Riccoldo's stay.

Daily lifePersiac. 1320s

Licking fingers and wiping hands on their shoes

After describing how Persians eat sitting on the ground, several from one dish, Jordanus says they lick their fingers instead of using a napkin and wipe the leftover grease on their shoes. The Tartars, western and eastern, do exactly the same, he adds; only the Indians eat cleanly, though also with their hands. A fastidious European's table report.

they lick their fingers with tongue and lips and clean them instead of with a napkin, and afterwards, if any grease is still left on their hands, they wipe them on their shoes; and so do the western and eastern Tartars in everything, except the Indians, who alone eat quite cleanly, though with their hands.

Original wording

ligant digitos cum linguâ et labris, et mundant loco manutegnii, et posteà, si adhuc superest de pinguedine in manibus, mundant eas super calceos suos; et sic faciunt, per omnia, Tartari occidentales atque orientales, Indis exceptis, qui soli comedunt, licèt cum manibus, satis mundè.

Who Persians and Tartars, western and eastern

Saw itJordanus Catalani of Séverac, O.P. (Latin text, ed. Coquebert de Montbret, Recueil de voyages IV, Paris 1839)

Keep in mind. 'ligant' is the printed spelling (for lingunt, 'lick').
