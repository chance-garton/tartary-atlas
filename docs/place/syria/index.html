Daily lifeNear Palmyra and Hama, SyriaApril 1303

'They made a rampart of their saddles'

Reinaud quotes Abu'l-Fida's own chronicle of a skirmish before the battle of Marj al-Suffar in 1303, in which Abu'l-Fida fought. A group of Tartars who had dismounted were surrounded and offered their lives; they answered with arrows, made a barricade of their horses' saddles, and fought until every one was killed. It is an eyewitness picture of Mongol soldiers from the other side.

Some of the Tartars had dismounted; we surrounded them and offered them their lives, but they answered our offer with arrows, and, making a rampart of their horses' saddles, fought a fresh battle. They were all killed to the last man.

BuildingsDamascus to Samarkand1401

The craftsmen of Damascus marched off to Samarkand

When Timur took Damascus in 1401, Ibn Arabshah says he carried off its learned men and craftsmen, 'everyone skilled in any art': weavers, tailors, stone-cutters, carpenters, cap-makers, farriers, tent-makers, painters and decorators, bow-makers and falconers. They were shared out among the army commanders, who were ordered to deliver them to Samarkand. Ibn Arabshah, then a boy, was himself taken from Damascus to Samarkand.

He took from Damascus the men of learning and the craftsmen, everyone skilled and outstanding in any art: weavers and tailors, stone-cutters and carpenters, cap-makers and farriers, tent-makers, painters and bow-makers, and falconers; in short, the people of every craft whatever … He divided these groups among the heads of the army and ordered them to deliver them to Samarkand.

BuildingsDamascus1400

'Their houses are only of cords and canvas'

As Timur approached Syria, the Mamluk governors rejected advice to make peace. Yazdi gives them a proud speech: other kingdoms fell because their towns were mud and brick, but Syria's fortresses are cut from solid rock. The Tartars, they sneer, live in homes and walls of rope and canvas, meaning tents. The boast was followed by defeat.

Their towns were built only of mud and brick ; but ours are all of solid stone, cut out of the almost impenetrable rock ;… The houses and walls of these Tartars are only of cords and canvas ; while we live in good fortresses, which are of hewn stone

Who Timur's Tartar army, described by the Mamluk council of Syria

Keep in mind. A speech composed by a court historian for enemies of his patron's grandfather, not a transcript.

CitiesDamascus1260

Aleppo, Hama and Damascus fall; Acre tears down its towers in fear

In his year-by-year chronicle Sanudo notes that in 1260 the Tartars broke in violently and took Aleppo, Harim, Hama, Homs and Damascus, killing every Saracen who met them, then entered the kingdom of Jerusalem and took Sidon, but not its sea castle. The people of Acre, afraid of them, cut down their orchards and threw down the orchard towers outside the walls, even carrying off cemetery stones. The Tartars were beaten by the Sultan of Egypt at Ain Jalut (the 'plain of Tiberias').

The next year the Tartars, breaking in violently, took Aleppo, Harim, Hama, Homs and Damascus, killing all the Saracens who came against them. Then they entered the kingdom of Jerusalem and took Sidon, but could by no means take the sea castle. The people of Acre, for fear of them, cut down the orchards outside the city, and threw the orchard towers to the ground, removing even the stones of the cemetery along with the stones of the buildings.

Strange talesSyriac. 1280

The officer boiled alive by the Tartar women

After Abaqa's army turned tail before the Mamluks in Syria, Riccoldo says, a great Tartar baron was caught plotting revolt. When the khan wanted to execute him, the Tartar ladies asked for him instead, boiled him alive and fed him in tiny pieces to the whole army as a warning.

the ladies and women of the Tartars opposed it, asking that he be given to them. When he was handed over they cooked him alive and gave him, cut into the smallest pieces, to the whole army to eat, as an example to others.

Daily lifeSyria1260 to 1303 (written c. 1321)

Why the Tartars could never hold Syria: summer heat and no grass

Arguing against landing a crusade in Armenia, Sanudo notes that the Tartars had often overrun almost all of Syria but always had to give it up and let the Sultan retake it. They could not cross the desert into Egypt, and could not stay through the summer because of the great heat and the lack of pasture for their horses and herds. It shows how nomad armies were tied to grass.

And it should be considered that many times the Tartars overran the land and took almost all the districts of Sham and Syria: but because they could not cross the desert by which one passes into Egypt ... And if one wished to say that the Tartars were forced to give up what they had taken, this was because they could not stay in summer on account of the intense heat, and on account of the lack of grazing, which they did not have for pasturing their horses and herds
