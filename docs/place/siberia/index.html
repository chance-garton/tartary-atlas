Strange talesSiberia1783 (written by 1800)

Heaps of monster bones across Tartary and Siberia

Discussing why lions are carved on Tibetan buildings though none live nearby, Turner remarks that if lions ever lived there wild, it was in the age of the huge animals whose bones lie in heaps in Tartary and Siberia. He takes the bones (mammoths) as proof of a great convulsion of the globe. This is Turner's own speculation, a common idea in his day.

it must have been at the same time with those vast monsters, whose bones are found in huge heaps in various parts of Tartary and Siberia at this day, and clearly point to some great convulsion, and change, in the order of our globe.

Who (none: fossil animals)

Read itSamuel Turner (1800)

Keep in mind. Turner's own inference from reports of mammoth bones; he did not see them.

Birch-bark tents and reindeer in the Siberian forests

The note on the Kyrgyz lands of the upper Yenisei describes a forest people whose poor lived in tents of birch bark, loaded their goods on white deer (reindeer), drank deer milk, gathered pine nuts and roots, and hunted in winter on 'wooden horses' (skis). Nearby Qianzhou had a few thousand households of Mongols and Uighurs, and workshops of Chinese craftsmen moved there early in the dynasty.

The poor, who have no fixed property, all make their tents of birch bark and load their baggage on white deer. They take deer's milk, gather pine nuts and dig up the roots of mountain lilies and peonies for food. In winter they also ride 'wooden horses' (skis) to hunt... [In Qianzhou] the inhabitants, several thousand households, are all Mongols and Huihe (Uighurs); there are several workshops of craftsmen, who are Han Chinese moved there at the start of the dynasty.

Strange talesSiberia (north of Mongolia)1254

Bone skates, but no monsters

From informants at Mongke's court Rubruck learned of northern peoples: the Orengai, who tied polished bones under their feet and sped over snow and ice fast enough to catch birds and beasts. He also asked about the monstrous races described by Isidore and Solinus; his informants said they had never seen any such thing, which, he writes, 'we much wonder whether it is true'.

There are also the Orengai, who tie polished bones under their feet and push themselves over the frozen snow and over the ice so fast that they catch birds and beasts. … I asked about the monsters, or monstrous men, that Isidore and Solinus tell of. They told me they had never seen such things, at which we much wonder whether it is true.

CitiesSiberialate 16th to 17th centuries

New Russian towns on Tatar land

After Yermak's death the Russians returned, built the fort of Tyumen and then the fortresses of Tobolsk, Tara and others (previous page). Coxe sums up the following century bluntly: the Tartars were subdued or wiped out, and new towns and colonies went up everywhere. In his account the Siberian towns were new Russian foundations, not inherited 'Tartar' cities.

The Russians pushed their conquest far and wide: wherever they appeared, the Tartars were either reduced or exterminated. New towns were built and colonies were planted on all sides. Before a century had well elapsed, all that vast tract of country now called Siberia … was annexed to the Russian dominions.

Who Siberian Tatars and other native peoples

Read itWilliam Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller)

Keep in mind. The previous page lists the forts as Tumen, Tobolsk, 'Sungur' (probably a scan error for Surgut) and Tara.

Gold in the old graves: 'from whom but the former Tatars?'

Arguing that the history of Chinggis Khan belongs to Siberian history, Müller points to the antiquities of southern Siberia: old entrenchments across the steppes, many memorial stones and statues, and graves from which gold and silver had been dug. He asks from whom such treasure could come but the former Tatars, to whom the riches of China, Persia, Russia, Poland, Bohemia and Hungary had fallen as plunder. On the next page he adds that the poorer old graves near Baikal show the earliest owners lived simply.

what treasures of gold and silver have not been brought out of the graves? From whom can they come but from the former Tatars, since it is known that all the treasures of China, Persia, Russia, Poland, Bohemia and Hungary had to fall to them as prey?

BuildingsSouthern Siberia (Yenisei, Irtysh and Tobol steppes)1711 to 1721

The 'pyramids' of Tartar kings on old maps

Strahlenberg describes the burial mounds of southern Siberia: some are earth heaps as high as houses, lying so thick that they look like a ridge of hills; others are ringed with rough or squared stones, and some are built entirely of stone. He explains that the 'pyramids' drawn on old European maps of Great Tartary, labelled 'Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings', must mean these tombs, which are not really pyramids.

Some are only raised up of Earth, as high as Houses, … that, at a Distance, they appear like a Ridge of Hills. … In some Places these Tombs are entirely built of Stone. Hence we find in the ancient Maps of Tartary the Greater, a Number of Pyramids, with these Words, in Latin; viz. The Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings,

Who ancient peoples of the Siberian steppe (Strahlenberg's 'Scythians')

Saw itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Tartary: a European label for everything east of the Ob

Introducing Siberia, Witsen explains his own usage: in Europe the name Tartary is commonly given to all lands east of the Ob and the Caspian Sea, and he uses it that way too, though strictly speaking Siberia does not lie in Tartary. A clear statement that 'Tartary' on his maps was a convention, not the name of a state.

just as, under the name Tartary, most commonly in Europe, all lands east of the Ob and the Caspian Sea are known, as I too include them under that name; although, properly speaking, this Siberia does not lie in Tartary

Log houses roofed with birch bark

Klaproth translates the Chinese encyclopedist Ma Duanlin on a northern people called the Piebald Horses. They built houses of logs laid one on another like the curb of a well, roofed them with birch bark, slept on hay, did not ride but drank mare's milk and fattened black-maned horses to eat. Klaproth notes that Ostyaks and Samoyeds still build this way and thinks the people were one of those tribes.

Their dwellings are made of trees laid one upon another, as the curbs of wells are made. The roof is covered with birch bark. Earth, wood, grass or straw serve them as carpets, and the place where they sleep is covered with hay. Others have no fixed dwelling.

A nation born of a princess and a wolf

Retelling a Chinese legend of the Uighurs, Deguignes says a Hun ruler shut his two beautiful daughters in a tower in the north so that heaven might marry them. An old wolf howled round the tower and made its den at the foot, and the younger sister, taking it for the god, went down to it. He adds that the Uighurs were said to imitate wolf howls in their songs, and that their tents stood on unusually high wheels, so the Chinese called them Kao-tche, 'high carts'.

An old wolf prowled night and day around the prison of the Tartar princesses; he watched the tower, howling frightfully, and finally made his den at its foot. The younger one, persuaded that this wolf, which had been watching the tower for so long, could only be the god to whom her father had dedicated her, urged her sister to go down with her.

Strange talesYeniseisk region, Siberia1713

The mammoth: a giant beast that lives under the earth

Near Yeniseisk the envoys heard of a huge animal in the coldest north that burrowed underground and died if exposed to sun and air. Its white, ivory-like bones were dug from river mud and carved by Russians into cups and combs, and its flesh was eaten against fever. The foreign name was Ma-mien-tou-va (mamont), the Chinese called it kee-shoo (the 'hidden rat'). Staunton notes that John Bell heard the same tale and that frozen mammoth flesh had since been found.

In the very coldest parts of this northern country, a species of animal is found which burrows under the earth, and which dies if it is at all exposed at any time to the sun and air. Its bones are very white and shining, like ivory. … The Russians collect the bones of this animal, in order to make cups, saucers, combs

Who Russians and Siberian natives (reported to the envoys)

Heard itTulishen (translated from the Chinese by George Thomas Staunton, 1821)

Keep in mind. Hearsay reported by the envoys; the belief that the animal lives underground is a folk explanation for frozen mammoth remains.

Strange talesSiberian frontier lines1750s (story told to Chappe after 1762)

Nomads who asked to be fired on

Back in Paris Chappe heard from the Chevalier de Saint-Pierre that a large band of Tartars once approached a Russian fort on the Siberian lines. Warned that they would be fired on unless they withdrew, they held a council and sent word asking the commander to fire. After the first cannonade killed several of them they held another council and asked again, and only left after the third.

A large detachment of these Tartars approached the Siberian lines a few years ago; the commander of one of the Russian forts saw them and, not knowing what this band of Tartars intended, let them know he would open fire on them if they did not withdraw. The Tartars gathered, held a council, and sent to ask the commander to order the firing; it was done.

Strange talesSouthern Siberia (Tara, Tomsk, Krasnoyarsk)about 1700 to 1710

Governors licensed the looting of tombs

Before the tsars took an interest, Strahlenberg says, the governors of Tara, Tomsk, Krasnoyarsk and other towns let townsmen go out in caravans to rob the steppe graves in return for a tenth of the gold and silver. The looters smashed the finds and shared the pieces out by weight. He owned only a broken fragment of a bell-metal mirror to show for it.

…the Governours of the Cities of Tara, Tomskoi, Crasnoyahr, … used to give Leave to the Inhabitants, to go in voluntary Caravans, to these Tombs, in Order to ransack them, on Condition that … the Governour should have an Allowance, generally the Tenths. These Caravans, … used, … to knock to Pieces these choice Antiquities, and give to each Person his Share, by Weight.

The nameSiberia1824

'Never so happy as in the wilds of Tartary'

In his preface Cochrane calls all of northern Asia 'Tartary' and invites scientists to explore 'the vast fields of Tartary' after him. He closes by saying he was never so happy as in the wilds of Tartary. For him, as for many readers in the 1820s, 'Tartary' simply meant Siberia and the lands beyond it, not a country or a state.

I may be allowed to add, that after such a journey, I might be supposed cured of the spirit of travelling, at least in so eccentric a way; yet the supposition is far from the fact, for as I am conscious that I was never so happy as in the wilds of Tartary, so have I never been so anxious to enter on a similar field as at this moment.
