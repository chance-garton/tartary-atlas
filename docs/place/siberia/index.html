Strange talesSiberia1783 (written by 1800)

Heaps of monster bones across Tartary and Siberia

it must have been at the same time with those vast monsters, whose bones are found in huge heaps in various parts of Tartary and Siberia at this day, and clearly point to some great convulsion, and change, in the order of our globe.

Editorial context

Discussing why lions are carved on Tibetan buildings though none live nearby, Turner remarks that if lions ever lived there wild, it was in the age of the huge animals whose bones lie in heaps in Tartary and Siberia. He takes the bones (mammoths) as proof of a great convulsion of the globe. This is Turner's own speculation, a common idea in his day.

Samuel Turner (1800)

New Russian towns on Tatar land

The Russians pushed their conquest far and wide: wherever they appeared, the Tartars were either reduced or exterminated. New towns were built and colonies were planted on all sides. Before a century had well elapsed, all that vast tract of country now called Siberia … was annexed to the Russian dominions.

Editorial context

After Yermak's death the Russians returned, built the fort of Tyumen and then the fortresses of Tobolsk, Tara and others (previous page). Coxe sums up the following century bluntly: the Tartars were subdued or wiped out, and new towns and colonies went up everywhere. In his account the Siberian towns were new Russian foundations, not inherited 'Tartar' cities.

William Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller)

what treasures of gold and silver have not been brought out of the graves? From whom can they come but from the former Tatars, since it is known that all the treasures of China, Persia, Russia, Poland, Bohemia and Hungary had to fall to them as prey?

Working translation

Original wording

was für Kostbarkeiten an Gold und Silber hat man nicht aus den Gräbern hervorgeholet? von wem können dieselbe anders, als von den ehmahligen Tataren, herstammen, indem bekannt ist, daß alle Schätze von China, Persien, Rußland, Polen, Böhmen, Ungarn, ihnen zum Opfer werden müssen?

Editorial context

Arguing that the history of Chinggis Khan belongs to Siberian history, Müller points to the antiquities of southern Siberia: old entrenchments across the steppes, many memorial stones and statues, and graves from which gold and silver had been dug. He asks from whom such treasure could come but the former Tatars, to whom the riches of China, Persia, Russia, Poland, Bohemia and Hungary had fallen as plunder. On the next page he adds that the poorer old graves near Baikal show the earliest owners lived simply.

Gerhard Friedrich Müller (Sammlung Russischer Geschichte, vol. 6, 1761-1762)

The 'pyramids' of Tartar kings on old maps

Some are only raised up of Earth, as high as Houses, … that, at a Distance, they appear like a Ridge of Hills. … In some Places these Tombs are entirely built of Stone. Hence we find in the ancient Maps of Tartary the Greater, a Number of Pyramids, with these Words, in Latin; viz. The Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings,

Editorial context

Strahlenberg describes the burial mounds of southern Siberia: some are earth heaps as high as houses, lying so thick that they look like a ridge of hills; others are ringed with rough or squared stones, and some are built entirely of stone. He explains that the 'pyramids' drawn on old European maps of Great Tartary, labelled 'Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings', must mean these tombs, which are not really pyramids.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

The mammoth: a giant beast that lives under the earth

In the very coldest parts of this northern country, a species of animal is found which burrows under the earth, and which dies if it is at all exposed at any time to the sun and air. Its bones are very white and shining, like ivory. … The Russians collect the bones of this animal, in order to make cups, saucers, combs

Editorial context

Near Yeniseisk the envoys heard of a huge animal in the coldest north that burrowed underground and died if exposed to sun and air. Its white, ivory-like bones were dug from river mud and carved by Russians into cups and combs, and its flesh was eaten against fever. The foreign name was Ma-mien-tou-va (mamont), the Chinese called it kee-shoo (the 'hidden rat'). Staunton notes that John Bell heard the same tale and that frozen mammoth flesh had since been found.
