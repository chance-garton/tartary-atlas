CitiesChelm (Kholm), Poland1230s to 1250s

Kholm: a new town that craftsmen fled to from the Tatars

Editorial contextDaniil founded Kholm on a wooded hill he spotted while hunting, and the chronicler notes that the Tatars could not take it when Batu conquered Rus. Daniil invited Germans, Rus, Poles and other foreigners, and young men and master craftsmen of every kind 'fled from the Tatars' to it: saddlers, bowyers, quiver makers, and smiths of iron, copper and silver. Here the arrow runs the other way: craftsmen leave Tatar-held lands to build a Rus prince's town.

he began to invite newcomers, Germans and Rus, foreigners and Poles; they came day after day, and young men and masters of every kind fled from the Tatars, saddlers and bowyers and quiver makers and smiths of iron and copper and silver, and there was life, and they filled the courtyards around the town, the fields and the villages

Daily lifeKrakówabout 1500

An oath sworn on drawn daggers

Editorial contextWhen envoys of Khan Sachmat (Sheikh Ahmed of the Great Horde) concluded an alliance against Moscow with Polish and Lithuanian lords at Kraków, the Tatars sealed it their own way. They poured cold water over drawn daggers and, one after another, sipped it as it dripped from the points, cursing whoever first broke the treaty to vanish like that water. In a footnote Schlözer remarks drily that this Christian alliance with Tatars came decades before the French scandalised Europe by allying with the Ottomans.

whereby the Tatars, after the custom of their country, poured cold water on drawn daggers and, as it dripped from the point, sipped it one after another, with the curse that whoever first broke the alliance should pass away like this water.

A 13th-century poem calls the Tartars cannibals

Editorial contextIn his notes d'Avezac prints lines, copied by Thomas Wright from a Cotton manuscript, of John of Garland's poem On the Triumphs of the Church. It paints the Tartars as Satan's household and the Church's plague who eat human flesh: no lion eats a lion and no wolf tears a wolf, but these men roast human limbs on spits. D'Avezac traces the poet's 'reliable' information to atrocity letters from priests in central Europe.

A people exceedingly savage, of Satan's household, a plague to the Church, at odds with the faith, in love with slaughter. … Hungry, man gnaws man; no lion fills its slack belly with lion's flesh; no wolf tears a wolf with its claw; this people turns limbs spitted on hazel skewers over the fire.

Working translation

Original wording

Gens est sæva nimis, Sathanæque domestica, pestis Ecclesiæ, fidei dissona, cædis amans. … Esuriens hominem corrodit homo; leo nullus Carne leonina viscera laxa replet; Non lupus ungue lupum lacerat; gens ista colurnis In veribus vertit membra veruta foco.

John of Garland (De triumphis Ecclesiae, 13th century), quoted by Thomas Wright in Armand d'Avezac's notes

A magic head on a Tartar banner, breathing smoke at the Poles

Editorial contextIn the history of the Polish kings Guagnini tells how the Poles were winning against the Tartars until a Tartar standard-bearer came forward with a banner marked with a Greek X and, on top of the lance, a terrible head made by magic art that breathed foul smoke over the Poles. Bewitched, they lost their strength and were cut down 'like grain by hail', and the Tartars cut off every dead man's right ear and filled nine great sacks.

but a certain standard-bearer of the Tartars came forward with a banner on which was written a Greek letter in this manner, X; and above, on the point of the lance, was fixed a terrible head, made by magic art, spewing smoke and foul vapour from its mouth over the Poles, by which, as if infected with a plague and bewitched by the force of spells, they lost their strength.

Original wording

sed quidam Vexillifer Tartarorum, cum vexillo, in quo erat scripta litera Graeca tali modo, X, processit: superius vero in cuspide hastae erat caput quoddam terribile infixum, arte magica confectum, ore fumum vaporemque sordidum in Polonos evomens: quo velut peste quadam infecti, & incantationum vi fascinati, vires amiserunt.

Alessandro Guagnini, Sarmatiae Europeae descriptio (Speyer, 1581), lives of the Polish kings, following Polish chroniclers

Kholm's church: carved heads, Roman glass, a floor like a mirror

Editorial contextThe chronicler describes Daniil's Church of St John in Kholm in unusual detail: vaults resting on four carved human heads, windows glazed with 'Roman' glass, a floor cast of copper and pure tin that shone like a mirror, and doors trimmed with white Galician and green Kholm stone, carved by 'a certain craftsman Avdei'. Nearby stood a tower on a stone base 15 ells high, built of hewn timber and whitewashed. The builders are Rus, and the materials are named.

vaults, with an arch from each corner, and their supports on four human heads carved by a certain craftsman; the windows adorned with Roman glass … its floor inside was cast of copper and pure tin, so that it shone like a mirror; its two doors were adorned with hewn stone, white Galician and green Kholm stone

Original wording

Daily lifePoland1663

Water, millet beer and mare's milk

Editorial contextHere Chevalier speaks as an eyewitness: in Poland he saw Tartar prisoners carry dead horses out of stables to feast on. He then lists their drinks: water, snow in winter, horse broth, 'Breha' (a millet drink, the steppe boza), and for the better-off mare's milk, mead and spirits, even though Islam forbade them wine.

I have seen at my being in Poland, divers Tartar prisoners come and take away dead Horses out of the Stables to feast one another with … their drink is ordinarily clear water, and in winter snow … or else Breha, which is a drink made with boiled Millet, but those who are better accommodated, and more delicate, drink Mares milk, Mead, and Strong waters

Pierre Chevalier (translated by Edward Brown, 1672)
