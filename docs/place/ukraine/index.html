BuildingsYedisan steppe (Nogai lands, southern Ukraine)1767

A Tartar house like a giant poultry basket

I employed myself, in the mean time, in examining the structure of my Tartar house. It was like a large poultry basket, built in lattice work, and formed in a circular inclosure, over which was a dome, open at the top; a felt of camel's hair covered the whole on the outside, and the hole at the top, intended occasionally as a vent-hole for the smoke.

Editorial context

On his first night among the Nogais, de Tott is given a brand-new felt tent (oba) and inspects how it is made: a circular lattice wall, a dome open at the top, and a covering of camel-hair felt. On the next page he admires the lattice joined with rawhide and learns that the tent was part of a new bride's dowry.

Baron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

Moabites from the desert: a Russian origin legend

They say that the Moabites, who were afterwards called Tartars, and who differed from the rest of mankind in language, manners, and dress, came to the river Calka ; but that no one knew whence they came, or what religious doctrine they held.

Editorial context

From the Russian chronicles Herberstein copies a story that the Tartars were 'Moabites' who came out of the north-eastern deserts, driven by a prophet named Gideon who preached the end of the world. The chronicle's date of AD 1025 for the Kalka battle is wrong (it was 1223), and the editor Major notes the attribution to Bishop Methodius is an anachronism.

Sigismund von Herberstein, retelling the Russian annals (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)
See the original page (p. 49)

About Tartars (Mongols of the 13th-century invasion)

Keep in mind

A biblical-style legend from the chronicles; Herberstein reports it without endorsing it.

he then asks for one of the white balls, which was cheese, made of mare's milk, saturated with salt, and dried… he then draws out his flat knife, empties the kettle on his hand, and presents me with a cylinder of puff-paste in a spiral form. I was anxious to taste it, and was really better pleased with the mess than I expected.

Editorial context

Curious about Nogai food, de Tott watches a young cook stir roasted millet flour into boiling water, then crumble in balls of salted, dried mare's-milk cheese until the mixture turns to a dough he turns out by hand. He liked the dish better than expected, but admits he could not bring himself to enjoy the mare's milk.

BuildingsMolochna River (southern Ukraine)1838 to 1841

A Nogai cottage of sun-dried brick and reeds

The Nogai constructs his own cottage with bricks dried in the sun, and whitewashes it regularly once a year within and without. Its dimensions are scarcely more than two or three-and-thirty feet by thirteen. The roof consists of a few rafters on which are laid reeds and branches of trees loaded with earth and ashes.

Editorial context

Settled by Russian officials only a generation earlier, the Nogais now built their own cottages. The walls are sun-dried brick, whitewashed yearly; the roof is rafters covered with reeds and branches loaded with earth and ashes. Inside are two rooms, and a man with two wives builds a separate room for each.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

The nameMolochna River (southern Ukraine)1838 to 1841

'Mayst thou never know happiness': where the Nogais got their name

their chronicles allege that the name of the nation is derived from neogai (which may be translated by the phrase, mayst thou never know happiness), and that it was bestowed on them in their old country, on account of their precarious and vagabond life.

Editorial context

European books said the Nogai horde was named after a Tatar general called Nogai. Xavier collected the Nogais' own traditions, which say nothing of such a general and derive the name from 'neogai', a curse on their hard wandering life. He prefers the Nogai version, arguing that a people so proud of their ancestry would have remembered a founder.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)
See the original page (p. 269)

About Nogais

Keep in mind

A folk etymology; modern historians generally connect the name with the 13th-century Mongol commander Nogai, the view Xavier rejects.

Daily lifeNew Serbia (Kirovohrad region, Ukraine)January 1769

How a raider carried off his captives

Five or six slaves, of different ages, sixty sheep, and twenty oxen, the prize of a single man, do not embarrass him. The children, with their heads peeping out of a sack, hanging by the pummel of the saddle; a young girl sitting before, leaning on her left hand; the mother behind on the crupper, the father on a led horse, the son upon another

Editorial context

Riding with the khan's army on its last great slave raid into Russian New Serbia, de Tott watches a single Tartar manage a whole captured family and flock: children in a sack at the saddle, the girl in front, the mother behind, the father and son on led horses. He admires the skill but calls the scene an image of 'avarice' and 'the most cruel injustice'. He reckons about 20,000 captives were taken.

Baron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

BuildingsAkeima, Molochna river, southern Ukraine1843

A Nogai village built on a Mennonite plan

The houses in this village were all built with regularity and solid structure ; they had chimneys and enclosed courtyards. In front of the house-doors generally stand two poplars, with small flower-beds on each side ; in the gardens I noticed a great many fruit-trees, and in the courtyard ploughs, harrows, and carts of Mennonite construction

Editorial context

Near the Mennonite colonies on the Molochna, Haxthausen visited a Nogai Tatar village that looked 'perfectly German'. The Mennonite farmer Johann Cornies had persuaded the formerly nomadic Nogais to build this way and, Haxthausen was told, had already settled 17,000 of them. Solid houses with chimneys, poplars, gardens and Mennonite ploughs had replaced the old steppe life.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

'To us he has given wheels'

Such is the order established by God himself," cried the Nogai, " to us he has given wheels, to other nations fixed dwellings and the plough."

Editorial context

Describing the Nogais before their settlement in villages, Xavier says a family could pack wife, children and goods into its felt-covered wagon (araba) within an hour and roll off towards the horizon. He quotes a Nogai saying that sums up the old nomad view of the world: God gave wheels to them and houses and ploughs to others.

Portable Nogai huts of twigs and felt

They encamp themselves in little cabins of felt; their tents, according to Thunmann, are a kind of portable huts, of a circular form, of about eight feet in diameter, made of lath-work or hurdles of twigs, about the size of a thumb, forming a sort of wall about four feet in height, upon which rests a dome or roof of the same structure

Editorial context

Reuilly describes the seven or eight thousand Nogais roaming between the Berda and the Molochna. Their round huts, about eight feet across, have a wicker wall and dome covered with rush mats and brown felt, a smoke-hole and a tiny door; the furniture is a few cushions, a low table, pots and dishes. He adds that they were starting to build fixed houses and farm.

Jean Reuilly (English translation, 1807), citing Johann Thunmann

Daily lifeMolochna River (southern Ukraine)1838 to 1841

The price of a Nogai bride

A handsome girl of good family costs four or five hundred rubles, besides a couple of score of cows and a few other beasts. Young widows are cheaper, and old women are to be had for nothing. The bride's price is paid on the spot by the wooer

Editorial context

Among the Nogais a groom never saw his bride before the wedding and bargained with her family for her price. Xavier gives the going rates: four or five hundred rubles and a couple of score cows for a handsome girl of good family, less for widows. On the same page he adds that Crimean mountain Tatar and Kalmyk wives cost less and were bought by poorer men.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)
See the original page (p. 275)

About Nogais

Keep in mind

Written from outside and with some disdain; how widely these prices applied is not stated.

The nameNogai steppe (southern Ukraine)1795

The Nogais: a splinter of the Golden Horde

The Nogay Tartars, who have wandered for some ages in this desert (a part of the primitive or little Scythia of the ancients), were originally a subdivision of the famous golden horde placed in the Kaptchak by the conqueror Zingis Chan, (properly Tschinghis), who, on some quarrel among themselves, separated from the main body of their nation, under a chief or general named Nogay

Editorial context

Crossing the Nogai steppe she explains the horde's name: the Nogais split from the Golden Horde under a leader called Nogai, which, she suggests, let them escape Timur's destruction of the Horde in the 1380s and 1390s. She adds that Russian rule has greatly changed them since earlier travellers' accounts.

Maria Guthrie (letter, translated by Matthew Guthrie, 1802)

Where the Dobruja Tatars came from

Those who escaped the sword then settled on the Dobruja plains on the Danube, where their descendants … still know that their forefathers were driven out of Podolia by the Lithuanians.

Working translation

Original wording

Die dem Schwerdt entflohen, ließen sich hierauf in den Dobruczer Ebenen an der Donau nieder, wo ihre Nachkommen … auch noch wissen, daß ihre Vorfahren durch die Littauer aus Podolien vertrieben worden.

Editorial context

Schlözer tells how the Tatars, after wasting Russia, had settled on the steppe on both sides of the Dnieper and ruled Podolia through their baskaks (governors), until Grand Duke Olgerd of Lithuania beat three Tatar khans at Siniawoda (the Blue Waters) and cleared them from the land as far as Ochakiv. The survivors, he says, settled on the Dobruja plains by the Danube, where their descendants still remembered that the Lithuanians had driven their ancestors out of Podolia. Olgerd's nephews then built castles in the freed land.

August Ludwig Schlözer (1785), following Kojałowicz
See the original page (p. 67)

About Golden Horde Tatars of Podolia; the Dobruja Tatars

Keep in mind

Schlözer keeps his chronicle source's date of 1331; the battle of the Blue Waters is now usually dated 1362. The Dobruja tradition is reported, not checked.

The Nogays, a wandering horde made to settle

The Nogay Tatars, if not the original inhabitants of the country, at least wandered over the immense Stepps of New Russia, with their tents, flocks, and herds, at the time of the Russian Conquest, and for ages before: this remnant of the Nomades of Nogay Tatary, are now collected together in a number of villages

Editorial context

Holderness explains who the Nogay Tatars were: the old nomads of 'Nogay Tatary', who had roamed the steppes north of Crimea with tents, flocks and herds for ages. By her time the Russian government had gathered them into villages and pushed them to farm, though they still preferred grazing and horses.

Mary Holderness
