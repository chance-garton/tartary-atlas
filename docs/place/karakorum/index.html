CitiesKarakorum1254

Karakorum: worse than Saint-Denis, with two quarters and many temples

Rubruck's famous verdict on the Mongol capital: apart from the Khan's palace, it is not as good as the French town of Saint-Denis, whose abbey is worth ten Mongol palaces. It has a Saracen quarter with markets and a Cathayan (Chinese) quarter of craftsmen, twelve idol temples, two mosques and a church. On the next page (n221) he adds earthen walls with four gates, each with its own market for grain, sheep, oxen and carts, or horses.

except for the Khan's palace, it is not as good as the town of Saint-Denis in France, whose monastery is worth ten times more than the whole palace of Möngke itself. There are two main streets, one called the Saracens', where the markets and fair are held … There are twelve temples of idolaters of various nations, and two mosques of the Saracens

CitiesKarakorum1235

Ögedei puts walls round the camp at Karakorum, 1235

The Chinese annals record that in spring 1235 Ögedei ordered walls built around the encampment of Holin (Karakorum) and a palace called Wan'an five li round. Gaubil elsewhere in the book says Holin is the Caracoram of other writers. It is a precise date for when the Mongol camp-capital became a walled town.

In the spring of the year 1235 the Emperor Ögedei ordered the camp of Holin to be enclosed with walls, and the palace called Ouangan to be made five li in circumference.

CitiesKarakorum13th century

Karakorum: an earthen rampart, for stone is scarce

Polo calls Karakorum a city about three miles round, enclosed by an earthen rampart because stone was scarce, with a citadel and a governor's palace, and 'the first city that the Tartars possessed'. Yule's note adds that Ögedei built its walls and palace in 1234, and quotes Rubruck's famous verdict that apart from the palace it was no better than the village of St Denis.

Caracoron is a city of some three miles in compass. [It is surrounded by a strong earthen rampart, for stone is scarce there. And beside it there is a great citadel wherein is a fine palace in which the Governor resides.] 'Tis the first city that the Tartars possessed after they issued from

Marco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871; square brackets mark Yule's additions from other manuscripts)

BuildingsKarakorum1254

Karakorum's mud wall and its Chinese artisans

Rubruck describes who lived in and built up Karakorum: a Muslim ('Saracen') quarter with the markets, and a quarter of Cathayans (northern Chinese), all artisans. There were twelve temples of various peoples, two mosques and one church, inside a mud wall with four gates, each gate with its own market. Rockhill's note adds that the Mongols themselves mostly lived in tents even at the capital.

one of the Saracens in which are the markets … the other is the quarter of the Cathayans, all of whom are artisans. … There are there twelve idol temples of different nations, two mahummeries in which is cried the law of Machomet, and one church of Christians in the extreme end of the city. The city is surrounded by a mud wall and has iiii gates.

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

About Mongols, with Muslim merchants and Chinese (Cathayan) craftsmen

Keep in mind

'Mahummeries' means mosques; 'idol temples' are mostly Buddhist.

Strange talesKarakorum1254

No monsters, but hairy little men who cry 'chin, chin'

Rubruck asked priests at Karakorum about the dog-headed and one-eyed monsters described by Isidore and Solinus. They told him they had never seen any, which surprised him. Then a priest from Cathay told him of tiny hairy creatures with stiff knees living among rocks east of Cathay, caught by leaving out mead to get them drunk, whose blood made a red dye.

They told me they had never seen such, which astonished me greatly, if it be true. … in the countries east of Cathay there are high rocks, among which dwell creatures who have in all respects human forms, except that their knees do not bend, so that they get along by some kind of jumping motion

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

About Priests at the Mongol court; Cathay

Keep in mind

The creature story is hearsay from a single informant; the denial of monsters is a useful check on older book-learning.

CitiesKarakorum1230s, told c.1300 to 1310

Ogedei throws open the treasury of his new capital

Among the anecdotes of Ogedei's generosity, Rashid says that while Karakorum (Qara Qorum) was being founded the khan found about twenty thousand silver ingots (balish) piled in the treasury and saw no point in hoarding them. He had it announced that anyone who wanted balish could come and take it. The story assumes a real city with a population of rich and poor, nobles and commoners.

Another story: at the time when he had ordered Qara Qorum to be founded, one day he went into the treasury and saw nearly two tumans [some twenty thousand] of balish [silver ingots]. He said: 'What use to us is hoarding this?' … The people of the city, noble and humble, rich and poor, made for the treasury, and every one of them found a generous share.

BuildingsKarakorum1254

Karakorum's quarters, temples and mud wall

Rubruck describes Karakorum as two quarters: one of Muslims (Saracens) with the markets, where merchants gathered because of the court, and one of 'Cathayans' (northern Chinese), who were all craftsmen. Outside them stood the large palaces of the court secretaries. There were twelve temples of various idolaters, two mosques and one church; a mud wall with four gates enclosed the town, each gate with its own market for grain, sheep and goats, oxen and carts, or horses.

[one quarter] of the Saracens, where the markets are, and many merchants gather there because of the court … the other is the quarter of the Cathayans, who are all craftsmen. Outside these quarters are large palaces belonging to the court secretaries. There are twelve idol temples of different nations, two mosques in which the law of Mahomet is proclaimed, and one Christian church at the far end of the town. The town is enclosed by a mud wall and has four gates.

A pavilion raised by Muslim architects to outdo the Chinese

Juvaini follows Ögedei's yearly round of residences near Karakorum. In spring the khan moved to a palace called Qarshi Suri, which 'Muslim architects' had put up in rivalry with the Khitayans (Chinese builders): a lofty pavilion decorated with paintings and many-coloured carpets, with a throne and jewelled vessels. The Mongol khan's buildings are credited to foreign craftsmen competing for his favour.

Daily lifeKarakorum1254

Mongke Khan: one God, many ways, like fingers on a hand

After a debate between Christians, Muslims and Buddhists at his court, Mongke Khan explained the Mongol faith to Rubruck. The Moal believe in one God, he said, but God gives different peoples different ways, as he gives the hand different fingers. It is a vivid statement of the Mongol court's religious tolerance, spoken through an interpreter.

"We Moal," he said, "believe that there is only one God, by whom we live and by whom we die, and for whom we have an upright heart." … "But as God gives us the different fingers of the hand, so he gives to men divers ways.

Mongke Khan, as reported by William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

About Mongke Khan of the Mongols

Keep in mind

Mongke's words reached Rubruck through an interpreter, William Boucher's son.

A tent for two thousand people at Güyük's election

At the assembly that elected Güyük Great Khan, Carpini found a huge tent of white purple cloth which he reckoned could hold more than two thousand people, ringed by a wooden palisade painted with figures. The chiefs changed the colour of their robes each day. A few pages on he describes another tent, the 'Golden Horde', on pillars covered with gold plates fixed with gold nails.

There we found a very large tent of white purple, so large, in our opinion, that it could hold more than two thousand people. Around it they had set up a scaffold or palisade of wood, covered with various figures and paintings.

CitiesKarakorum (Orkhon valley)13th century; located in the 1842 recension

Where was Karakorum? Between the Orkhon and the Tamir

In the Khalkha section's list of ancient sites, the gazetteer copies the Yuan history's account of Hening (Karakorum) and then adds its own finding: according to the 'Map of the Northern Desert', the old capital stood east of the Khangai mountains, between the Orkhon and Tamir rivers. A few pages earlier the same section places the Erdene Zuu monastery on the upper Orkhon. Karakorum here is a known Mongol capital with a location, not a mystery.

According to the Map of the Northern Desert, north of Hening is the Xiluge (Selenge) river, and further north the Kokebuqa ferry; to the west are the Mi river and the Qara Qorum river; to the southwest is a hot spring; to the south the Wangji river. Note: the place lies east of the present Khangai mountains, between the Orkhon and the Tamir rivers.

BuildingsKarakorum1254

The silver tree made by a Paris goldsmith

At Karakorum Mongke had a large walled court beside the town wall with a great palace where he held drinking feasts twice a year. Because it was unseemly to carry skins of milk and drink into the hall, Master William of Paris, a goldsmith captured in Hungary and working for the khan, made at the entrance a great silver tree with four silver lions at its roots, from which mare's milk flowed through a pipe.

Mongke himself has at Karakorum a great court next to the town walls … There is a great palace in which he holds his drinking feast twice a year … At the entrance of this great palace, since it was unseemly to bring in skins of milk and other drinks, Master William of Paris made him a great silver tree, at whose roots are four silver lions with a pipe, all spouting white mare's milk.

CitiesMongolia (Karakorum)c. 1245 (written c. 1321)

A land with no towns except 'Cracurim'

Sanudo's chapter headed 'the sordid origin of the Tartars' describes the land 'called Mongal or Tartaria' where the East meets the North: mountainous in parts, mostly gravel and sand, short of water, so that there are no villages or cities 'except one called Cracurim', said to be fairly good, though the land is excellent for raising herds. Cracurim is Karakorum.

The land called Mongal or Tartaria is described as lying in that part where the East joins the North ... and it lacks plenty of water: hence there are no villages or cities there, except one which is called Cracurim, and is said to be fairly good. For raising herds, however, the land is excellent.

A new khan lifted on white felt

At Güyük's enthronement the princes and nobles set him on a golden seat, then moved him down onto a felt on the ground and told him to look up to God in heaven and down to the felt on earth: rule justly and the earth would obey him; rule badly and not even the felt he sat on would be his. Then they raised him and his wife together on the felt and proclaimed them khan and great lady. Caps flew, belts were thrown over shoulders, the crowd knelt nine times, and for seven days meat, salt and mare's milk were handed out from the khan's tent; the women's seats in the golden throne tent were of white felt.

they moved him from the golden seat onto a felt rug on the ground and said: Look up to God in heaven and down at the felt on the earth; if you rule well, if you are just and generous and honour the princes and lords, the earth will be subject to you at your will; otherwise you will be poor, despised and wretched, and not even the felt you sit on will be yours.

CitiesKarakorum1235

Karakorum built on an old ruin, with craftsmen from China and Islam

Juvaini says Ögedei chose the Orkhon valley and the Qaraqorum mountains for his throne, though there was no town or village there, only the trace of a wall of the old Uighur city of Ordu-Baliq. An inscribed stone found there named its founder, Buqu Khan. On top of the ruin Ögedei ordered a new city built, 'known as the city of Qaraqorum', and craftsmen of every kind were brought from Khitai and from the Islamic lands, along with farmers.

In that place there had been neither town nor village, only the trace of a wall, which had been Ordu-Baliq … and on top of it he ordered that a city be built, and [it was] named Ordu-Baliq … known as the city of Qaraqorum. And from Khitai they brought [workers] of every kind, and likewise from the lands of Islam craftsmen and farmers.

CitiesKarakorum1254

Karakorum: smaller than the village of Saint-Denis

Rubruck lived for weeks at Karakorum, the Mongol capital founded by Ogedei in 1235. His verdict is famously unimpressed: apart from the Khan's palace the town was smaller than the village of Saint-Denis outside Paris. Rockhill's note records that the ruins were located on the Orkhon river in 1889 by the Russian traveller Yadrintsev.

Of the city of Caracarum you must know that, exclusive of the palace of the Chan, it is not as big as the village of Saint Denis, and the monastery of Saint Denis is ten times larger than the palace.

William of Rubruck (translated by W. W. Rockhill, 1900)

The silver tree that poured four drinks at Karakorum

Beside Möngke's palace at Karakorum stood a great silver tree made by a captive Parisian goldsmith, Master Guillaume. Four silver lions at its foot and pipes hidden in the trunk delivered wine, kumiss, honey-mead and rice beer, and a silver angel on top blew a trumpet (worked by a man hidden inside) to call the servers. It is a vivid case of a Mongol showpiece built by a foreign craftsman.

this Master Guillaume had made him a great silver tree, at whose foot were four lions, also of silver, each with a pipe or channel from which mare's milk flowed. The four pipes were hidden in the tree, rising to the top and flowing down from there. On each of these conduits were gilded serpents whose tails wound round the trunk of the tree.

CitiesKarakorumabout 1368 to 1415; rediscovered in scholarship by the 1820s

Karakorum destroyed, then so forgotten no one knew where it was

After the Mongol emperors were driven out of China, Ritter says, Karakorum briefly became their capital again and a base for raids on China, until it was destroyed in the wars between the Oirats and the northern Mongols, which he places around 1415. After that the records fall silent so completely that even its location was forgotten, until the French sinologist Abel-Remusat fixed it again from Chinese texts; Ritter wishes physicists and antiquaries would go and examine the site. On the same page he summarises William of Rubruck's description of the khan's brick-walled enclosure, palace and the foreigners from every nation living there.

Karakorum served them only for a short time as a stronghold from which to attack the Chinese frontier; for in the wars of the Oirats and the northern Mongols it was destroyed … All later reports cease so completely at this point that even the location of the place was forgotten, until Abel-Remusat's critical research could once again assign it its proper position.

Original wording

Es diente ihnen auch Karakorum nur kurze Zeit als Waffenplatz, um von da aus die Chinesischen Grenzen anzugreifen; denn in den Kriegen der Oirad und nordischen Mongolen wurde es zerstört, … Alle spätern Nachrichten hören hiermit so ganz auf, daß selbst die Lage des Ortes vergessen war, bis Ab. Remusats Critik demselben von neuem seine richtige Stelle anweisen konnte.

CitiesKarakorum (Kharkhorin), Mongolia1235

Karakorum: an old Uighur town, walled by Ögedei

The Chinese commentary says that Khorin (Karakorum) had been the town of a Uighur khan in Tang times. The Mongols first used it as their assembly place and in 1235 surrounded it with a wall about five li (Bichurin: about 3.5 versts) round. In the same year, the annal adds, Ögedei built the Wan'an palace there.

In the second month the Mongols surrounded Khorin with a wall. Khorin was formerly the town of the Uighur (Khoikhor) Pijia khan, who lived under the Tang dynasty. The Mongols earlier made it their place of assembly, and now surrounded it with a wall about five li in circuit.

Original wording

Karakorum explained as 'the circle of the common people'

In a footnote Zwick explains that kura means a circle, especially the ring of priests' huts around a Kalmyk temple tent, which served as the centre of a horde without a prince. He then derives the name of Genghis Khan's capital from the same word. It is a striking attempt to read an old imperial capital as a camp word.

Thus, the capital of the famous Genghis Khan, which is known in history under the name of Karakorum, was originally Chara Kura, or, in the Tartar dialect, Kara Kura, the circle of the Blacks, or common people.

Heinrich August Zwick (footnote, English edition, 1831)

About Mongols; Kalmyks

Keep in mind

This is Zwick's own etymology; the name is usually explained as Turkic qara qorum, 'black rocks'.

Nobody yet knew where Karakorum lay

In a footnote Humboldt lists the rival guesses for the ruins of Karakorum, Mongke Khan's capital: Klaproth put it at one set of coordinates, Abel-Remusat at one of two named ruins, and d'Anville had it two degrees too far south-east. Klaproth places the ruins of Karakorum at latitude 46° 41', longitude 100° 24'. … Abel-Remusat believes Karakorum identical either with Talarho-kara-balgasun (long. 100° 44', lat. 47° 32') or with Baisiri-buritu (long. 100° 36', lat. 48° 24'). … Mr Schott, to whom we owe ingenious and learned research on the Tartar languages, thinks that Karakorum means black town or black residence.
