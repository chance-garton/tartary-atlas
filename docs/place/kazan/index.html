How Mongol conquerors became the Turkic 'Tatars' of Kazan and Crimea

Editorial contextBichurin explains how the name moved: the Mongol lords on the Volga were few among Turkic subjects, married into them, took their language, customs and Islam, and so disappeared, reappearing under the Russian name 'Tatars' as a seemingly new people ruling Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea. A footnote adds that the Tatars do not call themselves by that name but Uzbeks, Nogais and so on.

The conquerors, taking from the Turkestanis through marriage their manners and customs, their language and religion, little by little were completely lost, so that at last the very name of the Tatans disappeared, and under the Russian name of Tatars they became as it were a new people, which afterwards ruled in the tsardoms of Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea.

How Turks came to be called Tatars

Editorial contextKlaproth explains the mix-up. When Chinggis Khan's son was sent west, the steppes north of the Caspian and Black Sea were full of Turkic peoples (Cumans, Pechenegs, Bulgars), who became subjects of the Kipchak khanate. Its rulers were Tatars (Mongols) but its people mostly Turks. When the empire broke up into the khanates of Crimea, Kazan and Astrakhan, the khans were still descendants of Chinggis, so the states kept the name 'Tatar' even though their armies and people were Turkic. On the previous page he tells how Chinggis himself got his birth name from a captured chief of the 'White Tatars'.

Its rulers were Tatars, but its subjects mostly Turks. Towards the end of the fifteenth century this empire broke up into several small ones, of which those of Crimea, Kazan and Astrakhan were the most important. Their princes descended from [Chinggis Khan] and were Mongols or Tatars, but their armies no longer were … Nevertheless these khanates kept the name 'Tatar', because the khans were Tatars.

'Ask a Kazan Tatar if he is a Tatar'

Editorial contextKlaproth says that if you asked a so-called Tatar of Kazan or Astrakhan whether he was a Tatar, he would say no, and that he called his language Türki or Turuk, not Tatar. Remembering that his forefathers had been conquered by the Mongols or Tatars, he took 'Tatar' as an insult meaning robber. Klaproth concludes that Tatars must not be confused with Turks, rebukes the historian Schlözer for doing so, and adds that Chinese historians never called the Manchus 'Ta-ta', so the missionaries' phrase 'Tartar-Manchu' came only from their habit of calling every Central Asian a Tartar.

Ask, however, a so-called Tatar of Kazan or Astrakhan whether he is a Tatar, and he will answer no; just as he calls his language not Tatari but Türki or Turuk. Remembering that his forefathers were subjugated by the Mongols or Tatars, he considers 'Tatar' a term of abuse, which to him means the same as 'robber'.

Editorial contextRose summarises the history of Bulghar on the Volga: it suffered from 'Mongol-Tatar' raids, was conquered by Batu Khan in 1236, and then served the Golden Horde khans as their summer residence, with Sarai on the lower Volga as their winter seat. The next page adds that Marco Polo's father and uncle stayed there in 1261.

…was completely subdued in 1236 by Batu Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan, and incorporated into the Kipchak empire. The descendants of Genghis Khan in this empire, whom the Russians called the khans of the Golden Horde, chose Bulghar as their summer residence and Sarai on the lower Volga as their winter residence.

Tatar, the French of the East

Editorial contextHaxthausen was impressed that nearly all Kazan Tatars could read and write, that their village schools were run by mullahs, and that students went to Orenburg and Bukhara. In a footnote he claims their Turkic language worked as a common tongue from Persia to China and even Tunis.

The Tatar language occupies the same place in the East as the French in the West. Eastwards from Persia to China, westwards throughout all the Turkish countries, and even in Tunis, any one can make himself understood by means of it.

August von Haxthausen, footnote (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Kazan Tatars and Turkic speakers generally

Keep in mind

Kazan, Astrakhan and Siberia conquered

Editorial contextIn his summing up of Ivan the Terrible's reign, Horsey lists the conquest of the Tatar 'kingdoms' of Kazan and Astrakhan and the peoples along the Volga to the Caspian, the end of tribute to the Crimean khan, and the conquest of the 'kingdom of Siberia'.

he conquered the kingdom of Casan and the kingdom of Astracan, and all the regions and great people of the Nagaie and Chercas Tartors, and many other of that kinde, inhabitinge above two thowsand miells of each side that famous river of Volga, sowthward eaven to the Mare Caspian Sea.

Sir Jerome Horsey (memoir; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

Princes of Tartar origin

Editorial contextAmong his hosts in Kazan were Prince Davydov, head of the post, and Prince Tenishev, an army general; Cochrane notes that both princes were 'of Tartar origin'. He then explains that Russian titles of Tatar or foreign origin did not by themselves give noble rank. The passage is a reminder that many Russian noble families traced their roots to Tatar ancestors.

Both the princes are of Tartar origin, though their character and conduct would do honour to the proudest rank of polished society.

John Dundas Cochrane (1824)
