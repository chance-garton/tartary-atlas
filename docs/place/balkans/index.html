Daily lifeFarsala, Thessaly1801

Ten minutes, a pipe and a spoonful of coffee

At a khan in Pharsalus Clarke watched 'Tartar' couriers change horses. Each squatted against the wall for about ten minutes, smoked hard, gulped a spoonful of muddy coffee, said 'God be praised' and rode on, travelling night and day. Clarke says no couriers in the world could match their endurance, and that Europeans travelling alone in Turkey disguised themselves in their dress.

the Tartar squats (for it cannot be called sitting) with his back against the wall, supporting himself upon the soles of his feet, and inhaling rapidly the fumes of his pipe upon his lungs; sending it back in curling volumes through his nostrils. Then, if he can procure about as much muddy coffee as would fill the bowl of a table-spoon, he utters his Alhandilla! (God be praised !)

BuildingsBalkans, on the road to Constantinople1332

A queen's travelling mosque, left at the border

Ibn Battuta escorted Uzbek Khan's Byzantine wife on a visit to her father in Constantinople. Like her husband, she carried a mosque (evidently a tent) that was set up at every halt for daily prayers. At the first Byzantine fortress she left it behind, and from then on, he says with some dismay, the call to prayer stopped and wine appeared at her table.

The Queen had with her a mosque, which she set up at every stage, just as her husband used to do. In this she had daily prayers. She left it, however, at the fortress. After this the office of the Moazin ceased : wine was brought into the banquet and of this she drank.

BuildingsLarissa, Thessaly1801

Unrobbed mounds, unlike those of Tartary

On the plain near Larissa Clarke saw many large tumuli in good condition, some with shepherds' huts built on top. He notes that, unlike the mounds of 'Tartary', they did not seem to have been dug open for treasure. The steppe kurgans were well known for being plundered for their gold.

They did not seem to have been opened and ransacked for the hidden treasure which it is possible some of them contain, as it has often happened in Tartary ; but, upon some of them, small huts had been constructed, as dwellings for the shepherds.

The namePodolia and Dobruja1331 in the source

Where the Dobruja Tatars came from

Schlözer tells how the Tatars, after wasting Russia, had settled on the steppe on both sides of the Dnieper and ruled Podolia through their baskaks (governors), until Grand Duke Olgerd of Lithuania beat three Tatar khans at Siniawoda (the Blue Waters) and cleared them from the land as far as Ochakiv. The survivors, he says, settled on the Dobruja plains by the Danube, where their descendants still remembered that the Lithuanians had driven their ancestors out of Podolia. Olgerd's nephews then built castles in the freed land.

Those who escaped the sword then settled on the Dobruja plains on the Danube, where their descendants … still know that their forefathers were driven out of Podolia by the Lithuanians.

Daily lifeEdirne (Adrianople)March 1205 (compared with 1760s)

Shooting backwards while fleeing, 'as the Tartars still do'

Telling how Cuman horsemen raided the Latin crusaders besieging Adrianople in 1205, Peyssonnel describes their tactic of fleeing while loosing arrows backwards without stopping, 'as the Tartars of today still do'. The Latins could not catch them, because the Cumans were more lightly armed and had faster, better-trained horses.

The Cumans took flight, contenting themselves with loosing a volley of arrows behind them from time to time without halting, as the Tartars of today still do. The Latins could not overtake these enemies, who were more lightly armed than they and had faster horses, better trained for running.

Original wording

Les Comains prirent la fuite, en se contentant de faire de temps en temps quelques décharges de flèches, par derrière, sans arrêter leur marche, comme font encore les Tartares d'aujourd'hui. Les Latins ne purent atteindre ces ennemis, qui étoient armés plus légèrement qu'eux, & avoient des Chevaux plus vîtes, & mieux exercés à la course.

BuildingsMalkara, Thrace (Turkey)January 1802

Burial mounds 'precisely similar to those of Tartary'

Riding across the bare plains of Thrace, Clarke is reminded of the steppes of southern Russia and its tumuli (kurgans), which he had seen on his earlier journey. He does not say who built either set; he treats them simply as the same kind of ancient earth mound.

This part of Thrace resembles the steppes in the South of Russia ; and to add to the similitude of the two countries, there are here tumuli precisely similar to those of Tartary.

Strange talesBoeotia, Greece1801

No poets on the flat steppe

Admiring the mountains near Thebes, Clarke argues that great poetry needs great scenery, and that flat countries like Scythia or Belgium never produced it. Even Homer, he says, would not have been a poet if raised in 'oriental Tartary'. It is a period prejudice, not an observation, but it shows how Tartary served as a byword for dull, flat emptiness.

Even Homer himself, if he had been a native of oriental Tartary, and had been cradled and brought up under the impressions made by such scenery, and under the influence of such a climate, would never have been a poet.

Who Tartary as a stereotype

Legend or rumourEdward Daniel Clarke (1816)

Keep in mind. Clarke's own opinion and prejudice, not a report about any real people.
