BuildingsHohhot region, Inner Mongolia (Fengzhou)1550s

Chinese renegades build a walled town and palaces: 'bansheng' means 'house'

The history says that Chinese outlaws led by Qiu Fu and Zhao Quan, who had gone over to the Mongols, gathered fugitives at Fengzhou, built a walled town to protect themselves, put up palaces and broke irrigated fields. Their settlements were called bansheng, which the history explains as the Mongol word for 'house'. Zhao Quan also taught the Mongols siege warfare.

At that time Qiu Fu and the others, living among the enemy, gathered fugitives and settled at Fengzhou. They built a walled town to defend themselves, put up palaces and opened irrigated rice fields, calling them baisheng; baisheng in Chinese means 'house'. Zhao Quan taught the enemy to become more practised in attack and warfare.

CitiesInner Mongolia13th to 15th century

Who built the lost cities of Mongolia

Reflecting on the Old Town, Huc says such ruined cities are common in Mongolia and that their story is unknown locally. His best guess, from Chinese annals, is that the Mongols built many large cities in 'Northern Tartary' while they ruled China, and that after their expulsion the Ming emperor Yongle burned their towns in three campaigns deep into the desert.

the probabilities are, that they date no earlier back than the 13th century, the period when the Mongols rendered themselves masters of the Chinese empire … During their domination, say the Chinese annals, they erected in Northern Tartary many large and powerful cities. … the Emperor Young-Lo, who desired to exterminate the Tartars, invaded their country, and burned their towns

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

A great stone ghost city near Lake Dalai

Describing Lake Dalai (Hulun Nur) from what the local peoples told him, Spafarii reports a great stone city a day's ride from the lake, 'with every kind of building', its stone halls still whole. Some said its people all died of plague and left their goods behind, so that no one dared enter and people only looked at it from afar; others said war had emptied it. On the same page he repeats that the lake is so deep it holds whales and seals.

A day's journey from the Dalai there is a great stone city with every kind of building, and the stone halls in it are whole; and they say that in that city the people all perished of plague, and all their goods remained there; and the natives dare not go into that city, but look at it all from afar; and others say that it was laid waste by war.

BuildingsEastern Inner Mongolia1693

Haloed figures: 'built by Christians'?

Looking at the carvings on the ruined tower, Ides saw kings seated cross-legged, crowned queens with folded hands, and attendants with rays around their heads 'as Saints are commonly painted'. From the haloes he concluded that Christians must have built the tower. Other panels showed warriors with pikes 'after the Chinese manner' and figures with 'hideous Diabolical Visages'. Seated, haloed, crowned figures with guardians are typical of Buddhist pagoda reliefs, so this is Ides's own guess, not a local tradition.

Others shew us several Figures seeming to represent Queens folding their Hands together, with their Servants on each side: The Queens having Crowns on their Heads, and the other being adorned with Rays or Lustres, as Saints are commonly painted, and also folding their Hands: all which hints that this Tower must needs be built by Christians.

Evert Ysbrants Ides (English translation, 1706)

About Unknown builders

Keep in mind

The carvings are eyewitness; the Christian builders are Ides's inference from haloes.

BuildingsShangdu (Xanadu)1256

A Chinese monk lays out the Mongols' new capital (Shangdu)

Finding Holin (Karakorum) inconvenient, Möngke had a new capital chosen in Tartary. The task went to a learned Chinese Buddhist monk, Lieoupingtchong (Liu Bingzhong), who picked a site east of Huanzhou; a city, imperial palace, temples, lords' palaces and courts were built inside high thick walls, and it soon filled with Chinese and Tartars. Gaubil notes it was later called Changtou (Shangdu, the Xanadu of legend).

He ordered a place in Tartary to be chosen that would henceforth be the capital of his states. This task was given to a Chinese monk named Lieoupingtchong. … A great city was built there, a palace for the Emperor, temples, palaces for the lords, and courts of justice. It was enclosed with high, thick walls.

A ruined city of the Tartar king 'Utai-chan'

On 10 October 1693 the caravan reached a large, old, deserted city called Taimingzinz, with two sealed towers, fallen masonry, and life-size stone statues of people and idols and huge stone lions, all carved so true to life that, Brand says, few such are found in Europe. The Chinese told him a Tartar king named Utai-chan had ruled there centuries before, until a Chinese king took it and drove the Tartar out; stone towers on the hills, they said, marked Tartar graves.

On 10 October we came to a great, old and ruined city called Taimingzinz, in which stand two towers … The Chinese told us that this place, many hundreds of years ago, when a Tartar king called Utaichan reigned there, was conquered by a Chinese king and the Tartar driven out

A princess's walled palace by the lake

Past the sandy desert, at the Yu'er lakes, Zhang found a walled detached palace belonging to a princess, with a sleeping hall, side chambers, galleries and a lookout tower, and a small settlement of commoners and craftsmen beside it. It is an early glimpse of Mongol-era buildings on the steppe, and of the artisans who lived around them.

On the eastern shore of the lake is the outer wall of a princess's detached palace, more than one zhang high and some two li square. Inside stands a sleeping hall flanked by two chambers, with a 'turtle' gallery behind, two side wings along its flanks and a lookout tower rising in front; climbing it gives a fine view. East of the palace, commoners and artisans live together, forming something of a settlement.

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Kingdom of Efe)18th century

A city built for a Mongol prince by imperial slaves

The Qianlong emperor married a daughter to a Khalkha prince, who hated court life and longed for his tents. As a compromise the emperor carved out land in the Chakhar, 'built for him' a small handsome city, and gave him a hundred families of slaves skilled in Chinese arts and crafts. The princess got her court; the prince could go back to the tent. Huc credits the city to the emperor and Chinese craftsmen, not to the Mongols.

He dismembered a portion of the Tchakar, and assigned it to the Mongol prince; he built for him, amid these solitudes, a small but handsome city, and presented to him a hundred families of slaves skilled in the arts and manufactures of China.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Gechekten)1844

A Mongol: 'We Mongols are formed for living in tents'

In Gechekten, where Chinese settlers had turned grassland into poor sandy fields, the priests asked a Mongol rider why his people did not farm. He answered that Mongols were made for tents and herds; since they had started farming and building houses they had grown poor, and the Chinese (Kitat) had taken their land, herds and houses. It is a Mongol's own verdict on houses versus tents.

"We Mongols are formed for living in tents, and pasturing cattle. So long as we kept to that in the kingdom of Gechekten, we were rich and happy. Now, ever since the Mongols have set themselves to cultivating the land, and building houses, they have become poor. The Kitats (Chinese) have taken possession of the country; flocks, herds, lands, houses, all have passed into their hands.

A Mongol horseman, as reported by Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Daily lifeInner Mongolia (Chakhar)1844

A minstrel's song: 'Return, O Timour!'

At a feast for the Moon festival a Mongol bard (toolholos) played a three-stringed fiddle and, at the priests' request, sang the 'Invocation to Timour', a lament for lost greatness that begs a hero to return and make the Mongols warriors again. Huc reproduces it in translation. It shows nineteenth-century Mongols remembering their imperial past in song.

"When the divine Timour dwelt within our tents, the Mongol nation was redoubtable and warlike; its least movements made the earth bend; its mere look froze with fear the ten thousand peoples upon whom the sun shines.

A Mongol minstrel, as rendered by Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

CitiesInner Mongolia (between Tolon-Noor and the Blue Town)1844

An abandoned city half buried in the soil

On the road to the Blue Town (Hohhot) the priests came upon a large abandoned walled city with towers and four gates facing the cardinal points. It was three-quarters buried by wind-blown soil and covered in turf, so that streets and buildings showed only as bumps in the ground. The only person there, a young Mongol shepherd, knew nothing except that it was called the Old Town.

we came, in the solitude, upon an imposing and majestic monument of antiquity, … a large city utterly abandoned. Its turreted ramparts, its watch towers, its four great gates, facing the four cardinal points, were all there perfect, in preservation, except that, besides being three-fourths buried in the soil, they were covered with a thick coating of turf.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

About Mongols (a local shepherd)

Keep in mind

Huc blames 'gradual accumulations of wind-borne soil' for the burial; he does not suggest any flood or catastrophe.

CitiesEastern Inner Mongolia1693

Stone lions, giant tortoises and a vanished Tartar king

Inside the ruined city lay heaps of brick, life-size stone statues, gravestones, stone lions and huge stone tortoises, and hares ran in through the four empty gates. Ides guessed a khan had once lived there. His Chinese companions said that many centuries earlier a Tartar king called Utaichan ruled here until a Chinese king drove him out. In the hills nearby he saw brick turrets that he took for old Tartar tombs.

Several large heaps of Bricks lay in the City, besides a great many Stone Statues, as big as the Life, of Men, and Idols, Grave-Stones, great Stone Lions, and Tortoises of an uncommon largeness … The Chinese relate that many hundred Years past Utaichan, a Tartarian King, governed here, but was conquered and driven out of his Country by a Chinese King.

Evert Ysbrants Ides (English translation, 1706)

BuildingsInner Mongoliaabout 1700 (reported 1785)

Mongol felt tents versus Manchu canvas tents

Grosier compares the two kinds of Tartar tent. Manchu tents are just single or double canvas, like European army tents. Mongol tents are round, shaped like a cut-off cone, covered in white or grey felt, with a smoke hole above a central fire; warm while the fire burns, freezing at night, stifling in summer. Three pages earlier he states flatly that the Mongols have 'neither towns, villages, nor houses', only wandering hordes living under tents.

These tents are however more commodious than the common tents of the Mantchews, which are composed only of double or single canvass, almost like those of our troops; these of the Moguls are circular, in form of the frustum of a cone, and covered with a large piece of white or gray felt. A round hole in the top gives a passage to the smoke,

Jean-Baptiste Grosier (English translation, 1788)

Compiled from Jesuit travellers, probably Gerbillon; Grosier did not see these tents.

Strange talesTenduc (Inner Mongolia, near the Yellow River bend)c. 1275

Gog and Magog are really Ung and Mungul

In Tenduc, which he says was once Prester John's capital, Polo meets the legend of Gog and Magog, the wild nations Alexander walled in. He offers a down-to-earth explanation: the local names Ung and Mungul, two peoples who lived there before the Tartar migration, had been misheard in the West as Gog and Magog. Yule's notes identify the Ung as the Ongut.

Here also is what we call the country of Gog and Magog ; they, however, call it Ung and Mungul, after the names of two races of people that existed in that Province before the migration of the Tartars. Ung was the title of the people of the country, and Mungul a name sometimes applied to the Tartars.

Marco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Ongut and Mongols

Keep in mind

Polo's 'Prester John' line here is King George of the Ongut, a Nestorian Christian prince.

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Chakhar, Red Banner)1844

How a Mongol tent is built

Huc visited Mongol families and describes their tent exactly: a cylinder about three feet high topped by a cone, a folding lattice of crossed bars below, a ring of poles meeting at the top like umbrella ribs, a thick covering, and a low folding door with a beam across the bottom. The next page describes the smoke-hole flap of felt and the men's and women's sides inside.

The Mongol tent, for about three feet from the ground, is cylindrical in form. It then becomes conical, like a pointed hat. The woodwork of the tent is composed below of a trellis-work of crossed bars, which fold up and expand at pleasure. Above these, a circle of poles, fixed in the trellis-work, meets at the top, like the sticks of an umbrella.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Chakhar)1844

'No towns, no edifices' in the land of grass

Crossing the prairies of the Chakhar, Huc sums up what makes Tartary unlike anywhere else: no towns, no buildings, no crafts, no farming and no forests, only endless grassland, lakes, rivers and mountains. In the next paragraph he describes encampments of tents appearing and vanishing as herders moved to fresh pasture.

There are no towns, no edifices, no arts, no industry, no cultivation, no forests; everywhere it is prairie, sometimes interrupted by immense lakes, by majestic rivers, by rugged and imposing mountains; sometimes spreading out into vast limitless plains.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Verbiest explains that the 'Tartarian Monarch that Conquered China' won over the Mongols partly by courting their Buddhist lamas, who persuaded their people to submit. Marriage as a contract of sale

Huc says Mongol marriages were arranged by parents through go-betweens, and that the bride's family was paid in horses, oxen, sheep, cloth, butter, brandy and flour. The Mongols themselves spoke of 'buying' and 'selling' a daughter. He goes on to describe a mock fight at the bride's door, a gallop to the new tent, polygamy and easy divorce.

The thing is indeed very clearly expressed in their language; they say, "I have bought for my son the daughter of so and so." "We have sold our daughter to such and such a family." The marriage contract is thus simply a contract of sale.
