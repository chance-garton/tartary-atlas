CitiesShigatse, Tibet1642

Gushri Khan's Tartars take Shigatse fortress

Das summarises Sumpa's account of how the Khoshut Mongol ruler Gushri Khan invaded Tibet, besieged the fortress of Samdruptse (Shigatse), took thirteen district forts and captured the king of Tsang, who was thrown into the Tsangpo river. Gushri was then proclaimed king of all Tibet and handed religious authority to the Dalai Lama's church. The 'Tartars' here are Oirat Mongols from Kokonor.

The seige of Sam-dub-tse (now fort Shi-ga-tse) by the Tartars and the capture of thirteen Jong by them under the lead of Gucri Khan … The king of Tsang surrendered himself. He was thrown into the river Tsang-po

Read itSarat Chandra Das, English analytical contents to his edition of Sumpa Khenpo's Tibetan text (Calcutta, 1908)

Mongol pilgrims bow nine times before the Lama

Turner watched a party of 'Kilmauks' (Kalmyk or Oirat Mongols) waiting outside the palace for a sight of the infant Lama. When he appeared they prostrated themselves nine times and then handed over bullion and goods. Turner learned that such offerings were one of the main sources of the Lamas' wealth.

There came this day, a party of Kilmauks, (Calmuc Tartars) for the purposes of devotion, and to make their offerings to the Lama … they dropped upon their knees, and struck their heads against the ground. This, with the same motions, was repeated nine times.

Saw itSamuel Turner (1800)

Strange talesShigatse (U-Tsang, central Tibet)1628

Shambhala placed in 'Great Tartary' on the maps

Joao Cabral, writing in 1628 after travelling in central Tibet (U-Tsang), says the kingdom is bounded on the north by the Tartars, with whom the king sometimes fights, and that many Tartars come there who say their religion is the same. He then decides that the famous kingdom of 'Xembala', which his companion Cacella had hoped was Cathay, is really the 'Great Tartary' of European maps. Xembala is the European spelling of Shambhala, the hidden kingdom of Tibetan Buddhist tradition, so here a legendary land is being matched to a map label.

On the north it borders on the Tartars, with whom this king sometimes quarrels, and many of them come here who say that their religion is the same. … Xembala, which in my opinion is not Cathay but that which on the maps they call Great Tartary, lies further off to the north.

Original wording

Pollo norte confina com os Tartaros com os quaes este Rei as vezes briga, e aqui vem muitos os quaes dizem que a sua Lei he a mesma. … Xembala que a meu ver não he o Catayo, mas aquella que nos Mapas chamão gram Tartarea fica mais desviada para o Norte.

Heard itJoao Cabral, S.J., Portuguese letter from Hugli, 17 June 1628, printed in Wessels's Appendix III

The rumour of the Lama's holy excrement

Stewart repeats a rumour, common in European books, that Tartar chiefs were given small portions of the Lama's excrement, kept it in gold boxes and mixed it into their food. On the same page he reports that Bogle 'strenuously insists' the Lama never gave such gifts, only little balls of consecrated flour, which credulous followers might then imagine to be something else.

It is even reported, that many of the Tartar chiefs receive certain presents, consisting of small portions of that, from him, which is ever regarded in all other persons as the most humiliating proof of human nature, and of being subject to its laws, and treasure it up with great reverence in gold boxes, to be mixed occasionally in their ragouts.

Legend or rumourJohn Stewart (Philosophical Transactions, 1777)

Strange talesTibet and Mongolia (and Europe's scholars)1801

Scholars who moved Atlantis to Spitsbergen

Pallas rejects the theory, pushed by the Augustinian friar Georgi, that Tibetan and Mongol Buddhism grew out of Nestorian Christianity or Manichaeism, calling it a heap of rubble glued together in a polemical spirit. He lumps it with other learned fantasies: the Chinese and Indians as an Egyptian colony, the Lapps as Israelites, pagan gods as biblical patriarchs, and the recent placing of Atlantis and the first seat of all learning as far north as Spitsbergen.

With such learned bombast the Chinese and Indians have before now been made into an Egyptian colony, the Lapps into Israelites, the idols of heathendom into patriarchs of the Bible, and only recently the Atlanteans, and with them the first seat of all learning, have been moved as far as Spitsbergen

Prester John was really the Dalai Lama

Strahlenberg offers one example of how getting a name right can clear up history: the famous 'great priest John'. He says this priest, who sits between India and China in the land of Tangut, is the one Kalmyks and Mongols call Dalai Lama, with the added title 'Jegan', meaning holy. On the next page he claims the thirteenth-century friar envoys Carpini and Rubruck turned that title into the Christian name John, which is why the king of Portugal later sought Prester John in India and then in Abyssinia.

of the so-called great Priest John; he who has his seat between India and China in the land of Tangut … is called Dalai Lama by all Kalmyks, Mongols and … from which it is now clearly to be seen that this name came from the Indians as well as from the Kalmyks and Mongols

von dem sogenannten großen Priester Johann; dieser, welcher seinen Sitz zwischen Indien und China im Lande Tan-guht … hat, wird von allen Kalimucken, Mungalen und … Dalai Lama genannt … woraus nun klärlich zu ersehen, daß dieser Nahme sowol von den Indianern, als auch von den Kalmücken und Mungalen hergekommen

Holy relics sold among the Tatar tribes

Warren Hastings's briefing memorandum for Bogle passes on what he had been told about Tibetan Buddhism: each Tatar tribe had its own living Buddha (Kutuktu), all of whom deferred to the Dalai Lama, 'so that his excrements are sold as charms at a great price among all the Tatar tribes of this religion'. It is a startling piece of hearsay from a governor who had never been there.

Among the different Tatar tribes which are of this religion, there are persons called Ku-tchuck-tus, who are likewise esteemed living Fos. … it is even pretended that the Ku-tchuck-tus admit a superiority in the Dalai Lama, so that his excrements are sold as charms at a great price among all the Tatar tribes of this religion.

Daily lifeShigatse (Tashilhunpo)1775

Chess with the Kalmuks at Teshu Lumbo

Bogle's happiest hours at the Panchen Lama's monastery were spent playing chess with a party of Kalmuk pilgrims. He notes their rules (no castling, no stalemate) and how they played as a team: whenever a 'Siberian' sat down to play, two or three friends put their 'great bare heads together' over every move. Bogle learned to recruit his own team of Tatars and beat them.

In my first trials of skill with the Tatars, I used often to come off loser. For when a Siberian sits down to chess, he gets two or three of his countrymen to assist him; they lay all their great bare heads together canvassing and consulting about every move.

Saw itGeorge Bogle (edited by Clements R. Markham, 1876)

About Kalmuks (Mongol pilgrims; Markham glosses them as Sokpas of north-east Tibet)

A Tibetan prophecy: the Mongol whose house is 'a wooden net'

The chronicle tells how the Tibetan lama Dagba Gyaltsan foretold to his nephew, the Sakya Pandita, that the ruler of 'the people called Monghol' would send an envoy named Dorda to summon him. The envoy's hat would look as if a sparrowhawk sat on it, his boots would be shaped like a pig's snout, and his house would look like a wooden net. The image fits the collapsible wooden lattice of the Mongol felt tent. The Sakya Pandita set out in 1244 and reached Godan Khan in 1247.

From now on, after some time, the ruler of the people called Monghol … will send you an envoy named Dorda from the northern region. His headgear will look as if a sparrowhawk were sitting on it, his boots will have the shape of a pig's snout, and his house will look like a wooden net

Original wording

Von nun an nach einiger Zeit wird der Beherrscher des Monghol genannten Volkes … aus der nördlichen Gegend einen Gesandten, Namens Dorda, an dich senden. Die Kopfbekleidung desselben wird aussehen, als sässe ein Sperber darauf, seine Stiefeln werden die Gestalt eines Schweinsrüssels haben, und sein Haus wird wie ein hölzernes Netz aussehen

The nameTibetabout 1730

Tibet: 'the kingdom of dwellers in houses'

The Capuchin della Penna, who lived in Lhasa for many years, explains the name Tibet as a Tatar (Mongol) word: Tangat katzar, which he says means the kingdom of people who live in houses, because the Tatars lived in tents of felt or yak-hair cloth while Tibetans lived in houses. Whatever the real etymology, it shows how people at the time drew the line between the two peoples by their dwellings.

The word Tibet is a Tatar corruption, the proper term being Thobot, or Tangat katzar. Tangat means dwellers in houses, and katzar country or kingdom; so the whole expression signifies "the kingdom of dwellers in houses," the Tatars dwelling in tents of felt, or of a coarse material woven from the hair of the yak or hairy ox, and the Tibetans in houses.

Heard itFrancesco Orazio della Penna, Capuchin missionary (English version in Markham's appendix, from Klaproth's edition)

About Tibetans contrasted with Tatars (Mongols)

Keep in mind

The etymology is della Penna's and is doubtful; the contrast in ways of living is the useful part.

Mongols descended from the Asuras

Among the opening legends of the Tibetan history, Das lists one that gives each great people a different origin: the Indians from the gods, the Chinese from the naga demigods, and the Mongolians from the asuras, the demons who fought the gods. It sits beside the story of the Tibetans' descent from a monkey.

The origin of the people of Aryavartta (India) from the gods, that of the Chinese from the Naga demi-gods, and that of the Mongolians from the Asura (demons who fought with the gods)

Legend or rumourSarat Chandra Das, English analytical contents to his edition of Sumpa Khenpo's Tibetan text (Calcutta, 1908)

Daily lifeTashilhunpo, Shigatse1783

Yellow caps and red caps across all Tartary

Watching monks in yellow robes and conical yellow hats, Turner explains that Tibetan Buddhism is split into yellow-hat and red-hat sects that between them cover 'almost the whole of Tartary'. The red hats allow their priests to marry. The yellow, favoured by the Emperor of China, is the most orthodox and powerful.

I notice this peculiarity of colour in their dress, as it is a distinction adopted, to mark one of the two religious sects that divide almost the whole of Tartary, from Turkistan to the eastern limits of this continent. The other colour is red; and the tribes are known as belonging to the red, or the yellow cap.

Saw itSamuel Turner (1800)

About Tibetan and Mongol Buddhists

Keep in mind

The claim that these sects cover all Tartary 'from Turkistan' is a large generalisation from what he was told.

Near his camp Turner met Dukba herders who lived only in tents. One brought him milk and butter. Their 200 to 300 yaks, owned by three families, grazed the mountains by day and at dusk were called in, tied in a double line before the tents and guarded by two big Tibetan mastiffs. BuildingsShigatse region, Tibet1774 to 1775

A lama's castle of stone or brick, with no stairs or windows

Relaying Bogle, who lived five months with the Panchen Lama, Stewart describes one of the Lama's castles: stone or brick, many courts and halls, rooms finished in Chinese style with gilding and varnish. The surprise is what is missing: upper floors are reached by wooden or iron ladders, and light comes through covered holes in the ceilings. Stewart does not call this a Tartar building; it is the seat of the lama the Tartars revered.

The castle is of stone or brick, with many courts, lofty halls, terraces, and porticos ; and the apartments are in general roomy, and highly finished in the Chinese stile, with gilding, painting, and varnish. There are two conveniencies to which they are utter strangers, stair-cases and windows.

Read itJohn Stewart, relaying George Bogle

About Tibetans (the Panchen Lama's court)

Keep in mind

Second hand, from Bogle's letters. The castle is Tibetan, not built by or for Tartars.

The Kalmyk story of Shakyamuni

In a footnote Strahlenberg records the Kalmyk account of 'Xacamuni', that is the Buddha Shakyamuni: once a prince in India, taken up to heaven alive for his holiness, whose celibate sons explain why lamas may not marry, and one of whom brought the religion to Tibet. It is a garbled but recognisable outline of Buddhist teaching as told to a Swedish captive.

They say that 4000 Years ago, he was only a Soveraign Prince, in India; But on Account of his unparallel'd Sanctity, God had taken him up to Heaven alive; But the Place whither he was translated, none of them pretend to know.

Heard itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg, author's footnote (English translation, 1738)

They stayed a month, went on to Lhasa, and then went home, about three months' journey north. Elsewhere Bogle says Kalmuk tribes came every year with camels loaded with Siberian furs and hides.

Among the rest of his votaries were a large caravan of Kalmuks, who offered up to his shrine talents of silver, furs, pieces of silk, and dromedaries. They remained about a month at Teshu Lumbo, and then proceeded to Lhasa, where, having spent about ten days, they returned to their own country, which was about three months' journey to the northward.

Strange talesTibet and Mongolia1768

Prester John was a mishearing

Like Strahlenberg before him, Fischer explains the legend of Prester John as a European mistake: the first Europeans in Tibet and Mongolia misheard a title, their fancy shaped it into 'Prêtre Jean' or 'Juan', and so it spread through the world. They forgot that Tibetans and Mongols spoke neither French, Italian nor Portuguese, and were pleased to find what they hoped for; later came strange etymologies and fables of an Indian 'Johannes Presbyter'.

the name Prêtre Jean or Juan was wrongly heard by the first Europeans who came there, then badly shaped by their fancy, and so sent out into the world. It did not occur to these good people that the Tibetans and Mongols among whom they found themselves understood neither French, nor Italian, nor Portuguese

Diesen guten leuten fiele nicht ein, daß die Tibetaner und Mogolen, unter denen sie sich befanden, weder französisch, noch italiänisch noch portugiesisch verstunden

Every northern empire was a 'Tatar confederacy'

In his memorandum Hastings sketches Tibetan history from Chinese sources and calls every northern power a 'Tatar confederacy': the Typa (Tuoba) in the fourth century, the Turks in the eighth and ninth, and on the next page the Mongols under Möngke Khan. It shows 'Tatar' used as a catch-all for any steppe people in the 1770s.

In the fourth century, the Tatar confederacy of the Typa subdued the north and east of Tibet. In the eighth and ninth centuries, when the Tatar confederacy of the Turks became feeble, the power of those nations, which now acknowledge the supremacy of the Dalai Lama, was very great.

Dice worn on every Tartar belt

Turner says that no journey, marriage or naming was done in Tibet without first seeking an omen, often from a monk skilled in divination. For small decisions people simply threw dice, which is why a pair of dice hangs from a Tartar belt. He cheerfully admits he travelled only when his own monk-diviner said so.

in trivial affairs, the mind is often governed by a casual cast of the die; and hence, dice are almost always found to constitute an appendage to a Tartar dress.

A lama builds a new wall against the conquerors

Describing 'Giegazze' (Shigatse), seat of the lama second only to the Dalai Lama, Desideri praises the lama's courage in 1717. When the conquerors of Lhasa, the Dzungar Tartars, marched on the town to plunder its rich temple and monastery, he quickly fortified both with a new wall, armed his people and drove them off. On the next page Desideri adds that the damage from the siege was repaired and the town enlarged in 1720.

Every one admired his firmness and courage when he offered heroic resistance to the conquerors of Lhasa, who after the sack of this town marched on Giegazze to make the fabulously rich temple and the monastery share the same fate. In a short time he fortified both with a new wall, armed his people and forced the enemy to retreat.

Heard itIppolito Desideri, in C. Wessels's English translation (1924)

Tents woven from yak hair

On the high plain beyond Phari and the Tongla mountain, Turner saw herds of yaks and 'Tartar tents' made entirely from the animals: the tent cloth woven from yak hair, and the guy ropes spun from it too. These are the black tents of Tibetan herders, quite different from the round felt yurts of the Mongols.

On its skirts were large herds of the bushy-tailed cattle, grazing; and we saw some Tartar tents, formed from the hair of these cattle, woven into cloth; the ropes also which fastened them to iron pins driven into the ground, were spun from the same materials.

'The high plains of Tartary' are not the centre of Asia

Explaining why he calls his book 'Central Asia', Humboldt warns that terms like 'Central Asia' and 'High Asia' are used loosely. Travellers from India who cross the Himalaya to the sacred lakes of Tibet, on the plateau once called 'the high plains of Tartary', are in fact as far from the true centre of Asia as a Siberian at Tobolsk.

When travellers from India cross the Himalaya … and reach the two Sacred Lakes, on the plateau that people once saw fit to call 'the high plains of Tartary', they are no doubt heading towards Central Asia, but they are still … as far from the centre of Asia as are the inhabitants of Siberia on the parallel of Tobolsk

A Lama's tomb with 'Chinese, or Tartar' decoration

Turner describes the mausoleum of the previous Panchen Lama at Tashilhunpo. He sorts its style carefully: Indian-looking pillars and arches inside, Chinese or 'Tartar' decoration outside (the tented canopy and imperial dragon), and a plain but solid stone building with cement. It is a good example of a traveller naming the traditions a building borrowed from.

the external decorations are of Chinese, or Tartar origin, similar to those of the watch tower, or temple of the former; the tented canopy and imperial dragon. As far as the mason has been concerned … a plain substantial building of stone, with cement.

Saw itSamuel Turner (1800)

A Tatar gift that made Bogle look Russian

An eccentric official decided that English dress was exactly like Russian dress, because Bogle wore a tunic and square cap that the Lama had been given by 'some Turki Tatars'. Markham adds a footnote calling this 'an instance of the confusion caused by the indiscriminate use of the word Tatar or Tartar'.

the tunic which I wore, and also my cap, the cut of which was four-square instead of being round, had been made a present of to the Lama by some Turki Tatars.

Tartar pilgrims from Russia, and letters from the Czar

According to Bogle, the Panchen Lama knew of only one European empire, Russia, because many of its Tartar subjects came to Tibet and the Czar had sent him letters and presents. The Lama admired Russia but could not believe it was a match for China ('Cathay').

Many of the Tartar subjects of Russia come to Thibet; and the Czar has even, at various times, sent letters and presents to the Lama.

The Tatars' mare's-milk beer

Della Penna compares Tibetan barley brandy with 'the beer which the Tatars make from the milk of mares', which he says looks and tastes like water but is surprisingly strong. It is a passing but clear reference to Mongol distilled milk spirit (arkhi) or kumiss.

a kind of brandy (mixed with the beer referred to), which does not differ greatly from the beer which the Tatars make from the milk of mares, and which is like water in appearance and taste, but is of considerable strength.

MixedFrancesco Orazio della Penna, Capuchin missionary (English version in Markham's appendix, from Klaproth's edition)

Daily lifenear Phari, Bhutan-Tibet borderSeptember 1783

Kumiss dried in bags under a horse's belly

Served soured milk (dhy) by the Lama of Phari, Turner compares it with the kumiss of the Tartars, made from mare's milk in the same way. He adds a hearsay detail: it is sometimes dried into chalk-like lumps, and the drying is done by hanging it in cloth bags under the horses' bellies.

The Kummuz of the Tartars is mare's milk, prepared by the same process: this is sometimes dried in masses till it resembles chalk; and is used to give a relish to the water which they drink, by solution with it. I have been told, that the operation of drying it, is sometimes performed by tying the Dhy tight in bags of cloth, and suspending it under their horses' bellies.

Heard itSamuel Turner (1800)

Tibetan robbers 'erroneously styled Kalmaks'

On a stage called 'the place of thieves', where robbers hid in underground burrows, Hodgson adds a footnote: the Catholic missionary Huc wrote much about Tibetan robbers, who were black-tent Tibetan nomads, 'erroneously styled Kalmaks'. It shows 'Kalmak' being stretched to cover any nomad raiders of Inner Asia.

M. Huc in his narrative speaks much of the robbers of Tibet, who, he says, are Kalos or black-tent nomadic Tibetans, erroneously styled Kalmaks. They are mounted gang robbers.
