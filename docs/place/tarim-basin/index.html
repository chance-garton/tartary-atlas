CitiesKarashahr1605

The scrap of paper that proved Cambalu was Peking

At Cialis, Goes met merchants coming back from a trade 'embassy' to Peking who had lodged in the same foreigners' palace as Ricci and his companions. They did not know the Jesuits' Chinese names, but they had rescued from a servant's sweepings a note written in Portuguese. That settled it: Cathay differed from China only in name, and the capital the Muslims called Cambalu was Peking. Cialis is usually identified with Karashahr in Xinjiang.

to make their story more certain they added a scrap of paper written by our men in Portuguese, which they had snatched from the rubbish of a servant sweeping out a room … with this news our Brother and his companion were much cheered, and from then on they had no doubt that Cathay differed from the kingdom of China in name only, and that the royal city the Saracens call Cambalu is Peking

CitiesTaklamakan Desertabout 1546

Hunters glimpse castles and minarets under the sand

Describing the deserts east and south of Kashgar and Khotan, Mirza Haidar says great towns once stood there, of which only the names Lob and Katak survive. Hunters of wild camels told of seeing foundations, castles, minarets, mosques and colleges that vanished under the sand again soon after. He does not say who built them.

In ancient times there were large towns in these [wastes] … all are buried under the sand. Hunters, who go there after wild camels, relate that sometimes the foundations of cities are visible, and that they have recognised noble buildings such as castles, minarets, mosques and colleges, but that when they returned a short time afterwards, no trace of these was to be found

Heard itMirza Haidar Dughlat (translated by E. Denison Ross, 1895)

About Unknown former townspeople of the Tarim basin

Keep in mind

Hunters' reports; the buildings named (minarets, mosques, colleges) are Islamic types, so these towns were not prehistoric in Mirza Haidar's mind.

CitiesChinese Tartary (Tarim Basin, Xinjiang)about 1830 to 1835

Walled towns built by forced labour

The pilgrims told Wathen that Chinese rule was very unpopular, and that resentment had grown because the Chinese were building large fortifications and walled towns with the forced labour of the local people. After the revolts of the 1820s the Qing did build new walled garrison towns in the region. Here the builders of the walls are named plainly: Qing authorities using conscripted local workers.

The feeling of dislike, with which the Chinese are regarded, has been latterly much increased, in consequence of their carrying on vast works of fortification, and building walled towns, by the forced labour of the natives.

Heard itW. H. Wathen, reporting his informants

About Turki-speaking Muslims ('Usbeks') under Qing rule

Keep in mind

The informants were Muslim subjects hostile to Qing rule. The corpus lists no printed page here; 662 comes from the page sequence.

Strange talesLop Nur region14th century (as told)

The town of Katak, buried by a rain of sand

A holy man warns the town of Katak of God's wrath and leaves. The muezzin goes back for a last call to prayer, sees sand falling like dry snow, and finds the town buried up to the minaret, from which he jumps to escape. Mirza Haidar adds that winds sometimes uncover the minaret or a house where everything is in order but the owner is white bones. The editor notes that sand does not fall like snow but that dunes do bury towns.

So he again ascended and, looking round, discovered that it was raining sand, and to such a degree that the whole town was covered ; after a little while he noticed that the ground was rising, and at last only a part of the minaret was left free.

Legend or rumourMirza Haidar Dughlat, retelling a story from Maulana Khwaja Ahmad (translated by E. Denison Ross, 1895)

About Muslim townspeople of Katak in the Tarim basin

Keep in mind

A pious legend passed down in one family; the editor's note compares it with real sand-buried ruins seen by Forsyth, Bellew and Przhevalsky.

The nameDzungaria and Eastern Turkestan1723

The peoples of the Dzungar realm, none of them called Tatars

Unkovsky lists the peoples under the Kontaisha: the Dzungars proper ('of the left hand'), Kyrgyz, Uriankhai, Telengits and others, the town-dwelling Bukharans of Yarkand, the Buruts (Kyrgyz) of lake Issyk-Kul and the Barabinians, who paid tribute to both Russia and the Dzungars. No one here is called a Tatar; the same land appears on Western maps of the time as part of 'Great Tartary'.

Under the Kontaisha's rule are various peoples, namely: his own people, called the Dzungars (or 'of the left hand'), the Kyrgyz, Uriankhai, Telengits, Mingats, Khoits, Khoshuts, the Bukharans of Erken (Yarkand), who live in towns, though the better sort roam with him, the Buruts, who roam around lake Tuskel (Issyk-Kul), and the Barabinians, who pay tribute to His Imperial Majesty and to the Kontaisha.

CitiesSouthern Tarim Basin, between Niya and Cherchen, Xinjiangabout 644

Empty cities of an old kingdom in the desert

Crossing the drifting sands east of Niya, travellers piled up bones to mark the way, and people heard singing or wailing that led them astray, which Xuanzang blames on demons. After 400 li he reached the 'old country of Tukhara', long empty, its cities all desolate. He credits the ruins to a vanished kingdom, not to any Turks or Tartars.

So those who come and go pile up the bones of the dead to mark the way. Water and grass are lacking and hot winds are frequent... At times one hears singing and whistling, or wailing and crying, and between what is seen and heard one is bewildered and does not know where one is going; many die this way, which is the work of demons and goblins. Going 400 li or so one reaches the old country of Tukhara. The country has long been empty and its cities are all in ruins.
