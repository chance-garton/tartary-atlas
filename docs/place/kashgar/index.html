Daily lifeKashgar1069 to 1070

Herdsmen give kumis, milk, wool and cheese

The poem's chapter on how a ruler should treat the keepers of the horse herds lists what they provide: food, drink and horses to ride, and kumis (fermented mare's milk), milk, wool, butter, sour milk and cheese, food and clothing and coarse wool for ropes. The poet calls them useful and honest but 'without knowledge', people from whom a prince should not expect law or manners. It is a courtier's view of the herding life that fed the court.

3 Food, drink, horses to ride … stallions come from these (people), and also … 4 Kumis, milk, wool, butter, sour milk and cheese, food and clothing, as well as coarse wool for ropes.

Working translation

Original wording

3 Zu essen, zu trinken, Pferd zu reiten . . . . . Hengste Stammt von diesen her, so auch . . . . 4 Kimis, Milch, Wolle, Schmalz, saure Milch und Käse, Speise und Kleider, sowohl als grobe Wolle für Stricke.

Yusuf Khass Hajib, Kutadgu Bilig (1069 to 1070), in Vambery's German translation (1870)

About Turkic herders of the Qarakhanid realm

BuildingsKashgar1069 to 1070

'The wanderer builds no farmsteads and no palaces'

A line of the poem, quoted in Vambery's glossary under the word for 'nomad', says that the person who moves from place to place does not build farmsteads or palaces. The poet is contrasting nomad life with the settled court life of Kashgar, where palaces (saray) were built. It is an eleventh-century Turkic writer's own view that nomads and palace-builders were different ways of living.

köchükli kishi etmes urtu saray: he who moves from one place to another builds no farmsteads and no palaces. (chapter 87)

Working translation

Original wording

köcükli kisi etmes urtu saraj. Der von einem Orte zum andern ziehet, bauet keine Gehöfte und Schlösser. (87)

Yusuf Khass Hajib, Kutadgu Bilig (1069 to 1070), verse quoted with German translation in Vambery's glossary (1870)

About Turkic nomads of the Qarakhanid realm

Keep in mind

A moral saying about the uncertainty of worldly life rather than an ethnographic report; Vambery's readings are now outdated (Arat 1947).

BuildingsKashgarearly 18th century

Stone houses and mud-brick ovens in Little Bukharia

The anonymous author, who says he lived in Little Bukharia, describes its townspeople's houses as built of stone and fairly good, with few furnishings: no tables or chairs, only Chinese chests on which mattresses were laid. Slaves bought from the Kalmyks, Russians and others cooked in iron pots set into a hearth, and some houses had small ovens of clay or unbaked brick, like their walls.

The houses of the Bukharans are of stone and passably good. Their furniture is scanty and plain. They have neither tables nor chairs. … Some also have small ovens, which are made, like the rest of their walls, of clay or of unbaked bricks.

Working translation

Original wording

Les maisons des Bouchars sont de pierre, & passablement bonnes. Leurs meubles sont en petit nombre, & de peu d'ornement. Ils n'ont ni tables ni chaises. … Il y en a qui ont aussi de petits fours, qui se font, comme le reste de leurs murailles, de terre grasse ou de briques crues.

Anonymous author of the 'Relation de la Boucharie'

About Bukharans (townspeople of Little Bukharia) under Kalmyk rule

Keep in mind

The text says both stone houses and walls of clay or mud brick; which was usual is not clear.

Daily lifeKashgarearly 18th century

Cutting out the illness with a knife

The Relation describes the Bukharans' cure for sickness: a mullah reads from a book, blows on the patient several times and makes passes with a sharp knife around his face, believing this cuts the root of the illness, which they hold to be the Devil's work. The dead, it adds, are buried with a Koran on the chest in a pleasant grove fenced with a hedge or palisade.

When a Bukharan falls ill, this is the remedy he uses: a mullah reads him a passage from some book, blows on him several times, and with a well-sharpened knife makes various passes all round the sick man's face. They imagine that by this operation they cut the root of the illness, which they say is the work of the Devil.

Working translation

Original wording

Lorsqu'un Bouchar tombe malade, voici le remede dont il se sert; Un Mula lui lit un passage de quelque livre, souffle à plusieurs reprises sur lui, & d'un couteau bien aiguisé fait plusieurs gesticulations tout autour du visage du Malade. Ils s'imaginent que par cette opération ils coupent la racine de la maladie, qu'ils disent d'ailleurs être l'ouvrage du Diable.

Anonymous author of the 'Relation de la Boucharie'

About Bukharans (Muslim townspeople of Little Bukharia)

Keep in mind

An outsider's description of a healing rite, written with some condescension.

The nameKashgar1069 to 1070 (prose preface added later)

One book, many names: 'the Turkish Shahnameh'

The prose preface of the Herat manuscript says the book was finished 'in the land of Kashgar' and presented to Bughra Khan, who made the author his chamberlain, hence his title Khass Hajib. It boasts that the Chinese called it 'The Manners of Kings', the Iranians 'the Turkish Shahnameh', and the 'Turanians' Kutadgu Bilig. The Turks here are a named people with their own literary language, 'the Turkish tongue' of the eastern lands.

The people of Chin called it Adab al-Muluk, 'the manners of princes'; the scholars of the prince of Machin called it Ayn al-Mamlaka, 'the eye of the realm'; the easterners called it Zinat al-Umara, 'the ornament of princes'. The Iranians called it the Turkish Shahnameh.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Leute von Cin haben es Adab ul Muluk, Sitten der Fürsten genannt; die Gelehrten des Fürsten von Macin haben Ajn ul Memleket, das Auge des Reiches geheissen; die Morgenländer haben es Zinet un Umera, Zierde der Fürsten betitelt. Die Iranier hiessen es das türkische Schah Nameh

Anonymous prose preface to the Kutadgu Bilig, in Vambery's German translation (1870)

About Qarakhanid Turks

Keep in mind

The preface is a later addition praising the book, not the author's own words; its claims about foreign names are a boast.

CitiesKashgar1758-1759

Walled Muslim towns of Kashgar and Yarkand, unlike the Eleuths

Amiot explains why the Muslim princes of Yarkand and Kashgar thought they could resist the Qing after the Eleuths fell: unlike the nomad Eleuths they had firearms, walled towns and strongholds, and Muslim neighbours to help them. The Qing generals took both towns; the younger Khoja's head was sent to Peking in a box and displayed over the palace's Wu Men gate.

They had the advantage over the Eleuths of firearms; they had walled towns and some strongholds, and they could be helped by the other Mahometans, their neighbours.

Working translation

Original wording

Ils avoient par dessus les Eleuths l'avantage des armes à feu, ils avoient des villes murées, quelques places fortes, & ils pouvoient être secourus par les autres Mahométans leurs voisins.

Joseph-Marie Amiot (note to the Eleuth monument)

About Muslim townspeople of 'Little Bukharia' under the two Khojas; contrasted with the Eleuths

Keep in mind

These town-dwellers are the Turkic Muslims of the Tarim oases, not Tartars in Amiot's usage; the passage is useful for the contrast.

Daily lifeTian Shan, west of Kashgar1813

Kirghiz snow-treaders keep the pass open

In winter the snow in the passes west of Kashgar lay a man's height deep or more. Izzet Ullah describes Kirghiz posted along the road whose job was to trample the snow hard so caravans could cross. Even so, several horses died, including his own.

The Kirghizes tread the snow down so as to make it hard, and then the caravans pass; a certain number of Kirghizes are appointed to this duty,

Mir Izzet Ullah (translated by H. H. Wilson)

Daily lifeKashgar1069 to 1070

Merchants and 'the caravan from Cathay'

In the chapter on merchants the poet advises the prince to welcome them: they travel from east to west, bring the finest clothes and 'wonders', and without them nobody could dress well in winter or see strings of pearls. When 'the caravan of Cathay' (China) sets out, thousands of gifts follow. It places the Turkic court of Kashgar on the great trade road between China and the west.

7 If there were no merchant who travels the world, how could you clothe yourself in the middle of harsh winter? 8 When Cathay's caravan sets out, it is a caravan from which thousandfold gifts will come.

Working translation

Original wording

7 Gäbe es keinen Kaufmann, der die Welt durchzieht, Wie könntest du dich kleiden in der Mitte des rauhen Winters? 8 Wenn Chatai's Karavane dahinzieht, so ist es eine Karavane, Von wo tausendfache Spenden kommen sollen.

Yusuf Khass Hajib, Kutadgu Bilig (1069 to 1070), in Vambery's German translation (1870)

CitiesKashgarQing survey, 1770s (published 1829)

Kashgar, the 'many-coloured brick' city

The Qing gazetteer describes Kashgar as one of the great cities of Turkestan, so famous that foreigners called all East Turkestanis 'Kashgarians'. It lists its Manchu and Chinese garrison, taxes paid in cash, grain and cotton cloth, silk and gold-thread products, and skilled jade polishers and goldsmiths. A note explains the name as 'many-coloured brick building', because the city had many buildings of coloured brick.

Kash means many-coloured, gar a brick building. There are many buildings of coloured brick here, which is where the name comes from. It is also said that the word Kashgar means rich.

Working translation

Original wording

Кашб значитъ разноцвѣтный, гаръ кирпичное зданіе. Здѣсь много разноцвѣтныхъ кирпичныхъ зданій, отъ чего и названіе дано. Еще говорятъ, что слово Кашгаръ означаетъ богатый.

Note in the Chinese original ('Author's note'), translated by Iakinf Bichurin, 1829

About Turkestanis (Uighurs) under Qing rule

Keep in mind

A folk etymology from the Chinese compiler; the true origin of the name Kashgar is uncertain.

The nameKashgar (Little Bucharia)1820 to 1821

Turkestan speech sounds like Kazan Tatar

While in Peking, Timkovsky talked through an interpreter with men from Little Bucharia (the Tarim basin towns) and noticed that their language was very like that of the Kazan Tatars, 'who are also of Turkish origin'. Here 'Tartar' means the Turkic-speaking Muslims of the Volga, a different people entirely from the Mongols he had just crossed.

In the conversations I had in China, through the medium of an interpreter, with natives of Little Bucharia who had come to Peking, I observed that their language much resembles that of the Tartars of Kasan, who are also of Turkish origin.

George Timkowski (English translation, 1827)

Daily lifeLittle Bukhara (Yarkand and Kashgar)1730s

Frozen half-moon dumplings for winter journeys

Part 2 of Weber's work prints an account 'Of Tartarian Bukhara' by an author who says he had been in Little Bukhara himself. He notes that the men's dress differs little from the Tartar, that they eat with their fingers and wooden spoons, drink tea with milk, salt and butter, and fill half-moon-shaped pastries with chopped meat, which they freeze and carry in a sack on long winter journeys to boil into soup.

They fill a kind of pastry, shaped like a half moon, with it. On long journeys, especially in winter, they take a good supply of these pastries with them and carry them in a sack. When this food has first frozen thoroughly in the air, it is cooked in boiling water, and a fairly good soup is made of it.

Working translation

Original wording

Sie füllen mit denselben eine Art Pasteten, die wie ein halber Mond gestaltet sind. Auf weiten Reisen, insonderheit in Winter, nehmen sie einen guten Vorraht von diesen Pasteten mit, und tragen sie bey sich in einem Sakke. Wenn nun diese Speise vorher an der Luft rechtschaffen durchgefroren ist, so wird sie in einem siedenden Wasser gekocht, und eine ziemlich gute Suppe davon gemacht.

Anonymous account 'Von der Tartarischen Bucharey' in Weber, part 2 (1739)

About Bukharans of Little Bukhara (Turkic Muslims of the Tarim oases, then under Zunghar rule)

Keep in mind

The author is not named; he says he had visited Little Bukhara.

BuildingsTian Shan, between Kashgar and Osh1813

Felt tents pitched beside a domed tomb

Crossing the mountains west of Kashgar in deep winter, Izzet Ullah notes Kirghiz felt tents lining the road (p. 321). At the stage called Mizar Gumbaz he found a domed building with Kirghiz tombs and the living Kirghiz camped in tents beside it: permanent structures for the dead, portable ones for the living.

Mizar Gumbuz, … west by north, eight hours. A building with a cupola and Kirghiz tombs; their tents were also pitched near the building; supplies plentiful;

Mir Izzet Ullah (translated by H. H. Wilson)
