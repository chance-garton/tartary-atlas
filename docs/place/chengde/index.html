BuildingsChengde1793

A Tibetan temple in Tartary that looked European

Near the summer palace the party visited the 'Poo-ta-la', the great lama temple the emperor had built (today's Putuo Zongcheng temple, modelled on the Potala of Lhasa). Staunton found it unlike any Chinese building and oddly like a European front, eleven stories of windows high, with a golden chapel inside whose roof plates were said to be solid gold. He credits the building to the emperor himself.

the outside very much resembling the front of an European edifice. It was of great height, containing eleven rows of windows, implying as many stories, or ranges of apartments, one above another. … The square, or principal building of Poo-ta-la, includes a quadrangle, in the centre of which is the golden chapel, so termed from the materials in which it, apparently at least, abounds.

Saw itGeorge Leonard Staunton

About Manchus and Mongols (Qing court); Tibetan Buddhist lamas

Keep in mind

The 'European' look is Staunton's comparison; the page before says the temples round Zhe-hol 'had been erected by the Emperor'. The gold was 'said to be' solid.

BuildingsChengde (Jehol)1793

Temples built in Tartary by the Qianlong Emperor

Describing the Qianlong Emperor, Barrow cites as proof of his piety the many splendid Buddhist temples he built and endowed in 'oriental Tartary', the grandest being the Potala-style monastery at Jehol (Chengde), where Macartney was received.

The sincerity of his faith may partly be inferred from the numerous and splendid temples he built and endowed in different parts of oriental Tartary, of which the Poo-ta-la, or convent of Budha at Gehol, is the most magnificent.

Heard itJohn Barrow

BuildingsChengde1793

An emperor who preferred a tent to a palace

The Qianlong emperor received the British envoy not in a hall of his summer palace but in a great tent in the garden, with small round tents pitched in front for the tributary princes. Staunton explains the choice: the Manchu rulers had adopted Chinese customs but kept a fondness for their old movable dwellings when they were on 'Tartar ground'.

The Tartar dynasty, in conforming in most instances, to the customs of a much more numerous and more civilized, tho vanquished nation, retained still a predilection for its own ancient manners, in which occasionally, and upon Tartar ground, it took a pleasure in indulging. The moveable dwelling of a tent was, more than a permanent palace of stone and timber, the favourite residence of a Tartar sovereign.

Saw itGeorge Leonard Staunton

About Manchus (the Qing 'Tartar dynasty')

Keep in mind

The explanation of the emperor's motive is Staunton's interpretation.

BuildingsChengde (Jehol), Hebei, ChinaSeptember 1793

The emperor's felt tent and its 'Tartar' name, Mungkubo

Before dawn on the day of the audience, the embassy waited in the imperial park, where 'Tartar tents' stood on the south side. They needed no supporting poles: a framework of split bamboo skilfully woven together, with thick coarse felt stretched over it. The largest, hung with yellow and furnished with carpets, painted lanterns and a throne, was called by the Chinese only by its Tartar name, Mungkubo, which the interpreter could not explain. Most of the courtiers who crowded in were Tartars.

They consist of splints of bamboo cane skilfully woven into one another, over which a kind of thick coarse felt is stretched. One was far higher and broader than the others, hung with yellow … The Chinese gave this tent exclusively the Tartar name Mungkubo, whose real meaning our interpreter could not tell me.

Eins war weit höher und breiter als die andern, gelb überhängt … Die Chinesen gaben diesem Zelte ausschliesslich den Tartarischen Nahmen: Mungkubo, dessen eigentliche Bedeutung unser Dolmetscher mir nicht zu sagen wusste.

Jehol: kept outside the palace gates

The guard marched through Jehol ('Gehor'), a town about three miles long, to the palace with the presents, but only the ambassador and gentlemen were allowed in. The soldiers went back disappointed, though Holmes says the palace's extent and elegance against the mountains, lit by the rising sun, were beyond description.

none, except his Excellency and the gentlemen, were suffered to enter the gates ; all the others returned mortified and terribly disappointed … the extent and elegance of it was beyond description, and contrasted with the adjacent mountains and precipices, the scene was great and beautiful indeed

Saw itSamuel Holmes (diary printed 1798)

BuildingsChengde (Jehol), Hebei, ChinaSeptember 1793

The golden-roofed 'Putelah' temple at Jehol

The most remarkable of Jehol's seven lama and bonze temples was the 'Putalah', the temple with the golden roof, said to have more than eight hundred lamas, set on a hill commanding the valley. Hüttner climbed two long stairways into a wide court paved with dressed stone, with a square temple about 100 feet high in the middle and the lamas' rooms along the inner sides of the outer building. He found it overloaded with gilding and glaring colours; many lamas sat on the ground singing what he calls Tartar chants. It is the Putuo Zongcheng temple, built by the Qianlong emperor as a copy of the Potala at Lhasa.

One climbs by two long stairways into a wide courtyard paved with dressed stone, in the middle of which stands the temple. It forms a regular square and is about 100 feet high. Along the four inner sides of the outer building are the rooms of the lama priests. The eye is offended here in every direction by gilding and glaring colours; everything is overloaded.

Working translation

Original wording

Man steigt auf zwei langen Treppen in einen weiten, mit Quadersteinen gepflasterten Hof, auf dessen Mitte der Tempel steht. Er bildet ein regelmässiges Viereck, und ist etwa 100 Fuss hoch. An den vier innern Seiten des Aussengebäudes sind die Zimmer der Lamapriester. Das Auge wird hier in jeder Richtung von Vergoldungen und grellen Farben beleidigt; alles ist überladen.

Saw itJohann Christian Hüttner (1797)

The nameNorth China and Jehol1793

Tartars and Chinese: one government, one language, two characters

In a section headed 'Character of the Tartars', Hüttner says mixed marriages, a single government and a common language had made Tartars and Chinese hard to tell apart in their customs. Yet he found the Tartar, as travellers described him, rough, hard and blunt: sturdier in build, clumsier in manners and less clean in his dwellings than the Chinese, but without the deceitful double-dealing he attributes to his Chinese neighbour. On the next page the two mandarins conducting the embassy blamed the stubbornness and pride of the Tartars for trouble getting supplies in Tartary.

Mixed marriages of Tartars and Chinese, one and the same government, one and the same language naturally produce the same customs. But since a people can never wholly deny its own character, here too some traits shine through that clearly distinguish the Tartar from the Chinese. Travellers describe the former as rough, hard and blunt; and so we really found him.

Working translation

Original wording

Vermischte Ehen der Tartarn und Chinesen, einerlei Regierung, einerlei Sprache bringen natürlicher Weise dieselben Sitten hervor. Da aber ein Volk seinen eigenthümlichen Charakter nie gänzlich verläugnen kann, so leuchten auch hier einige Züge hervor, die den Tartarn merklich von dem Chinesen unterscheiden. Reisende beschreiben den erstern als roh, hart und gerade; und so fanden wir ihn wirklich.

Saw itJohann Christian Hüttner (1797)

Jehol: the emperor's hunting palaces in 'Chinese Tartary'

Amiot explains why the painter-brother Attiret was summoned north: some ten thousand Dzungars had fled their ruler's usurper with families and baggage to submit to the emperor, who settled them in 'Chinese Tartary' and ordered their leaders to Gé-hol (Jehol, modern Chengde), where he hunted each year and had palaces nearly as fine as those around Peking. Attiret, it was guessed, was wanted to paint the portraits of the leading newcomers at the ceremony.

The Emperor ordered them to go to Gé-hol, a place in Chinese Tartary where he goes every year for the pleasure of hunting, and where he has palaces almost as beautiful as those around Peking.

Working translation

Original wording

L'Empereur leur ordonna de se rendre à Gé-hol, lieu de la Tartarie Chinoise où il va chaque année pour prendre le divertissement de la chasse, & où il a des palais presque aussi beaux que ceux qui sont aux environs de Peking.

Saw itJoseph-Marie Amiot, letter to Père de La Tour (Peking, 17 October 1754)

The nameChengde1793

Kalmyk chiefs who fled Russia for China

At the emperor's birthday theatricals most of the guests who came to meet the British envoy were Tartars, since few Chinese were invited to Zhe-hol. Among them were two chiefs of 'Calmoucks' whose people had recently left Russian rule on the Caspian and migrated to Chinese Tartary, an event of 1771.

Most of them were Tartars ; few original Chinese being invited to Zhe-hol. There were likewise two Mussulmen, chiefs of some hordes of Calmoucks, who, not long since, on occasion of discontent or misunderstanding with the government of Russia, migrated in great numbers from the northern coast of the Caspian sea into Chinese Tartary, and put themselves under the Emperor's protection.

Saw itGeorge Leonard Staunton

About Manchus and Mongols at court; Kalmyks from the Volga

Keep in mind

The Kalmyks (Torghuts) who made the 1771 migration were Tibetan Buddhists, so calling their chiefs 'Mussulmen' looks like a confusion.

CitiesChengde1793

Hovels and palaces at the emperor's summer town

Zhe-hol (Jehol, modern Chengde), beyond the Great Wall, was the Qing emperors' summer capital in Tartary. Staunton contrasts the poor, crooked town with the grandeur of the imperial palaces, gardens and temples just beyond it. He adds that the principal buildings 'in this part of Tartary' differed little from those of China.

The town of Zhe-hol, excepting the houses of mandarines, consisted of miserable hovels full of people. The streets also were crooked, unpaved, and dusty. Immediately beyond it, the imperial garden, the palaces, the temples displayed much grandeur; and magnificence and wretchedness knew no medium.

Saw itGeorge Leonard Staunton

CitiesChengde (Jehol), Hebei, ChinaSeptember 1793

Jehol: wretched huts beside imperial palaces

Hüttner contrasts the emperor's summer seat with the town that served it: apart from the palaces, the magnificent park and the rich lama temples, the town offered only miserable huts, crooked streets and great dirt. He praises the fertile valley ringed by mountains, which he thought could carry vineyards and orchards. A useful corrective to any idea of Jehol as a grand 'Tartar' city: the splendour was the court's, not the town's.

one finds nothing in the town but wretched huts, crooked streets and great filth. The imperial palaces, the magnificent park and the rich lama temples make a great contrast with it!
