The nameHazarajat (between the Hindu Kush and Bamiyan)1832

The Tatar Hazaras, said to descend from Genghis's army

Burnes notes a Hazara tribe of about a thousand families called Tatar Hazaras, whom tradition made descendants of Genghis Khan's army. He points out that the only other people who called themselves Tatar were the Nogais on the Russian frontier. Earlier (vol. I, p.178) he says the Hazaras looked like Chinese and were 'Tartars by descent'.

Tradition states these people to be descendants of Jengis Khan's army, but their name of Tatar deserves remark, since the only other tribe so denominated by the people themselves is the Nogai on the frontiers of Russia.

Heard itAlexander Burnes (1834)

Keep in mind

The nameKunduz1837

Where the word 'Tartary' came from

Wood suggests the peoples of Central Asia descend from the ancient Huns and that most of their names came from a person: Tartar, Nogai, Uzbek, Moghul. 'Tartar', he says, had been turned into 'Tartary', a catch-all word for what the Romans called Scythia, yet he never heard the Uzbeks of Kunduz use it, and he blames the habit for much confusion.

Most of their names have had a personal origin, such as Tartar, Noghai, Uzbek, and Moghul. The first, corrupted to Tartary, has now become a generic term for the Scythia of the Roman historians, though I do not remember to have heard it used by the Uzbeks of Kunduz. From this custom much ambiguity has resulted.

Read itJohn Wood

About Peoples of Central Asia: Kalmyks, Kirghiz, Uzbeks, Turkmens

Keep in mind

The Hun ancestry and the 'personal origin' of the names are Wood's own theories from reading, not local testimony.

BuildingsAybak (Heibuk), AfghanistanMay 1832

Beehive houses of the first 'Tartar' villages

North of the Hindu Kush Burnes saw houses with domes instead of flat roofs, a hole in the top for smoke, so that a village looked like 'a cluster of large brown beehives'. He explains the style by the lack of timber. The people too had changed: conical skull-caps, long brown boots, and unveiled village women he calls fair and handsome, 'though they were Tartars'.

they have domes instead of terraces, with a hole in the roof as a chimney; so that a village has the appearance of a cluster of large brown beehives. The inhabitants adopt this style of building, as wood is scarce.

Saw itAlexander Burnes (1834)

BuildingsBamiyanMay 1832

The caves and giant statues of Bamiyan

At Bamiyan Burnes found a valley honeycombed for eight miles with caves, still home to most of the people, and the two great statues. He reports local traditions: the caves were the work of a king named Julal, the statues were made by 'Kaffirs' (infidels) about the time of Christ, or by the Pandavas of Hindu epic. He notes a theory that they were Buddhist, which the long ears made likely. He does not credit them to Tartars.

A detached hill in the middle of the valley is quite honeycombed by them … It is called the city of Ghoolghoola, and consists of a continued succession of caves in every direction, which are said to have been the work of a king named Julal.

Saw itAlexander Burnes (1834)

CitiesBalkh, AfghanistanSeptember 1222

Balkh, empty but for barking dogs

Travelling by night after crossing the Amu Darya on the way to Chinggis's camp, the party passed Balkh, a very large city whose people had recently revolted and fled, and all they heard inside was the barking of dogs. Bretschneider's note adds, from Rashid al-Din, that Balkh fell to Chinggis in 1221 and most of its people were massacred.

travelling during the night, we passed Ban-li a very large city, the inhabitants of which had revolted not long ago, and fled. We heard the barking of the dogs in the city.

Saw itLi Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Inhabitants of Balkh under Mongol rule

Keep in mind

The diarist's 'revolted and fled' and Rashid al-Din's massacre are two versions of the city's fate; the travellers only passed by in the dark.

CitiesKunduz, Afghanistanabout 644

A Turkish king who will not stay in his own capital

Kunduz (Huo) had a walled chief town and farmland, but its king was a Turk who ruled all the small kingdoms south of the Iron Gates. He did not live in the town: he kept moving 'like a bird'. Chains of neighbouring states in the old Tukhara lands are each described as having a capital but 'no chief ruler', being dependent on the Turks.

The king is of the Turkish clan; he governs all the little kingdoms to the south of the Iron Gates. He constantly shifts his dwelling, like a bird (his bird-dwelling), not constantly occupying this town.

Saw itXuanzang and Bianji (translated by Samuel Beal, 1884)

Daily lifeHimatala, Badakhshan, Afghanistanabout 644

Dressed like the Turks, crowned with a wooden horn

In Himatala, near Badakhshan, people behaved and dressed like the Turks, in wool, skin and felt. Married women wore a wooden horn about three feet long on the head, with two branches standing for the husband's father and mother; when one parent-in-law died a branch was removed, and when both were dead the horn was given up.

In respect of their modes of behaviour and forms of etiquette, their clothes of wool, and skin, and felt, they are like the Turks. Their wives wear upon their head-dress a wooden horn about three feet or so in length. It has two branches (a double branch) in front, which signify father and mother of the husband.

Saw itXuanzang and Bianji (translated by Samuel Beal, 1884)

Strange talesTaloqan (Talikan), Afghanistanwinter 1824 to 1825

Reading Gibbon on Genghis Khan to an Uzbek holy man

Sheltering with a respected Uzbek pir (khaja) at Talikan, Moorcroft tried to translate into Persian the chapters of Gibbon's Decline and Fall about Genghis Khan and Timur. His host corrected the Persian style, was surprised a European knew so much about the 'Moguls', and admitted many facts were new to him. A European history of the Mongols thus travelled back to Central Asia.

Whilst under his protection, I attempted to render into Persian, for his use, the chapters of Gibbon which treat of Jangez Khan and Timur, in which the khaja took much interest, and corrected the style of the translation. He was much surprised that a European should have known so much of the history of the Moguls, and admitted that many of the facts were new to him

Saw itWilliam Moorcroft (edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)

The nameHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

Hill people with 'Tartar' faces who say they came from the Moguls

Elphinstone is puzzled by the Aimaqs and Hazaras: they live in the heart of Afghanistan but look, speak and behave quite differently. They have no history of their own origin and speak a dialect of Persian, yet their features and a tradition point to Mongol ancestry. He goes on to cite the historian Abu'l-Fazl, who says they are remnants of a Mongol prince's army.

Their features, however, refer them at once to the Tartar stock, and a tradition declares them to be the offspring of the Moguls. They are, indeed, frequently called by the name of Moguls to this day, and they are often confounded with the Moguls and Chagatyes, who still reside in the neighbourhood of Heraut.

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

BuildingsHimatala, Badakhshan, Afghanistanabout 644

Dozens of fortified towns, yet the people live in felt tents

Because the land bordered the Turks, its people took up Turkish ways and were raided by them. They scattered, held dozens of strong walled towns each under its own chief, and lived in domed felt tents, moving about. Towns and tents side by side, in the same breath. Beal's English of this passage runs across pages 290 to 291.

[They took on] their [the Turks'] customs, and were also raided by them, and so defended their own borders. Therefore the people of this country are scattered in different districts, in several tens of strong walled towns, each with its own chief. They live in domed felt tents and move about from place to place.

Daily lifeHindu Kush (near Kabul), AfghanistanMay 1222

'Have you a medicine of immortality?'

At his first audience, in the Khan's camp south of the Hindu Kush, Chinggis welcomed the sage, fed him and asked outright for an elixir of immortality; Changchun answered that there were ways to preserve life but no medicine for immortality, and the Khan praised his honesty. At a later audience the sage firmly refused to drink the tung lo set before him, which Bretschneider explains as kumiss or other fermented milk (p.94). His teaching was translated into Mongol by the councillor A-hai and written down in both Chinese and Mongol (p.95).

After this he asked: "Sainted man, you have come from a great distance. Have you a medicine of immortality?" The master replied: "There are means for preserving life, but no medicines for immortality." Chinghiz lauded him for his sincerity and candour. By imperial order two tents were pitched for the master east of the emperor's tents.

Saw itLi Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Strange talesBalkh, Afghanistanearly 7th century

A Turkish prince speared in a dream for raiding a monastery

The New Monastery at Balkh was so rich that rulers coveted it. A son of the Turkish Yabghu khan (named on the previous page) led his whole horde to plunder it and camped nearby. That night he dreamed that the guardian god Vaisravana drove a long halberd through his chest; he woke in pain, sent for the monks to beg forgiveness, and died before the messenger returned.

[He gathered] his tribes and led his troops to fall on the monastery by surprise, meaning to seize its treasures. He camped in the open not far from here. That night he dreamed he saw the god Vaisravana, who said: 'What power have you, that you dare to destroy this monastery?' and drove a long halberd through him from chest to back. The khan woke in terror, with a pain in his heart.

CitiesKunduz1837

Kunduz: a capital of mud huts and tents

The capital of the powerful Uzbek ruler Murad Beg was, Wood says, one of the most wretched towns in his lands: a few hundred mud hovels mixed with straw sheds and Uzbek tents, with fields running into the town and a fortress that was just a walled mound. On the next page he describes the ruler's citadel as an irregular building of kiln-dried brick with a moat.

Kunduz, though the capital of Murad Beg, is one of the most wretched in his dominions. Five or six hundred mud hovels contain its fixed population, while dotted amongst these, and scattered at random over the suburbs, are straw-built sheds intermixed with the Uzbek tent or Kirgah. … Nothing, in short, can be imagined less resembling a metropolis.

Saw itJohn Wood

About Uzbeks of Murad Beg's state

Keep in mind

Wood openly links the town's poverty to his low opinion of Uzbek character; the physical description is what he saw.

Strange taleswestern Afghanistan (Aimaq country)c. 1809

Victims' blood smeared on faces and beards

The Aimaqs are quiet under their chiefs, Elphinstone says, but savage in war. He reports throwing prisoners off cliffs and shooting them with arrows, and one occasion, told him by a man who was there, when they drank their victims' blood. A footnote (garbled) compares this to the Mongols' treatment of prisoners.

I have authentic accounts of their throwing their prisoners from precipices and shooting them to death with arrows; and on an occasion at which a … with whom I have conversed assisted, they actually drank the warm blood of their victims, and rubbed it over their faces and beards.

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

About Aimaqs

Keep in mind

An atrocity story from a single informant, the kind of tale told about enemies; Elphinstone could not check it.

Strange talesBamiyanLegend about c.1399; told 1832

Timur's archers could not hit the giant Buddhas

At Bamiyan, Mohan Lal repeats the local story that Timur, returning from India, ordered his best archers to shoot down the colossal statues, but that not one arrow struck them. He had just recounted that Hindus credited the statues to the five Pandava brothers of the Mahabharata; Muslims linked them with the pre-Islamic idols Lat and Manat.

Being tempted by his religion, he ordered his expert archers to discharge their arrows against the images, which he wished to level to the ground ; but none of the arrows struck them, the cause of which I know not.

Legend or rumourMohan Lal (1846 edition)

About Timur (Tamerlane)

Keep in mind

Tradition, not history; the statues were in fact Buddhist, carved around the sixth century AD.

BuildingsKhulm (Tashqurghan), Afghanistan1824

Tash Kurghan: domed houses of clay and sun-dried brick

Moorcroft describes the Uzbek-ruled market town of Tash Kurghan: about 20,000 one-storey houses of clay and sun-dried brick, domed 'in the usual fashion of the country', each in its own walled yard with fruit trees, along straight streets that crossed at right angles with water running down them.

Tash Kurghan is about three miles in circumference, and may comprise about twenty thousand houses. They are built of clay and sun-dried bricks, of one story, with domes in the usual fashion of the country, and each stands by itself in a walled enclosure, often containing fruit-trees.

Saw itWilliam Moorcroft (edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)

Daily lifeHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

Hazara wives who run the household

Elphinstone is surprised to find that among the Hazaras, a tribe he calls nearly savage, women have more standing than anywhere nearby. They manage the house and property, are consulted on everything, are never beaten and are not secluded. He also records, from hearsay, lurid claims about their morals which he admits are contradictory.

The wife manages the house, takes care of the property, does her share of the honors, and is very much consulted in all her husband's measures. Women are never beaten, and they have no concealment.

Heard itMountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

BuildingsBetween Kunduz and Khulm (Tashkurgan), northern Afghanistan1838

Roadside buildings of the Mughal emperors

On the road west from Kunduz, Wood found a domed water cistern and a guard tower that he credits to the 'Mongol' emperors, meaning the Mughal dynasty of India founded by Babur. He admired them as public works for travellers, and notes that the emperors had kept post-houses all the way to Balkh.

At the bottom of their western slope stands a water-cistern, a dome-covered building, and a guard-tower … remnants of Mongol munificence, evincing, by their usefulness rather than their beauty, the philanthropic spirit of the monarch who erected them. … for during the palmy days of Baber's line the emperors of Hindustan had post-houses

Saw itJohn Wood

Keep in mind

Daily lifeKunduz1837

Priests who dealt in slaves

Wood says the Sunni Uzbeks of Kunduz treated persecution of Shia Muslims as proof of faith, and that their religious leaders were the biggest slave dealers, buying Shia captives and justifying it as good for the soul. Elsewhere he describes chained slave tributes on the road to Kunduz.

These mullahs, or priests, are the most notorious slave dealers in the land, and encourage the odious traffic among their disciples, by readily purchasing of them whatever victims of the Shiah creed they may entrap. The good of the soul is assigned as ample extenuation

Heard itJohn Wood

About Uzbeks of Kunduz and their mullahs

Keep in mind

Wood writes as a hostile Protestant observer; the slave trade itself is confirmed by many sources.

An Afghan court ceremony 'borrowed from the Chinese Tartars'

Conolly retells a Persian anecdote about presentation at the court of Ahmad Shah Durrani: two officers ran the visitor up to the king at a trot, shouted his name and whisked him away again. He calls the ceremony 'borrowed, apparently, from the Chinese Tartars', then admits this is only 'the Persian account'.

The Dooraunee ceremony of presentation at court is a very strange one, borrowed, apparently, from the Chinese Tartars, … the person upon whom the honour is to be conferred, being placed between two of the officers of the court, who run him up at a trot to within a certain distance of the king

Daily lifeHindu Kush, Afghanistan1221

Chinggis Khan's terms to the Jin envoy

The envoy's own report says nothing about his audience, so Bretschneider adds the entry from the Yuan shi, the official history of the Mongol dynasty. Chinggis reminds him that he had offered peace if the Jin ruler gave up everything north of the Yellow River and took the lesser title of wang (king), but his general Muqali had since conquered those lands anyway. On the next page the Khan adds that the Jin must also hand over the cities of Kuan si that still held out, and only then may their ruler keep the south as a king.

The emperor said to him: "I formerly asked of your sovereign to cede the land north of the Yellow River, and to reign over the country south of it with the title of wang (king). On these conditions I would have suspended the war. But now Mu-hua-li … has already conquered all these countries, and you are compelled to sue for peace."

Official recordYuan shi (official history of the Mongol Yuan dynasty), annals for 1221, translated by E. Bretschneider as an addition to the Pei shi ki

About Chinggis Khan and the Jin envoy

Keep in mind

Not part of the envoy's report. Bretschneider notes that the Yuan shi dates the events of the western campaign a year too early.

The evil eye that eats a man's liver

In a footnote Elphinstone records an Afghan belief that some Hazaras have a power of 'fascination': reading the damaged scan, they can eat out the liver of anyone they stare at. He calls it a fable, common in India and Persia and pinned on various tribes. It is a good example of a travelling rumour attached to a people seen as outsiders.

who … the liver of any person on whom they fix their eyes. This fable is very common in India and Persia, and is attributed to various tribes.

Legend or rumourMountstuart Elphinstone (footnote, 1815)

BuildingsHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

Hazara villages guarded by loop-holed towers

Most Hazaras live in villages, not tents, though some use 'Tartar tents' like the Aimaqs. Each village has a defensive tower with a kettle-drum to sound the alarm. On the page before, Elphinstone says their houses are thatched and half sunk into the hillsides.

The Hazaurehs generally live in villages of from twenty to two hundred houses, though some live in Tartar tents like the Eimauks. Each village is defended by a high tower, capable of containing ten or twelve men, and full of loop-holes.

Daily lifeKunduz1837

Sell your wife, never your dog

Wood notes a twist in Uzbek manners: asking a man to sell his wife was no offence, but asking to buy his dog was an insult, and 'dog-seller' was about the worst name one Uzbek could call another. He goes on to praise Uzbek women as fine housekeepers.

To ask an Uzbek to sell his wife would be no affront, but to ask him to sell his dog would be an unpardonable insult, "Suggee ferosh," or dog-seller, being about the most offensive epithet that one Uzbek can apply to another.

Heard itJohn Wood

Daily lifeNorthern Afghanistan (Oxus plain)June 1832

Turkmen tents half-buried in snow

On the desert road to Bukhara the caravan passed Turkmen tents. Mohan Lal notes that they had large herds, lived on meat, curds and bread, fed their horses on meat and gave them salt water, and spent the winter in tents half-buried in snow. He adds bluntly that plundering caravans was their real trade and that they held many Persian (Qizilbash) slaves.

There we observed a few tents of Turkmans; they possess large herds of cattle, and live upon meat, curd, and bread. They feed their horses generally with flesh, and give them salt-water to drink. They do not much mind the cold of the winter, which they pass in tents, half-buried under the snow. Plundering the caravans is the proper occupation of these Turkmans.

Saw itMohan Lal (1846 edition)

About Turkmens

Keep in mind

He saw the tents; the details of winter life and horse-feeding are probably what he was told.

The nameKunduz1824 to 1825

Murad Beg of Kunduz, 'a Tartar' and a robber

In his preface Wilson blames the failure of the expedition on Murad Beg, the Uzbek chief of Kunduz, whom he calls 'a Tartar of activity and ambition, but a barbarian and a robber'. Moorcroft's own journal (T0023, n459, p.446) quotes Murad Beg answering a plea to keep his word: 'What have I, what has an Uzbek to do with character?' Here 'Tartar' means an Uzbek.

His death, and the dissensions which ensued amongst his sons, transferred the ascendancy to Murad Beg, a Tartar of activity and ambition, but a barbarian and a robber.

Read itHorace Hayman Wilson (editor's preface, 1841)
