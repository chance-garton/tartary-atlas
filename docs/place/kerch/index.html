CitiesKerch Strait (Kerch, Yenikale, Taman)1773

Kerch and Yenikale for Russia, a fortress on a non-existent island for the Ottomans

All the fortresses and places on the side of Crimea, Taman and Kuban … shall be left and handed over to the Tatars; and secondly Yenikale and the port of Kerch, with their districts and territories, shall be made over to Russia; and at most the Sublime State may build a fortress on an imaginary island, whose name and body do not exist, between Yenikale and Taman.

Working translation

Original wording

قريم وطمان وقوبان جانبنده اولان قلاع وبقاع بالجمله … تاتاره ترك وتسليم اولنه وثانيا یکی قلعه وکرش ليمانی الکا وناحيه‌لريله روسيه دولتنه تمليك اولنوب نهايه یکی قلعه ايله طمان بيننده اسم وجسمی معدوم بر اطه موهومه ده دولت عليه بر قلعه بنا ايتمك جايز اوله

Editorial context

The memoir lists Russia's peace terms as the Ottomans understood them: the fortresses and places on the Crimean, Taman and Kuban side would be handed to the Tatars, while Yenikale and the port of Kerch, with their districts, would go to Russia. At most, it says with open sarcasm, the Ottomans would be allowed to build a fortress 'on an imaginary island, whose name and body do not exist' between Yenikale and Taman. The Tatar towns of the strait are real fortresses bargained over by two empires.

Ottoman memoir on Russia's demands, 1773 (item II in the library catalogue, apparently translated from a French memoir), undated copy c.1775 to 1780

About Crimean Tatars, Russians and Ottomans

Keep in mind

A hostile Ottoman summary of Russian demands, perhaps translated from French; the sarcasm about the island is the memoir's own. Russia did gain Kerch and Yenikale by the treaty of 1774.

BuildingsCrimea (Kefa, Kerch, Kozlov, Perekop)1782

Genoese forts razed by Tatars, rebuilt by Turks

All these places were well fortified as long as the Genoese ruled them, but the Tatars razed the fortifications when they took them. When they later became subject to the Turks, these restored the fortifications at Kefa, Kerch and Kozlov and built the fortress of Arabat … as well as Perekop

Working translation

Original wording

Alle diese Oerter waren, so lange die Genueser sie beherrschten, wohl befestigt, die Tatarn schleiften aber, bey der Einnahme derselben, die Festungswerke. Wie sie nachher den Türken unterwürfig wurden, stellten selbige in Kefa, Kertsch und Koslow die Festungswerke wieder her, und legten die Festung Arabat … wie auch Perekop an

Editorial context

Zuev credits the Crimean fortresses to three builders: the Genoese fortified the towns, the Tatars razed the walls when they took them, and the Turks later restored the fortifications at Kefa, Kerch and Kozlov and built Arabat and Perekop. At Perekop a broad, deep, stone-lined ditch cut the isthmus from sea to sea behind an earth rampart, while the fortress itself had become heaps of brick, bombs and cannonballs.

Vasily Zuev (Sujef), 'Nachrichten von der Krim', in Neue Nordische Beyträge vol. 4 (1783), edited by P. S. Pallas

BuildingsKerch1836

Kurgans raised by each mourner's handful of earth

these tumuli were voluntarily erected by the people ; as when any of their great warriors or kings expired, his ashes were placed in the tomb, and every man who admired his virtues carried a portion of earth and threw it over his grave.

Editorial context

After watching a kurgan being opened at Kerch, Spencer reflects on the countless mounds of the region. He reports the tradition of 'descendants of the aboriginal inhabitants' in the Crimean mountains: when a great warrior or king died, every admirer carried a portion of earth and threw it on his grave. On the next page he calls this 'the tradition of the Tartars' and compares it to Scottish cairns.

Edmund Spencer
See the original page (p. 246)

About Crimean Tatars and older mountain peoples (as tradition-keepers)

Keep in mind

A local tradition; the Kerch kurgans are mostly Greek and Scythian burials many centuries older than the Tatars. Spencer credits the builders to 'a great and powerful people' of antiquity, not to the Tatars.

Strange talesKerch1839 to 1840

The 'Golden Hill' of Kerch

The Tartars have a tradition, that upwards of forty pouds, nearly one thousand four hundred and forty pounds English, of this metal, in ornaments and coin, were, several hundred years ago, taken from one of the tumuli in the neighbourhood; and they call it in consequence the Golden Hill. This is, no doubt, fabulous

Editorial context

In the Kerch museum of ancient Greek finds, Jesse reports a Tatar tradition that centuries earlier about forty poods (some 1,440 pounds) of gold had been taken from one of the nearby burial mounds, which they therefore called the Golden Hill. He calls the story 'no doubt fabulous'.

Captain William Jesse

About Crimean Tatars (local tradition)

Keep in mind

The mounds were ancient Greek and Bosporan burials, not Tatar; the tradition is about finding treasure, not building.

BuildingsSouthern Russia and Kerchlate 1830s

Kurgans built by others, dug up by everyone

the countless graves of the Greeks of Taurus, and of the original inhabitants of the Caucasus and Siberia, have been objects of zealous research … the Tartars and the Cossacks of the present day, have plundered them, and melted down the treasures found therein. The greater part of the kurgans and mohilos of South Russia, have been long since burrowed like rabbit-warrens

Editorial context

Describing the Crimean gold in the Hermitage, Kohl credits the southern burial mounds to the Greeks of Taurida and older peoples of the Caucasus and Siberia. The Tartars appear not as builders but as one of many later peoples, with Goths, Huns and Cossacks, who dug into the kurgans and melted down the treasure.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

The nameKerch1836

Altyn Obo, the Tatars' 'hill of gold'

we then extended our promenade to the Museum, the collection of which has been considerably augmented since the opening of a tumulus in 1830, called by the Tartars, Altyn Obo, or the hill of gold ; pretended to have been the tomb of Mithridates.

Editorial context

At Kerch's museum Spencer saw finds from a mound opened in 1830 that the Tatars called Altyn Obo, 'hill of gold', and which was claimed to be the tomb of Mithridates. The gold vases and ornaments justified the name, he says, though the best had gone to St Petersburg. The Tatars named the mound; the treasures were Greek work.

Edmund Spencer
See the original page (p. 242)

About Crimean Tatars (as name-givers)

Keep in mind

The 1830 excavation was of the Kul-Oba kurgan; Spencer's 'Altyn Obo' may conflate names. He himself says the finds were 'unquestionably of Grecian workmanship'.
