Nomads who fear that houses will fall on them

Soopoon Choomboo told Turner that the wandering people beyond the desert disliked entering houses. Turner passes on the explanation he heard (fear that houses would fall, perhaps from earthquakes) and adds his own guess: that the ability to flee smallpox made a mobile life attractive. Either way, he says, large hordes still live in tents with their herds.

This prejudice is said to have its foundation in a dread of their falling; an apprehension which may perhaps have originated, in remote times, from the calamitous effects of earthquakes … Thus large hordes of Tartars are still found to dwell in tents, tending upon cattle, and placing their chief dependance upon their herds

Heard itSamuel Turner (1800)

About Mongol nomads ('northern Tartars')

Keep in mind

Both explanations are guesses; the second (smallpox) is Turner's own.

Strange talesGobi Desertabout 1341

The Tartars crossed the 'impassable' torrid zone

East of Almaliq the legates reached wind-built 'mountains of sand' beyond which, Marignolli says, nobody before the Tartars believed there was habitable land. The Tartars crossed them into a vast plain that philosophers called the Torrid Zone and thought impassable; on the next page he adds that he crossed it twice himself. He is mixing a real desert crossing with the old scholarly theory of uninhabitable climate zones.

we came to the Cyollos Kagon, that is, to mountains of sand made by the winds, beyond which, before the Tartars, nobody thought there was habitable land, nor was it believed that any land lay beyond. But the Tartars, by God's will and with marvellous skill, crossed them and came into a vast plain, the place called the Torrid Zone, held by the philosophers to be impassable

Daily lifeGobi, Mongolia1821

Obo: stone cairns for the spirits of the land

Bichurin explains the obo cairns he saw on hilltops: small round mounds of stones heaped on every notable height by a main road. Once a year Mongols gather there to honour the guardian spirits of the district with wrestling, horse racing and archery. Every rider passing an obo dismounts, kneels on the south side, bows three times and leaves something: a khadag scarf, an arrow, a scrap of cloth, horsehair or a pebble.

These are sacred places, where the Mongols gather once a year to worship the guardian spirits of the country. Here their festivals take place, accompanied by wrestling, horse racing and archery. At other times every Mongol riding past an obo gets off his horse, kneels on one knee before it on the south side and, raising his folded palms to his forehead, bows three times.

Ta-ta: the name in Chinese records

Klaproth says the Chinese first met the Tatars at the start of the ninth century and called them Ta-ta, later Ta-t'a-öl, a garbled form of 'Tatar' because Chinese has no R. He quotes the Chinese encyclopedia San-tsai t'u-hui of 1604, which places the Ta-ta in the Gobi as descendants of the 'barbarian Yuan' (the Mongols) and counts the Wa-la (Oirats) among them, and concludes that the Tatars were the present Mongols and Oirats (the Kalmyks of Central Asia).

The Chinese, who came to know the Tatars at the beginning of the ninth century, then called them Ta-ta, and in later times Ta-t'a-öl, which is the corrupted pronunciation of the word Tatar, since the Chinese have no R … One sees clearly from this passage that the [Tatars] were the present Mongols and Oelöts (that is, the Kalmyks of Central Asia).

Working translation

Original wording

Die Chinesen, welche die Tatar zu Anfange des neunten Jahrhunderts kennen lernten, nannten sie damals … Ta-ta, und in späteren Zeiten … Ta-t'a-öl, welches die verdorbene Aussprache des Wortes Tatar ist, indem die Chinesen kein R haben … Man sieht aus dieser Stelle deutlich, dass die … jetzigen Mongolen und Ölöten (d. i. Mittelasiatische Kalmücken) waren.

Read itJulius von Klaproth
