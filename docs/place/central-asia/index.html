The nameTurkestan and the Dasht (Central Asia)legendary origins, told c.1650

Mongol and Tatar: twin sons, and a name that swallowed the rest

Katib Celebi tells the Islamic-Persian genealogy: the children of Turk son of Japheth spread over Turkestan, most of them nomads living in tents. Later Alinja Khan had twin sons, Mongol and Tatar, who divided the kingdom, so two clans arose. Eight Tatar rulers reigned before their line died out, and the Mongol line followed. Then comes a clear statement of the naming problem: 'Mongol' became a generic name for many tribes, so that by extension people say 'Turk', 'Tatar' and 'Mongol' for all of them, although the Mongols were really one kind of Turk.

From one womb he had two sons, one named Mongol and the other Tatar, and he divided his whole kingdom between these two sons … The said word became a generic name for many tribes and peoples, and it is applied by extension even to those who are not of them, so they say Turk and Tatar and Mongol; but in truth the Mongols were one kind of Turk.

'Most incorrectly' called Tartars

Muraviev says the scattered Turkmen tribes share a common origin but have been 'most incorrectly' called a Tartar race by European writers. He suggests the word came from Tatar, the founder of a tribe of that name still living in the far steppes.

This people has been most incorrectly denominated the Tartar race by several European authors. The term was probably borrowed from Tatar, the Chief who founded the tribe of that name, existing in the remoter steppes to the present day.

Read itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

Strange talesCentral Asia1843

Flood geology and the 'Plateau of Tartary'

Humboldt explains how a geographic guess became a myth. If the highest lands had been the first to emerge from the waters, they must also have received the first seeds of civilisation; 'diluvial' geology built on ancient myths encouraged this, and so the vaguely named Plateau of Tartary gained a 'moral interest'. Travel, measurement and the study of Asian languages, he says, have corrected these exaggerations.

The high regions that had been the first to be left by the waters must also have been the first to receive the seeds of civilisation. Systems of diluvial geology, founded on ancient myths and local traditions, favoured these views. … gave this great massif, this continuous plateau vaguely called the Plateau of Tartary, a moral interest and a special importance.

The nameCentral Asia1826

Call it 'Central Asia', not 'Tartary'

Meyendorff objects that the usual map label 'Tartary' covers lands never settled by Tartars while leaving out places where Tartar (Turkic) was actually spoken. A country, he argues, should only be named after a people who live there, so he proposes 'Central Asia' instead.

If in geography we give to a country the name of a people, it should be conditional on such people inhabiting that country. It appears to me then, that Central Asia would be a more appropriate name to introduce in lieu of Tartary, since I have found it more correct, and more geographical.

Read itGeorg von Meyendorff (translated by William Monteith, 1840)

The nameCentral Asia (Tartary)1760s (theory)

Tartary as the first land after the Flood

Touching on the 'Atlantis' debate of his day, de Tott argues that the high plateau of Tartary must have been the first land inhabited in Asia, the source of the swarms that later poured into Europe as Goths, Ostrogoths and Visigoths. He says a Tartar tradition told him by Khan Krim Giray supports this, and on the next page traces the Crimean dynasty back to Genghis Khan.

this country, possessed at present by the Tartars, must have been the first discovered land in Asia, and the earliest inhabited; the source of that population which first sent forth those swarms which, constantly driven back by the wall of China, and the defiles of Thibet and of Caucasus, fell upon the northern parts of Asia, and overflowed our Europe, under the name of Goths, Ostrogoths, and Visigoths.

Legend or rumourBaron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

About Tartars (in general)

Keep in mind

A speculative 18th-century theory based on geography and one conversation, not on evidence; de Tott himself calls it a 'hypothesis'.

'Tartarestan', says Lorenzo: from the river Tartar

Here Katib Celebi reports what the European geographer he calls 'Lorenzo' wrote: the second part of Asia, beyond the Imaus mountains, is Tatarestan (Tartary). Its people are Mongol Tatars who have spread over most of Asia. Their origin is a river named Tatar, from which they took their name, and they were formerly known as Mongols. He is translating a European atlas (Mercator-Hondius), so this is the European 'Tartary' coming back into an Ottoman book.

The second part, which lies beyond the Imaus mountains, is Tatarestan. According to Lorenzo's account, they are Mongol Tatars who spread over most of Asia … These are the original Tatars, who took their name from a river called Tatar, and were known by the name Mongal.

Genghis Khan the blacksmith

At Bukhara Ibn Battuta retells what was said of the Tartars' founder: that Genghis Khan began as a blacksmith in the country of 'Khota', a big, generous man who won followers by feasting them, then conquered Khota, China, Kashgar and Almaliq before turning on Khwarezm. The translator notes that other histories give his origins very differently.

It is said that Jengiz Khan, who came with the Tartars into the countries of Islamism and destroyed them, was in his outset a blacksmith in the country of Khota.

Legend or rumourIbn Battuta (translated by Samuel Lee, 1829)

The nameCentral Asia (reported)1240

'Rightly called Tartars, as if from Tartarus'

Under the year 1240 Matthew describes the Tartars bursting out of a homeland walled in by mountains 'like demons loosed from Tartarus', and adds that this is why they are rightly called Tartars, as if 'Tartareans'. This pun on Tartarus, the classical underworld, is why Latin writers turned the steppe name Tatar into 'Tartar'. The same paragraph compares them to locusts covering the earth.

in that same year a detestable people of Satan, namely a boundless army of Tartars, burst out from its region walled in by mountains; and, having broken through the solid mass of impassable rocks, they came swarming out like demons loosed from Tartarus (so that they are well called Tartars, as if 'Tartareans'), and like locusts covering the face of the earth

The ancient people who 'taught us everything except their name'

Humboldt says the high plains of Tartary are no longer seen as the cradle of human civilisation and of the arts and sciences. He borrows d'Alembert's joke about the vanished ancient people imagined by Bailly: they taught us everything except their name and the fact that they existed. Solid research, he adds, has shown that learning reached the lands between the Pamirs, China and the Altai late.

These high plains of Tartary are no longer regarded as the cradle of human civilisation, the original home of the sciences and arts. It has vanished, that ancient people which, in d'Alembert's happy phrase, 'taught us everything except its name and its existence.'

Strange talesCentral Asia ('India' of Prester John)c. 1200 (legend)

Prester John's son cut to pieces, his daughter wed to Chinggis

Sanudo tells how the Tartars, once tributaries of 'David, son of Prester John, king of India', rose against him under Chinggis. Their best weapons were clubs with bows and arrows; most fought on foot, some rode oxen, a few rode asses and colts. David was cut limb from limb with all his family except one daughter, whom Chinggis took as wife. A blend of the Prester John legend with the real defeat of the Kereit ruler Toghrul.

Their best weapons were clubs, with bows and arrows: for the most part they were on foot; a smaller part sat on oxen; the smallest on asses and beasts of burden and colts. So King David, hearing of their coming ... was cut limb from limb with his whole family except his only daughter: which daughter Chinggis Khan took as his wife.

Rex itaque Dauid eorum aduentum audiens … & cum tota familia, exceptâ vnicâ eius filiâ, membratim truncatur: quam quidem filiam, Cyngis Chaam vxorem accepit.

Strange talesCentral Asia (realm of Kaidu)1280

The khan's daughter who wrestled her suitors for horses

Kaidu Khan's daughter Aijaruc ('Bright Moon') refused to marry anyone who could not beat her. Each challenger staked horses, and she won more than 10,000. When a handsome prince staked 1,000 horses and her parents begged her to lose, she threw him on the palace floor anyway. She then rode to war with her father, snatching enemies from their ranks 'as deftly as a hawk'.

When both had taken post in the middle of the hall they grappled each other by the arms and wrestled this way and that, but for a long time neither could get the better of the other. At last, however, it so befel that the damsel threw him right valiantly on the palace pavement.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Keep in mind

Polo was not present; Persian sources confirm a warrior daughter of Kaidu named Khutulun.

Tatar and Mongol, the two sons of one khan

Commenting on the new French edition of Abulghazi's 'Genealogical History of the Tatars' (Leiden 1726), Strahlenberg retells its origin story: a khan (Alenzie or Alinja Khan) had two sons, Tatar and Mungel, both of Turkish descent, whose followers split into two branches named after them. It is a Central Asian Muslim tradition, passed on through the Swedish prisoners' translation.

since Alenzie Khan had two sons, one Tater, the other Mungel, both indeed of Turkish descent, as the author rightly shows, these nevertheless divided at once into these two branches, the former going east with his Turkish people, who afterwards took their name from him, while Mungel stayed in the west with his own people

Prester John: 'ten times more than the truth'

Rubruck traces the legend of Prester John to a Nestorian shepherd-lord of the Naimans who made himself king after the death of the Kara-Khitai ruler. The Nestorians called him King John and said ten times more about him than was true, as they make great rumours out of nothing. Rubruck crossed his pastures and found that nobody knew anything of him except a few Nestorians.

When Con Khan died, that Nestorian raised himself up as king, and the Nestorians called him King John and said ten times more about him than the truth. For that is what Nestorians coming from those parts do: they make great rumours out of nothing … So the great fame of this King John went out; and when I crossed his pastures, nobody knew anything about him except a few Nestorians.

The nameCentral Asia1663

Five Tartaries on the map

Here 'Tartary' is a geographer's label for most of northern Asia, carved into five parts: Crim Tartary, the hordes under the Tsar (including Kazan and Astrakhan), Zagatay or the Uzbek country where Tamerlane reigned, Cathay or Great Tartary, and an unknown far north-east. It shows how loosely the name was spread over many peoples.

Tartary is devided into five parts, the first is the lesser Tartary, or Tartary of Precop, called also Crim Tartary. The second is Sarmatia Asiatica, which containeth many Hordes of the Tartars subject to the Great Duke of Moscovia, as the Czeremisses, the Nagais, Zauolhans, and those of Cazan and Astracan.

Read itPierre Chevalier (translated by Edward Brown, 1672)

The nameCentral Asia1826 (writing about the 13th century onward)

How Tatars and Mongols merged

Meyendorff offers his theory of the name: Genghis Khan's Mongols conquered the Tatars, who were more numerous, and took up their Turkic language. From that mixture, he says, came all the peoples called 'Mongol Tartars', and he sees no real difference in looks between a Kazakh, an Uzbek, a Turkmen and a Tatar of Kazan or Crimea.

The Asiatic people to whom the Russians give the name of Tartars, have the nose slightly flattened, the cheek bones prominent, little beard on the chin, and small eyes … Gengis Khan, at the head of the Mongols, subjugated the Tartars, who became incorporated with his army, and those of his successors. As they were the most numerous, the conquerors adopted the Turkish language, barbarous as it was

Read itGeorg von Meyendorff (translated by William Monteith, 1840)

Strange talesInner Asia ('east of the 26th clime')Before 1275 (story); Ottoman compilation about 1600

Trial by goat and sieve among the pagan Tatars

Hammer's first appendix translates the Ottoman geography Menazir ul-awalim, which here copies al-Qazwini's Athar al-bilad (1275). The Tatars are described as a hard, bloodthirsty Turkic people who worship the sun; their coming is counted among the Prophet's foretold wonders, with a saying about broad-faced, small-eyed 'Turks' who will tie their horses to the pillars of the mosques. A woman once held captive tells how, when her captor fell ill, his kin suspected her of poisoning him, set her on a goat and had a sorceress swing a sieve over her while they stood round with drawn swords; when the goat bleated she was freed. The Ottoman author adds that these pagan Tatars differ from the Crimean and Nogai Tatars, long since Muslims.

I was for a time a captive among the Tatars, when the man who had taken me prisoner fell ill. … One day they held a great assembly, brought a goat and set me on it. A sorceress came with a sieve in her hand, swung it and spoke some words … While this was happening, the goat bleated under me. They said: it is not as we believed, and let me go.

The other two Tartar empires

Besides the Great Tartar of Cathay and the Tartar emperor of Persia, Jordanus has heard of two more Tartar empires: one 'now of Osbet' called Gatzaria, and one of 'Dua and Cayda', now of 'Elchigaday'. Yule's note identifies Osbet as Uzbek, khan of the Golden Horde (1313 to 1340), and Elchigaday as the Chagatai khanate, and explains that Chinggis Khan's conquests had split among his heirs. But other two empires of the Tartars, as I have heard, to wit, that which was formerly of Cathay, but now is of Osbet, which is called Gatzaria, and the empire of Dua and Cayda, formerly of Capac and now of Elchigaday, extend over more than CC days' journey.

The nameInner Asianote of 1848

Scythians, then Turks, then Tatars

In his introduction Reinaud explains that the same northern peoples went by different names in different eras: Scythians for ancient writers, Turks for early Arabic writers, and Tartars, or rather Tatars as Arabs, Persians and Turks pronounce it, for later writers. It is a short, clear account of how 'Tartar' became a catch-all label.

For ancient writers, the peoples of northern Asia and Europe were Scythians; for the early Arabic writers they are Turks; for later writers they are Tartars, or rather, as the Arabs, Persians and Turks pronounce it, Tatars.

The nameCentral Asiaearly 14th century

Why the Mongols are still called Tatars

Ney Elias's introduction quotes the Persian historian Rashid al-Din (writing about 1300 to 1310). The Tatars were once so powerful that other Turkic peoples called themselves Tatars for the prestige, just as in Rashid's own day everyone wanted to be a 'Mongol'. That, he says, is why the Mongols are still called Tatars in China, India, the Kipchak steppe, Arabia, Syria and Egypt.

Previous to this period it was the same with the Tatars, on account of their power, and this is the reason why the Mongols are still called Tatars in China and in India, by the Kirghiz, the Bashgirds, in the Kapchak country, in the north of Asia, in Arabia, in Syria, in Egypt, and in Africa.

Read itRashid al-Din, quoted (via d'Ohsson's French) in Ney Elias's introduction (1895)

'Turk' means a man from the highlands of Tartary

Chardin insists that the true 'Turkey' is the vast highland of Tartary, homeland of both the Turkmen herders and the Ottomans' ancestors. In the East, he says, 'Turk' simply means someone from those highlands, and Ottoman subjects find the name barbarous. He also scolds European travellers who write about countries without knowing the language.

For that among them Turk signifies one that comes from those High-lands of Tartary, let it be from what part it will.

Amazons beyond Samarkand, subject to Timur

Clavijo repeats what he heard at Samarkand: fifteen days toward China lived a land of women who met men once a year, kept their daughters and sent their sons to the fathers. He adds that they were Greek Christians, once subjects of the emperor of Cathay, now of Timur, and descended from the Amazons of Troy. A classic legend attached to a real direction on the map.

These women are subject to Timour Beg ; they used to be under the emperor of Cathay, and they are Christians of the Greek Church. They are of the lineage of the Amazons who were at Troy, when it was destroyed by the Greeks.

Legend or rumourRuy González de Clavijo (translated by Clements R. Markham, 1859)
