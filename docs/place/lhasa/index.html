BuildingsLhasa1640s to 1690s

The Potala's Red Palace, built under the 'Tartar kings'

Succession of Desrid or governors … under the Tartar kings … The erection of the nine-storeyed Red palace (Phodañ Marpo) of Potala during the prime-ministership of Desrid San-gye Gya-tsho

Editorial context

The contents list the regents (desi) who governed Tibet under Gushri Khan's descendants, the 'Tartar kings', and credit the building of the nine-storeyed Red Palace of the Potala to the regent Sangye Gyatso. The Mongol khans held the title of king; the great building is credited to the Tibetan regent.

Sarat Chandra Das, English analytical contents to his edition of Sumpa Khenpo's Tibetan text (Calcutta, 1908)

CitiesLhasa1717

The Dzungar 'Tartars' storm Lhasa, 1717

At their instance the Tartar king crossed into Tibet in 1717 with an army of 6,000 men under the command of General Zze-ring-ton-drup … in the night of November 30 a general assault on Lhasa was delivered the gates were thrown open by their confederates. In a couple of hours there was nothing left for Ginghes Khang but to seek safety in the Potala Palace.

Editorial context

Wessels summarises Desideri's eyewitness chapters on the Dzungar invasion. Invited by the discontented great monasteries, 'the Tartar king' of Independent Tartary sent 6,000 men under Tsering Dondup, who reached Lhasa in late 1717; on the night of 30 November the gates were opened to them by allies inside. Lhazang Khan ('Ginghes Khang') fled to the Potala, and the same page says the city was then plundered for two days. Desideri was at Sera monastery and lost only a few things left with the Capuchins.

C. Wessels (1924), summarising Ippolito Desideri's eyewitness account

BuildingsLhasa1661-1662 (journey); 1665 (interview)

A Tibetan king of Tartar descent in a four-storey castle

The capital city of this kingdom is called Lassa; the King is named Teva, and descends from a very ancient race of the Tartars of Tangut. He lives at Butala, a castle built on a high mountain in the fashion of European houses; it has four storeys of very fine architecture.

Working translation

Original wording

La ville capitale de ce royaume s'appelle Lassa; le Roy se nomme Teva, qui descend d'une race tres-ancienne des Tartares de Tangut. Il fait sa residence à Butala, chasteau basty sur une haute montagne à la façon des maisons d'Europe; il a quatre étages de fort bonne architecture.

Editorial context

The Jesuit Johann Grueber crossed the 'sands of desert Tartary', the Kokonor lake and Tangut to Lhasa. As told to a Florentine visitor in 1665, the king descends from an ancient race of Tartars of Tangut and lives at Butala (the Potala), a castle on a high mountain built like a European house, four storeys of fine architecture. The same page reports the Grand Lama worshipped as a god and said to have risen from death seven times.

Johann Grueber, as reported by an unnamed Florentine interviewer (generally identified as Lorenzo Magalotti), French translation in Thévenot, 1696
See the original page

About Tibetans; the king, of the 'Tartars of Tangut'

Keep in mind

Interview notes, translated; Grueber saw Lhasa, but the reporter wrote from conversation. 'Teva' is probably the Tibetan title Depa (the regent), not a personal name.

Strange talesLhasa1661

A living god in Lhasa, and relics made of his excrement

There are also two kings in this kingdom: the first attends to the administration of the whole kingdom; the other, whom they call God the Father or heavenly Father, or else the Great Lama, or high priest of the sacrificers, lives like a deity in the innermost recesses of his palace and receives the ceremonies of divine worship from all; and they hold him in such honour that they foolishly persuade themselves that even his filth and excrement are of great help against every kind of illness.

Working translation

Original wording

Sunt & in hoc Regno duo Reges, prior administrationi Regni totius incumbit; alter, quem & Deum patrem sive cœlestem, aut etiam Magnum Lamam, sive sacrificulorum Pontificem vocant, in ultimis Palatii sui recessibus Numinis instar residens, ab omnibus divini cultus recipit cerimonias; tantoque eum in honore habent, ut vel ipsius sordes & excrementa ad omnes infirmitatum adversitates plurimum conducere stultè sibi persuadeant

Editorial context

Kircher says traces of the famous Prester John survive in the 'Tanchut' kingdom that the Tartars call Barantola and the locals Lassa (Lhasa). There are two kings there: one governs, the other, the Grand Lama, sits hidden in his palace and is worshipped as God the Father, so much that people believe even his dung cures all ills. The Jesuits were refused access to him and drew only his portrait.

Athanasius Kircher, S.J. (China illustrata, Amsterdam 1667), from the oral report and drawings of Johann Grueber and Albert Dorville

Keep in mind

Hostile missionary framing; the Prester John link is Kircher's own speculation, and the excrement relics are hearsay the Jesuits did not see.

CitiesLhasa1717 to 1720

Dzungar 'Tatars' seize Lhasa, 1717

the Chinese army, composed of 107,000 Chinese soldiers and 30,000 Tatars, retook the metropolis of Lhasa, and made themselves masters of the whole of the kingdom of Tibet, on the 20th September, and overthrew completely the Gionkar Tatars, who had treacherously invaded Tibet, and with the connivance of a great part of its people, had plundered the country, and taken Lhasa, in November and December, 1717.

Editorial context

Della Penna records how the 'Gionkar Tatars' (Dzungars) invaded Tibet, plundered it and took Lhasa in November and December 1717, and how in 1720 a Qing army of 107,000 Chinese and 30,000 Tatars retook the city and installed a new Dalai Lama. Before this, he says, Tibet had a 'Tatar king' from the Kokonor chiefs as its protector. Here Tatars are the armies competing for Lhasa.

Francesco Orazio della Penna, Capuchin missionary (English version in Markham's appendix, from Klaproth's edition)
See the original page (p. 322)

About Dzungar (Oirat) Mongols; Qing army of Chinese and Tatars

Keep in mind

Della Penna was in Tibet in these years; the troop figures are his and may be exaggerated.

Daily lifeLhasa1777

A god-king for the Tartars from the Volga to Korea

Every body knows that the Delai Lama is the great object of adoration for the various tribes of heathen Tartars, who roam through the vast tract of continent which stretches from the banks of the Volga to Correa on the sea of Japan, the most extensive religious dominion, perhaps, on the face of the globe.

Editorial context

Stewart explains the Dalai Lama's place in the Tartar world: he is worshipped by the 'heathen Tartars' across the whole breadth of Asia, and the more distant ones treat him as God himself. He adds that even the Manchu emperor of China honours him and keeps a lama envoy at the palace in Peking.

John Stewart (Philosophical Transactions, 1777)

Daily lifeLhasa1720

Decrees in Chinese, Tartar and Tibetan

Till October 1720 Tibet was a monarchy under an absolute and independent king. From that time, however, all power has passed into the hands of the Emperor of China and the actual government consists of a body of ministers appointed by him, some of whom are Chinese, some Chinese Tartars, others again Tibetans. All decrees must be promulgated in three languages Chinese, Tartar and Tibetan.

Editorial context

After the Qing armies drove the Dzungars out in 1720, Desideri reports, Tibet's government passed to the Emperor of China, run by ministers some of whom were Chinese, some 'Chinese Tartars' and some Tibetans. Every decree had to be issued in three languages: Chinese, Tartar and Tibetan. 'Chinese Tartars' was the usual period term for the Manchus, so within one book 'Tartar' shifts from Mongols to Manchus.

C. Wessels (1924), summarising Ippolito Desideri's manuscript
See the original page (p. 256)

About 'Chinese Tartars' (Manchus of the Qing court)

Keep in mind

A summary by Wessels, not Desideri's own words. Which 'Tartar' language is meant (Manchu or Mongol) is not stated.

BuildingsLhasa1716

Lhasa's walls and gates, built by 'Ginges-Khang'

In ancient times, our missionary goes on to tell us, Lhasa was an open place, Ginges-Khang being the first to protect it at various points with gates and fortifications. Situated in a plain the town is enclosed by mountains so as to leave only four approaches each of them defended by a fortification.

Editorial context

Desideri says Lhasa had anciently been an open town, and that 'Ginges-Khang' was the first to protect it with gates and fortifications, with a fort guarding each of the four approaches through the mountains. Ginges-Khang is Desideri's name for Lhazang Khan, the ruler killed by the Dzungars in 1717. The editor's footnote on the same page adds that these defences were built late in the 1600s and pulled down again in 1722 by order of the Kangxi emperor, with only some gates and fragments of wall surviving.

C. Wessels (1924), paraphrasing Ippolito Desideri's manuscript
See the original page (p. 241)

About Lhazang Khan ('Ginges-Khang'), the Qoshot Mongol ruler of Lhasa

Keep in mind

Wessels's footnote insists Ginges-Khang is 'not the famous Mogul despot' (Genghis Khan) but a Tibetan king; modern historians identify him as Lhazang Khan of the Qoshot Mongols. Dating of the walls comes from Koeppen via the editor.

The nameLhasa1661

A Lhasa ruler 'of ancient Tartar stock'

There are two kings in this kingdom; one, the Deva (Deba), of ancient Tartar stock, carries on the government; the other, who is worshipped as a deity, lives in seclusion within the precincts of his palace. … the energetic Ngavang Lobsang, who from being the head of the Tashi Lhumpo monastery had, with the aid of the Tartar prince Gushi Khan, worked his way up

Editorial context

The Austrian Jesuit John Grueber reached Lhasa in 1661. As Wessels summarises his report, Tibet then had two kings: a secular governor 'of ancient Tartar stock' who ran the government, and the Dalai Lama, worshipped as a god in his palace. Wessels adds that the Potala was begun in 1642 by the Fifth Dalai Lama, who had risen to power 'with the aid of the Tartar prince Gushi Khan'. The Tartars here are the Qoshot Mongols who held military power in Tibet.

C. Wessels (1924), summarising John Grueber's 1661 report (via Kircher) and adding his own historical comment

The nameLhasa1811

Manning's 'Tatar mandarin' at Lhasa

I had learnt now, to my great sorrow and discomfort, that the chief mandarin at Lhasa was the identical Tatar chieftain who had been disgraced at Canton for his management during the Bengal expedition to Macao. That he was a man of a particular suspicious temper, detested at Lhasa, and that he certainly would give us all the trouble in his power.

Editorial context

Thomas Manning, travelling to Lhasa in disguise, was dismayed to learn that the chief imperial official there was 'the identical Tatar chieftain' who had been disgraced at Canton over the British occupation of Macao in 1808. Throughout his diary 'the Tatar' means this Manchu amban, whom Manning also calls 'the Tatar dog'.

Thomas Manning (diary, edited by Markham, 1876)

BuildingsDamxung (Dam), north of Lhasaabout 1730

A king's palace amid Tatar tents at Dam

in Dam there is nothing else but the palace belonging to the King and his court, the remainder of the inhabitants (which are mostly Tatars and the other Tibetans) dwelling in tents of felt and of cloth woven out of hair. Two days distant from Dam is Nak chu ka, where is the last fortress of Tibet, but no other dwellings except tents

Editorial context

Describing the northern province of Chang, della Penna says that at Dam, the summer seat of the Tatar king of Tibet, there was nothing but the king's palace; everyone else, mostly Tatars and some Tibetans, lived in tents of felt and of cloth woven from hair. Beyond that, at Nakchu, there was a last fortress and again only tents.

Francesco Orazio della Penna, Capuchin missionary (English version in Markham's appendix, from Klaproth's edition)

BuildingsLhasa1716

A Lhasa monastery 'reserved for the Tartars'

"There are at Lhasa", the narrator continues, "three other idolatrous temples and monasteries of the same sect [the Gelugpa or Yellow-caps]. One is situated in the north-eastern quarter and is reserved for the Tartars. The other two lie towards the east, one near the town-limit, the other just on it, both called by the same name Ramo-cce."

Editorial context

In his tour of Lhasa's temples Desideri notes three more Gelugpa temples and monasteries, one of them in the north-eastern quarter 'reserved for the Tartars', that is for Mongol monks who came to study in Tibet. Elsewhere he says the great monastic universities drew students from Ladakh, Tartary and China. The editor could find no later confirmation that this house was specially for Tartars.

Ippolito Desideri, in C. Wessels's English translation (1924); bracketed words are Wessels's
See the original page (p. 240)

About Mongol monks ('Tartars')

Keep in mind

'Idolatrous' is the missionary's own religious judgement. Wessels identifies the house tentatively with the Muru monastery and notes that later writers do not mention its being for Tartars.

CitiesLhasa1774 to 1777

Lhasa, a trading capital with a Chinese garrison

The city of Lahassa, which is the capital, is of no inconsiderable size, and is represented as populous and flourishing. It is the residence of the chief officers of government, and of the Chinese mandarins and their suite. It is also inhabited by Chinese and Cassemirian merchants and artificers, and is the daily resort of numberless traders from all quarters,

Editorial context

Stewart describes Lhasa as populous and flourishing, home to Chinese mandarins, Chinese and Kashmiri merchants and craftsmen, and a daily stream of caravans. Just before this he notes that while Bogle was with the Lama, 'several Mongols and Calmucs arrived from Siberia'. Neither Bogle nor Stewart saw Lhasa: the city is 'represented' to them.

John Stewart (Philosophical Transactions, 1777)
See the original page (p. 481)
