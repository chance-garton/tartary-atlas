Daily lifeUstyurt plateau, between Khiva and the Caspian1840

A Kazakh feast: mutton, broth and thumbs for napkins

At a Kazakh host's tent, the guests ate boiled mutton with no bread or vegetables, the men first and the women afterwards. Abbott is squeamish and scornful, but he records exactly how the meal was served and cleaned up.

It consisted of boiled mutton, soused in its own soup. Bread and vegetables are things quite unknown in these parts. Kuzzauks are exclusively carnivorous. … The bowls were handed to the women, who scraped them clean with their thumbs … The thumb and tongue are the only napkins in Khaurism … The Tartars are right not to eat with their women.

Saw itJames Abbott

About Kazakhs (Kuzzauks), called Tartars by Abbott

The nameKhiva khanate and Central Asia1822 (Russian original)

'Most incorrectly' called Tartars

Muraviev says the scattered Turkmen tribes share a common origin but have been 'most incorrectly' called a Tartar race by European writers. He suggests the word came from Tatar, the founder of a tribe of that name still living in the far steppes.

This people has been most incorrectly denominated the Tartar race by several European authors. The term was probably borrowed from Tatar, the Chief who founded the tribe of that name, existing in the remoter steppes to the present day.

Read itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

BuildingsKhiva1819 to 1820

Mud houses, felt tents, bricks from old ruins

Many Khivans, even the rich, lived in felt tents all year because they were used to nomad life. Houses were of cheap mud with a hole in the roof for smoke. The fine buildings were a few mosques, above all the turquoise-domed great mosque, and bridges were built of fired bricks taken from old ruins.

The houses are very bad and wretched, and a great portion of the people live in felt tents all the year round. Even the wealthy do this, although they possess houses, because they are so accustomed to a nomadic life. The houses are of mud … Bridges are numerous, and are constructed of the burnt bricks which they get from old ruins.

Saw itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

BuildingsKhiva1840

The Khan holds court in a felt tent

At his first audience Abbott found the ruler of Khiva not in a hall but in a plain black felt tent pitched inside his court, with a wood fire burning in the middle. Even a settled king with a brick citadel kept the nomad's tent as his reception room.

He was seated upon a carpet, and supported by cushions. Before him a wood fire blazed up, sending its smoke and sparks through the sky-light of the tent. … The black tent of felt which he occupied, was of the usual dimensions, i. e. about 24 feet in diameter, and quite unadorned, its sole furniture being the carpet and cushions, on which he reclined.

Saw itJames Abbott

The Khan receives the envoy in a yurt

After weeks of waiting, Muraviev was led through a fine brick gateway and a series of mud-walled courtyards. In the last, weedy courtyard stood the Khan's yurt, and the ruler sat inside it cross-legged on a carpet while the Russian envoy stood at the entrance.

On reaching the end of this corridor, we had to go down two steps into a fourth court-yard, larger than the other three, but surpassing them all in filth, and covered here and there with weeds. In the middle of this stood the Khan's Yurt.

Strange talesKhiva1819 to 1820

The devil's claws on the moon

Muraviev says that even the court astronomer who could calculate eclipses believed, like everyone in Khiva, that an eclipse happened when the devil grabbed the sun or moon, and that shouting and firing guns would drive him off. They called the North Star the 'iron point' at the end of the earth's axis and compared the earth to a watermelon.

he does not even know the cause of an eclipse, but believes with the rest of the people that it is brought about by the devil fastening his claws on to the sun or moon, and thinks a loud noise and the firing off of guns necessary to get rid of this dreadful enemy. The earth they believe to be round and compare it to a water-melon.

Heard itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

CitiesKhiva khanate (New Urgench, Shavat, Kat, Gurlen)1819 to 1820

Mud-walled towns that last because it rarely rains

Muraviev lists the five towns of the khanate, with New Urgench larger and busier than Khiva itself. All were walled, but their buildings were mean and made almost entirely of mud, with walls faced with stone only here and there. They survived for years only because so little rain fell.

The buildings in all the above towns are very mean (the only ones approaching to anything like size being a few mosques), and mud is the almost universal material of the houses, as it may also be said to be that of the town walls … In spite of this perishable material, the buildings stand for many years, owing to the very scanty rain fall.

Saw itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

BuildingsKhiva1840

Khiva's manor houses of rammed clay

Riding into the Khiva oasis, Abbott describes the country houses of the gentry: tall clay-walled compounds with towers at the corners, locally called 'castles'. The walls were of rammed clay, generally fluted, an effect he thought perhaps came from the hurdles of straight branches that held the clay while it was rammed.

The houses have all one character, being an enclosure of very lofty clay walls, flanked by ornamental towers at the angles, which give them the appearance of castles. This name (Gullah) they bear at Khiva. … The walls, built with great regularity of rammed clay, are generally fluted, an effect given them perhaps by the hurdles of straight branches, between which the clay is supported whilst soft

Saw itJames Abbott

Strange talesKhiva1717

The khan who wanted no gold: the Khiva massacre

Bruce tells how Prince Alexander Bekovich, sent to find gold on the Amu Darya, was lured into quartering his men among the Uzbeks. The khan assured him he had no use for gold or silver, since his people owned only herds and tents and had no castles or towns to lose. That night the troops were massacred and Bekovich cut to pieces. The story reached Peter through a surviving captive officer.

for his own part, he neither valued gold nor silver, as it was of no use in their country, for they lived without that, or even bread, consequently had no use for either ; their whole riches consisting in herds of cattle, which, with their tents, they could remove at pleasure ; and, consequently, could not fear having either castles, towns, or villages, rifled or taken from them

Heard itPeter Henry Bruce, reporting the khan's words via a surviving officer (published 1782)

BuildingsKhiva oasis1819 to 1820

Mud castles of the Khivan landowners

Constant feuds had led landowners to build square fortified farmsteads with granaries, tanks, mills and stables for 100 to 150 people. Muraviev gives their size and materials: mud walls 18 to 20 feet high, sometimes of masonry, with domed corner towers and battlements that were only for show. He was himself held in one of these forts.

The buildings are in the form of a square, and are surrounded by walls generally of mud, but sometimes of masonry. The walls are 8 feet thick at the base, diminishing to 3 at the summit, and are from 18 to 20 feet high. They are supported on the exterior faces by round buttresses, also of mud, and are crenelated, but the battlements are merely for show

Saw itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

Slave caravans on the road to Khiva

Abbott travelled alongside caravans that had carried grain south and were returning to Khiva with captives, many from Herat. He describes the women riding with their captors and the men walking the whole way, chained by the neck at night.

They had brought grain from Khiva, and are returning thither laden with slaves, many of whom are natives of Heraut. … But the men are chained together by the throats at night, so that rest is scarcely possible, whilst the contact of the frozen iron with their skin must be torturous. For them also no carriage is found, they walk the whole way

Saw itJames Abbott

CitiesKhiva1819 to 1820

Khiva: a turquoise dome and mud streets

Muraviev describes Khiva as a walled town between two canals whose chief buildings were the great mosque with its turquoise-blue dome, a rather insignificant palace and some smaller mosques. Most building was in mud, the streets were narrow, and a market was held twice a week. Locals said its old name was Khivak and that it existed before the Amu Darya changed course.

The principal buildings are the great mosque, with its dome of turquoise-blue (a colour revered by Mussulmans) ; the Khan's palace, which is, however, an insignificant structure, and several smaller mosques. Mud is the material in general use for building purposes. The streets are narrow.

Saw itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

Daily lifeKhivaDecember 1819

A note hidden in a gun barrel

Preparing to leave, Muraviev found a slip of paper secretly pushed into his gun barrel by Russian captives, begging him to tell the Tsar of more than 3,000 Russian slaves suffering in Khiva. Elsewhere he says most were kidnapped on the Orenburg frontier by Kazakhs and sold at Khiva, and that Russian slaves were the khanate's only smiths.

As I was preparing to clean this gun, I discovered a slip of paper in one of the barrels, on which the following was written in Russian : "We venture to inform your Honor that there are over 3,000 Russian slaves in this place, who have to suffer unheard-of misery from labour, cold, hunger, &c. Have pity on our unhappy situation and reveal it to the Emperor.

Saw itNikolai Muraviev, quoting a note from Russian slaves (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

BuildingsKhiva, Uzbekistan1740

Khiva: walls of earth and houses of mud

The British factors Hogg and Thompson reached Khiva in 1740. They describe it as the khan's seat on rising ground, defended by a very thick earth wall higher than the houses, with turrets and a wet ditch. The houses are low, mostly mud-built, with flat roofs covered in earth. On the same page they note that the Khivans live in towns while their Kazakh neighbours live in tents.

It is situated on a rising ground, has three gates, and is defended by a strong wall of earth, very thick, and much higher than the houses: it has turrets at small distances, and a broad deep ditch full of water. The place is large, but the houses are low, the greatest part of them being built with mud; the roofs are flat, and covered with earth.

Saw itBritish factors Reynold Hogg and George Thompson, their 1740 journal as printed by Jonas Hanway (1753)

A khan who lived in a felt tent

Muraviev says the Khan of Khiva lived in a felt tent all year round and kept his houses only for his wives, spending most of his time hawking and hunting on the steppe. He also notes the Khan had banned alcohol and tobacco on pain of having one's mouth slit.

His mode of living is quite nomadic, for he occupies a felt tent all the year round, keeping his houses for his wives only. The greater part of his time is passed in the steppe, hawking or hunting, snipe and wild goats being his favourite game.

Heard itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

Daily lifeIsfahan, Iran, and Khiva, Uzbekistan1630s (told about 1670s)

A captive Tartar prince who galloped home from Isfahan

Chardin tells the court story of 'Abouel-Kazi', a prince of Khwarazm captured in a Persian victory over the Uzbeks. Shah Safi treated him as royalty in Isfahan for ten years, until he feigned illness, went to a spa, and rode off with twenty Uzbek horsemen sent to fetch him. He became khan and stayed a friend of Persia. This is Abu'l-Ghazi Bahadur, khan of Khiva, later author of a famous history of the Turks.

To whom the Yusbeck Prince thus briskly answer'd; I am highly oblig'd to the King of Persia, but I owe more my own Country and Relations that call me back. 'Tis my Father that sends me these Men, to facilitate my return to my Native soil

Heard itJean Chardin, 'The Coronation of Solyman III' (appended to the 1691 English edition)

Daily lifeKhiva1840

Singing kites over Khiva

For weeks Abbott heard a strange humming music over Khiva at night. It turned out to be paper kites with a taut string stretched across a bent crosspiece, which hummed in the wind and were left flying all night. He thought the idea worth copying in England.

Seeing some children on the road with their paper kites, I approached to examine the contrivance, by which these toys emit a musical sound, whilst floating in the air. … And, as numbers of these kites are left floating in the air all night, the effect is that of aerial music ; monotonous, but full of melancholy interest.

Saw itJames Abbott

The nameKazakh steppe, Bukhara, Khiva1820s

Russia's Tatars are 'Nogais' to the Kazakhs; 'Tatar' is a cursed name

In a footnote to his chapter on ruins Levshin notes that Kazakhs call the Tatars living in Russia only 'Nogais'. The word 'Tatar' itself they did not use; in their view it belonged to some base people cursed by a Muslim prophet. Bukharans and Khivans thought the same. The same story was reported by Rychkov seventy years earlier (W175).

The Kirghiz-Kazakhs call the Tatars living in Russia nothing but Nogais. The (word) Tatar itself is not used by them at all, and in their opinion belongs to some base people cursed by a certain Muslim prophet. The Bukharans and Khivans think the same, and also call the Russian Tatars Nogais.

The nameKhiva and Bukhara1740s to 1750s

In Bukhara and Khiva, 'Tatar' was an insult

Rychkov questioned Muslim scholars and Russian envoys sent from Orenburg to Khiva and Bukhara about the names 'Scythians' and 'Tatars'. He concludes that no people there called itself Tatar. The word meant a lawless, inhuman people supposed to live somewhere in far Siberia, and calling someone a Tatar was like calling him a barbarian or a worthless peasant.

for I am fully certain that in all those parts there is not one people that calls itself Tatars. By the name Tatars they understand a very lawless and inhuman people, which they suppose lives in the Russian, and above all in the most remote Siberian, places … And they regard the name Tatar as very dishonourable and abusive, as if to say barbarian, serf, and good-for-nothing.

Daily lifeKhiva1819 to 1820

Bards who sing all night

Rich Khivans kept bards who sang the deeds of ancient heroes on a lute, growing more ecstatic as the audience applauded, sometimes through the whole night while the listeners sat still and silent.

The wealthy Khivans generally keep a bard in their service, who entertains them with stories and songs, and airs played on a sorry kind of lute. Sometimes they compose ballads relating the deeds of ancient heroes. … This singing is kept up the whole night not unfrequently, during which the host and his guests sit attentively listening, motionless, and apparently buried in meditation.

Heard itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

BuildingsKhiva1840s

Khiva and Kokand: towns of mud

Drawing on Alexander Burnes and other authors, Wolff describes the capital of Khiva as a town of mud huts with three stone mosques and a mud palace, living largely by the slave trade, and says Kokand was likewise built of mud except for three stone bazaars.

The capital of Khiva is a town of mud huts, with three stone mosques, and a mud palace. Its chief trade is in slaves, of whom there are about forty thousand in the Khanat ; and it is said there are many captured Russians, who are slaves in Khiva. … It is, like Khiva, built of mud, with the exception of three stone bazaars.

Read itJoseph Wolff, summarising Alexander Burnes and other writers

Daily lifeKhiva khanate1819 to 1820

Oaths at a saint's tomb and fortune-telling sticks

The Yomut Turkmens settled disputes by swearing an oath over a saint's tomb, believing a perjurer could only escape damnation by dying on the spot. Muraviev also describes Khivan fortune-tellers who read letters from a book, or threw sticks onto a circle of other sticks each standing for a letter.

The Yomud Turcomans adore a Saint over whose tomb they settle their differences by an oath. A false oath under those circumstances is unknown, for it is firmly believed that whoever forswears himself over the tomb can only save himself from eternal punishment by dying on the spot.

Heard itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

The nameKhiva and the Turkmen desert1830s

The Turkmen: 'these Tartars' under Uzbek rule

In his essay on a possible Russian invasion of India, Conolly weighs whether Russia could win over the Turkmen, who then gave unwilling allegiance to 'their Oosbeg conquerors' at Khiva. He concludes the Turkmen would follow their interests, and that Russia, as in the Caucasus, might in time 'subdue these Tartars'. It shows 'Tartar' as a British strategic label for the Turkic peoples of the desert.

I am sure that the Toorkmuns would be chiefly influenced by what they thought their interests; and when I look to what we have done in Hindoostan, or, to speak nearer to the point, what the Russians have done in Persia, I cannot decide that they may not subdue these Tartars

MixedArthur Conolly (1834)

CitiesKhiva1819

Khiva's mud walls and towns

In the extract from Muraviev's journal that Monteith appended, Khiva appears as a walled capital of 10,000 to 15,000 people with mud walls 20 feet high, a palace and a fine mosque. The other towns of the khanate were of flat-roofed mud with a few brick mosques, and lasted well because the climate was so dry.

The walls of the city are built of mud and 20 feet in height … The buildings in all these towns are only of mud with flat roofs, some few mosques are of brick and mortar. They are however very permanent from the dryness of the climate.

Saw itNikolai Muraviev, in William Monteith's English extract

The nameKhiva1819 to 1820

Uzbek means 'own master'

Muraviev explains the Uzbek name as 'own master' (uz = self, beg = master) and adds, with some irony, that since Muhammad Rahim Khan made himself absolute ruler, the Uzbeks of Khiva now called themselves 'khidmatgar', servants.

"Us" in Turkish signifies "self" and "Beg" master, so Usbeg may be translated as "Own Master." The name was appropriate enough up to the time of the reigning Khan, Mahomed Ragim, who, by ability, craft, and force, established himself as uncontrolled Ruler, since when his countrymen in Khiva style themselves "Khidmatgar" (servant) instead of "Usbeg."

Heard itNikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

The nameCentral Asia (Khiva, Bukhara, Balkh)written about 1670s

'Yusbek': a hundred lords, or 'he is the lord'?

Chardin gives two etymologies for the Uzbeks. Persians derived it from 'yuz' (a hundred) and 'bek' (lord), mocking a land split among a hundred petty princes; the Uzbeks rejected that and said it meant 'he is the lord'. He adds that Tartar and Turkish are one language, Turkish being a dialect of it.

They are called Yusbecks, at least if the Persian Etymology be true, by reason of the great number of Sovereign Princes by which the Tartars are Govern'd; for Yus, as well in the Language of the Tartars as Turks, which is but a Dialect of it, signifies a hundred, and Bek, Lords

Heard itJean Chardin, 'The Coronation of Solyman III' (appended to the 1691 English edition)

Entering walled Khiva

Abbott rode to his first audience through a suburb, a bazaar and a fortified gateway, with women crowding the rooftops to stare. The royal citadel itself he found to be a modest brick building set into a corner of the town walls.

On approaching the town, we entered a considerable suburb, and afterwards passed through a miserable bazaar to the gateway ; for Khiva is fortified. … It was not long before we reached, through some very poor streets, the citadel, within which resides the "Father of Victories," the king of Khaurism. It is a poor, brick building, forming an angle of the city defences.
