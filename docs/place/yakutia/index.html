Daily lifeAmga, YakutiaJune 1788

Everyone who enters churns the kumiss

In a corner of the Yakut house hung a leather bag of mare's milk. Everyone who came in, above all the women, stirred it with a plunger for a few minutes before doing anything else, which produced the sour but pleasant drink Lesseps calls koumouiss. Left to ferment longer, it became strongly intoxicating.

In a corner of the yurt a leather bag is kept permanently; every day mare's milk is poured into it and stirred with a stick like the one used to churn butter. Everyone who comes in, the women above all, never fails to churn this milk for a few minutes before turning to other work; from this comes the sourish yet pleasant drink called kumiss.

BuildingsYakutsk region, between the Lena and the Aldan1820

A half-millionaire who still lives in a yurt

In an oval valley once held by a Tungus chief and later taken by Yakuts from the south, Wrangel found a thriving settlement: many yurts, some as big as Russian houses, two good churches with towers, and big herds of cattle and horses. The Yakut headman had paid for both churches and was said to be worth half a million roubles, yet he still lived in a yurt by an open hearth, drank kumis and ate horse-flesh.

Numerous Jurti, some approaching to the dimensions of Russian houses, two good churches with towers … owes much of its prosperity to the Jakutian Golowa or superintendent, who has built and endowed the two churches at his own expense. His fortune is said to amount to half a million of roubles, nevertheless he has in no respect altered his original national customs. He lives in a Jurte

Ferdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840)

BuildingsYakutsk region1820

Winter houses shaped like cut-off pyramids

In winter the Yakuts moved into their jurti: cottages of thin boards shaped like a truncated pyramid and covered thickly with branches, grass and mud. The small windows were closed with plates of ice in winter and fish-skin or oiled paper in summer. The floor was beaten mud sunk a few feet into the ground, or boarded in richer homes, with an open hearth burning all winter. Wrangel thought these suited the climate better than Russian-style houses.

These are cottages formed of thin boards in the shape of a truncated pyramid, and covered thickly on the outside with branches, grass, and mud. A couple of small openings which admit a scanty light are closed in winter by plates of ice, and in summer by fish-membrane, or oiled paper. The floor is generally of beaten mud, and is sunk two or three feet below the ground

Ferdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840)

Daily lifeYakutsk region1786-1788

An oath sworn over the shaman's drum

A Yakut accused of stealing cattle could clear himself by oath. He stood before the fire facing the sun, called down the loss of family, cattle, sunlight and life if he were guilty, stepped over the shaman's drum and robe, and breathed smoke from butter thrown on the coals. Some tribes also made him bite a bear's head, believing a bear would kill the perjurer. Many innocent men preferred to pay rather than swear.

A magician places his tambour and dress before the fire, the embers of which are burning. The accused stands before it, facing the sun, and says: "May I lose during my life all that man holds dear and desirable, father, mother, wives, children, relations; all my possessions and cattle; the light of the sun, and then my own life

Martin Sauer (1802)

The nameYakutsk region1820

A Tartar named Sachalar, forefather of the Yakuts

Wrangel writes that the Yakuts' faces and language confirm their tradition of descent from the Tartars. In a footnote he gives the tradition itself: their ancestor was a Tartar called Sachalar, who crossed the mountains to Kirenga on the Lena, settled and married a Tungus woman. That, he explains, is why the Yakuts still call themselves Sachalary (Sakha).

Their countenance and language fully confirm the tradition of their descent from the Tartars. … According to this tradition, their ancestor was a Tartar, named Sachalar, who came from his own country on the other side of the mountains, to Kirenga on the Lena, where he settled and married a Tungusian woman; the Jakuti still call themselves Sachalary.

Ferdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840), text and footnote

BuildingsYakutsk region1785-1786

Yakut winter house of poles, planks and dung

Sarychev, who slept in these houses on the road, explains how a Yakut winter jurt is built: three rows of posts, beams laid across and sloping, rough planks, then a covering of grass, earth and dung. A hearth with a pitch-lined chimney of sticks burns all winter, and the cattle live in an attached shed. Summer houses are different: conical frames of poles covered with bark.

On these poles they lay five beams crossways and sloping, which are covered with rough planks, and afterwards with herbs, mould, and dung. … The summer jurts are altogether different from the winter ones, and have another name, being called masses; they are round and conical, made of long poles, and covered on the outside with the bark of trees.

Gavriil Sarychev (English translation, 1806)

Daily lifeYakutia1788

A Yakut prince buried with his favourite horses

Lesseps had been shown old Yakut coffins hung in trees, but says the Yakuts now buried their dead like Christians. At a prince's funeral his favourite horse and the best of his herd, richly harnessed, were tied to two carved or painted posts by the grave and killed over his body. Their hides and heads were then hung in nearby trees as a 'mausoleum', and the mourners roasted and ate the two horses on the spot.

If it is a prince, he is dressed in his richest clothes and finest weapons. … The prince's favourite horse and the best of his herd, both richly harnessed … walk beside the procession. On reaching the burial place they are tied to two posts planted by the grave. While their master is buried, they are slaughtered over his body,

Arrivés au lieu de la sépulture, ils sont attachés à deux poteaux plantés auprès de la fosse. Pendant qu'on inhume leur maître, on les égorge sur son corps,

Birch-bark cone for the kumis festival

Each June, Yakut chiefs held a feast to bless their herds. Mare's milk was fermented into kumis in large leather bags, and a summer hut was raised on a meadow: a cone of thin poles covered with birch bark, decorated inside and out with birch branches, with a hearth in the middle. Guests of any nation were welcome; the shamans took the head seats. The shaman then poured kumis on the fire to each of the gods.

A summer hut is built of thin poles of a conical form, covered with the inner bark of birch, on some extensive meadow. It is ornamented inside and out with branches of the birch-tree, and a hearth is made in the centre. Relations and acquaintances are invited to the banquet; but all guests are welcome of every nation indiscriminately.

Martin Sauer (1802)

BuildingsAmga, YakutiaJune 1788

A Yakut prince's winter house of timber and earth

A Yakut prince at Amga, owner of two thousand horses, showed Lesseps his 'yurt'. Its walls were upright logs set side by side and plastered with clay, leaning inwards to carry a low-pitched roof. One half housed the family in bunk-like compartments, the other was a byre for cows and calves, with a round wooden hearth in the middle.

Beams set upright side by side and covered with clay form its walls, which do not rise vertically like ours. Drawing closer together towards the top, they carry a roof with a gentle slope … On the other side of the yurt live the animals, the cows and calves; it is quite simply a byre.

Working translation

Original wording

Des poutres posées debout les unes à côté des autres, & recouvertes de terre grasse, en forment les murs, qui ne s'élèvent point perpendiculairement comme les nôtres. Plus rapprochés vers le haut, ils supportent un toit dont l'inclinaison est peu rapide … De l'autre côté de la yourte demeurent les bêtes, les vaches, les veaux, c'est tout simplement une étable.

Jean-Baptiste Barthelemy de Lesseps

BuildingsYakutsk region1820

Summer tents of boiled birch bark

The Yakuts had two kinds of home. For summer they used the urasa, a light round tent of poles covered with sheets of birch bark that were boiled to soften them and then sewn together. White outside and yellow inside, they looked from a distance like canvas tents. The herders moved with them to the best pastures while they cut hay for the winter.

In summer they use Urossy, which are light circular tents formed of poles, and covered with birch-bark, which they strip from the trees in large pieces. These strips are first softened by boiling, and are then sewed together; the outside being white, and the inside yellow, the Urossy have a very pleasing appearance, and at a distance resemble large white canvas tents.

Ferdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840)

Daily lifeYakutsk region1820

Kumis, but not the intoxicating kind

In a footnote Wrangel says the Yakuts make kumis from mare's milk 'in the same manner as in Tartary', except that they have not learned, like the Tartars, to make it intoxicating. It is so nourishing that a couple of skins of kumis hung from the saddle are often the only food carried on a hunting trip of several days.

This well known beverage, prepared from mares' milk, is made here in the same manner as in Tartary; only the Jakuti have happily not learnt, like the Tartars, to make it intoxicating. It is an agreable beverage, and so nourishing, that a couple of large skins full of kumys hung to the saddle, are often the only provisions taken for a foraging excursion of a few days.

Ferdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840), footnote

The nameYakutia1740s

The Yakuts are 'of Tartar nation' by their language

Opening his chapter on the origin of the Itelmens, Steller says the Yakuts belong to the Tartar nation, as their language shows, being a dialect of Crimean Tatar and modern Turkish. It is a clear example of 'Tartar' used as a language family, not a single people, and he notes that the Yakuts knew nothing of Kamchatka or its people.

The Yakuts, who are of Tartar nation as their language shows, which is a dialect of the Crimean and modern Turkish language, know nothing at all of the Itelmens, just as they had not the slightest news of the land of Kamchatka itself

The nameAldan River, Sakha Republic1786

Yakut horse-breeders: 'very opulent Tartars'

Sauer and the Russians around him still called the Yakuts and their neighbours Tartars. Describing the Lena valley, he says the little Russian posts existed to collect fur tribute from 'the wandering tribes of Tartars' nearby. Here, crossing the Aldan on horseback with Yakut guides, he describes the plains as home to 'very opulent Tartars' with huge herds of horses and cattle: the cattle- and horse-breeding Yakuts.

The woods abound in wild beasts and game; and the plains are inhabited by very opulent Tartars, who possess immense numbers of horses

Martin Sauer (1802)

Doors to the east, men south, women north

Inside a Yakut winter hut the door always faced east, the fireplace stood near the middle with room to walk around it, and benches and small sleeping cabins lined the walls. Men sat on the south side and women on the north. A hut where someone had died was abandoned to ruin as a home of demons.

They always take care that the doors of their huts shall face the east. The fire-place is nearly in the middle; the back of the chimney towards the door, and a free passage quite round it. The sides of the hut are furnished with benches and small cabins, which serve for sleeping places. … The men keep the south side, and the women the north.

Martin Sauer (1802)

Daily lifeYakutsk region1785-1786

Horse-flesh: better than the tenderest beef

Many poorer Yakuts had been baptised, Sarychev says, perhaps to escape the poll-tax for a few years. The rich stayed pagan because Orthodox fasts would forbid their many wives and their food: meat, butter, milk and above all horse-flesh, their greatest delicacy. In summer they made kumis from mare's milk 'after the manner of the Tartars'.

The rich are not disposed to renounce a plurality of wives, nor the use of meat, butter, milk, and above all, horse-flesh, during the fasts … The latter is the greatest delicacy they can possibly conceive; and they often tell the Russians, that if they were once to make a proper meal from the flesh of a horse, they would in future prefer it to the tenderest beef.

Gavriil Sarychev (English translation, 1806)

The nameYakutia1788

Yakuts who look and talk like Tartars

Lesseps notes that the Yakuts are strong and tall, that their features resemble those of the Tartars, and that people say the two languages are closely related. He only vouches for the fact that Yakuts speak very briefly and do not run their words together.

The Yakuts are robust and generally tall; their features as a whole bear some likeness to those of the Tartars; it is even said that the two languages are closely related. All I can affirm is that the Yakuts speak extremely briefly and do not run their words together.

Tout ce que je puis affirmer, c'est que les Yakoutes ont la parole extrêmement brève, & qu'ils ne lient point leurs mots.

Daily lifeYakutia (Lena River)1711 to 1721

Mare's-milk brandy thrown on the fire

Strahlenberg confirms, from questioning Yakuts himself, Isbrand Ides's report that at a set time of year they sprinkle a brandy distilled from mare's milk into a great fire, towards the east, as an offering to heaven. On the same page he says Yakuts wore their hair long, unlike their neighbours, and until recently buried a faithful servant alive with a dead master, a custom the Russians had forbidden.

…That, at a certain Time of the Year, they make a great Fire, into which they sprinkle a Sort of Brandy distill'd from Mare's Milk, towards the East, and thereby make an Offering to Heaven. I took an Opportunity of enquiring into the Truth of this Relation, of several of the Jakutians themselves, who all confirmed it.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Daily lifeYakutsk region1786-1788

Shamans no better than an English conjurer

Sauer explains that shamans could be men or, more rarely, women, trained by an old master in lonely woods. He heard wonderful stories of their power even from Russians, but after watching them many times he was not impressed. He then describes a healing ritual in detail, with a horsehair switch, a sacrificed mare or cow, and a drum.

I have heard most wonderful relations of their power, even from the Russians; but, notwithstanding I have seen their enchantments or incantations many times, I never could discover any of their feats equal to that of a common conjurer in England.

Martin Sauer (1802)
