CitiesBukhara1844

Bukhara: eleven gates and 360 mosques

Wolff gives a thumbnail sketch of Bukhara: a walled city of fifteen miles' circuit with eleven gates, hundreds of mosques, caravanserais, baths and about a hundred colleges, and the old citadel (the Ark), which he says was built a thousand years earlier by Arslan Khan.

It has eleven gates, and a circumference of fifteen English miles; three hundred and sixty mosques, twenty-two caravanserays, many baths and bazaars, and the old palace called Ark, built by Arslan Khan one thousand years ago, and has about one hundred splendid colleges. The houses have neither roofs nor windows.

Who Uzbek emirate of Bukhara

Saw itJoseph Wolff

Keep in mind. The number 360 is a traditional round figure, and the date and builder of the Ark are local tradition. 'Neither roofs nor windows' probably means flat roofs and no outward-facing windows.

Bukhara: 'They came, they dug, they burned, they killed, they plundered, they left'

After telling how Bukhara's citadel was stormed and its walls levelled, Juvaini says a man who escaped reached Khorasan and was asked what had happened. His answer was six verbs. The clever men who heard it agreed that nothing more concise could be said in Persian, and Juvaini says his whole chapter is only a commentary on those few words.

He said: 'They came, they dug, they burned, they killed, they plundered, and they left.' The shrewd people who heard this account agreed that nothing could be said more concisely in Persian.

Bukhara's brick mosques and ruined bazaars

George Thompson went on alone to Bukhara. He describes low mud houses inside a slender earth wall, but caravanserais and many mosques of brick, and a college for priests of brick and stone. The bazaars had been 'stately buildings' but were now mostly in ruins. He adds a lurid note about guinea worms ('rishtas') from the bad water.

…the houses are low, and mostly built of mud: but the caravanserais and the mosques, which are numerous, are all of brick: the bazars, or market-places, have been stately buildings, but are now the greatest part of them in ruins; these are generally built of brick and stone.

Behind its famous mosques, Bukhara was built of humble materials. Most houses had one storey, a few more than two, made of mud, wood and mats. The great roofed bazaar built by the late king, with its long straight line of shops hung with Chinese porcelain and Russian bottles, impressed him most.

The houses are mostly of one story high; some of them of more than two; they are neatly constructed of mud, wood, and mats, which prevent the falling of the dust from the roof. … The large bazar, which is roofed, has a very striking appearance.

BuildingsBukhara1220 (as told in 1710)

Bukhara burned because it was built of wood

Retelling the Mongol sack of Bukhara from Persian historians, Pétis explains why the fire was so complete: most buildings were of wood, because stone was hard to get around Bukhara. Only public buildings such as the mosques and caravanserais, built of brick, escaped. It is a clear statement of what one of Central Asia's great cities was built from.

all Parts of the Town were strait on fire, the greatest part of the Building being made of Wood, because of the difficulty of getting Stone in the Country round Bocara. … and all the Houses became a Prey to the Flames, except some publick Buildings, such as the Mosques and Caravansera's, which were made of Bricks.

Who Mongols of Genghis Khan

Read itFrançois Pétis de la Croix (English translation, 1722), citing Fadlallah (Rashid al-Din) and others

CitiesBukhara1220

Bukhara burned: wood gone, brick left standing

In his history of Bukhara, Fraser writes that Genghis Khan ordered the city burned when he found enemy officers hidden there. Because most houses were of wood, he says, nothing survived except mosques and public buildings of brick. He adds that Genghis's son Ögedei rebuilt the city.

as the greater part of its habitations were constructed of wood, its destruction was complete ; the sun, which rose upon its rich and crowded bazars, and thickly inhabited edifices, went down at night upon a waste of smoking ashes, among which, there was not one house standing, except some mosques and public buildings, that being built of brick, remained unburned.

Who Mongols under Genghis Khan

Read itJames Baillie Fraser

Keep in mind. A retelling from Persian histories, not an eyewitness account.

Russia's Tatars are 'Nogais' to the Kazakhs; 'Tatar' is a cursed name

In a footnote to his chapter on ruins Levshin notes that Kazakhs call the Tatars living in Russia only 'Nogais'. The word 'Tatar' itself they did not use; in their view it belonged to some base people cursed by a Muslim prophet. Bukharans and Khivans thought the same. The same story was reported by Rychkov seventy years earlier (W175).

The Kirghiz-Kazakhs call the Tatars living in Russia nothing but Nogais. The (word) Tatar itself is not used by them at all, and in their opinion belongs to some base people cursed by a certain Muslim prophet. The Bukharans and Khivans think the same, and also call the Russian Tatars Nogais.

Mohan Lal calls Bukhara the largest, most populous and richest city 'in the whole region of Tartary', and says it had 360 mosques and colleges, though he was not impressed by the students' Persian and Arabic. The nameKhiva and Bukhara1740s to 1750s

In Bukhara and Khiva, 'Tatar' was an insult

Rychkov questioned Muslim scholars and Russian envoys sent from Orenburg to Khiva and Bukhara about the names 'Scythians' and 'Tatars'. He concludes that no people there called itself Tatar. The word meant a lawless, inhuman people supposed to live somewhere in far Siberia, and calling someone a Tatar was like calling him a barbarian or a worthless peasant.

for I am fully certain that in all those parts there is not one people that calls itself Tatars. By the name Tatars they understand a very lawless and inhuman people, which they suppose lives in the Russian, and above all in the most remote Siberian, places … And they regard the name Tatar as very dishonourable and abusive, as if to say barbarian, serf, and good-for-nothing.

CitiesBukhara1832

Bukhara's brick bazaars and the Registan

Burnes describes Bukhara as a city of lofty, arched brick bazaars, each trade in its own quarter, massive colleges, mosques and minarets, twenty caravanserais and about a hundred ponds and fountains of squared stone. People believed the tallest minaret, about 150 feet, reached the level of Timur's Samarkand. In the Registan square a visitor could meet Turkmen, Kalmyks and Kazakhs from the deserts.

when the traveller passes its gates he winds his way among lofty and arched bazars of brick, and sees each trade in its separate quarter of the city; here the chintz sellers, there the shoemakers; one arcade filled with silks, another with cloth. Every where he meets with ponderous and massy buildings, colleges, mosques, and lofty minarets.

The Uzbeks sweep south and take Bukhara

Elphinstone explains who the Uzbeks were: a people who crossed the Jaxartes (Syr Darya) around 1500 and drove the heirs of Tamerlane out of Bukhara, Khwarazm and Ferghana. The next sentence is badly damaged in the scan, but it seems to say that the Uzbeks belong to the great division of mankind known in Asia as Turks, who together with the Moguls (and a third people whose name is illegible) 'compose what we call the Tartar nation'. So for him 'Tartar' is a European umbrella word for Turks and Mongols together.

The Uzbeks first crossed the Jaxartes about the beginning of the sixteenth century, and pouring on the possessions of the descendants of Tamerlane, soon drove them from Bokhaura, Khoarizm, and Ferghauna, and spread terror and dismay to the remotest parts of their extended empire.

A city of clay and a citadel on a man-made hill

Bukhara, three to four versts across, was ringed by a clay wall with loopholes; its streets were so narrow that a laden camel scraped the houses. Most houses were of clay, richer ones partly of brick, one or two storeys high, flat-roofed and windowless to the street. The khan's citadel, the Ark, stood on a clay hill about 60 feet high which Eversmann calls a colossal work of human hands, brick-faced below and clay above, with a palace of glazed brick on top. He does not call Bukhara a Tatar city; it is included here for its plain account of how a great Central Asian city of the time was built.

The dwelling houses are mostly built of clay, the richer ones partly of brick too, of one and two storeys, but no higher. … The khan's castle (called the Ark) stands high on a great clay hill about 60 feet high, which is a colossal work of human hands; it takes almost half an hour to walk round it; the lower half is of brick, at least on the outside, the upper of clay

Uzbek speech: a Persian-laced variety of 'Tatar'

Counting the peoples of Bukhara, Eversmann reckons Uzbeks at a quarter and Tajiks at three quarters of the townspeople. He calls the Uzbeks' language a variety of Tatar, known as Türki, which the Tatars can understand, though heavily mixed with Persian; Persian is the main language of the city. It shows how a Tatar-speaker from Siberia heard the Turkic languages of Central Asia as one family.

The language of the Uzbeks is a variety of the Tatar language which is called Türki, so that the Tatars can understand it: but it is much mixed with Persian words. The Tajiks speak Persian, and this is the main language here, for when an Uzbek speaks with a Tajik, Persian is spoken.

Frozen half-moon dumplings for winter journeys

Part 2 of Weber's work prints an account 'Of Tartarian Bukhara' by an author who says he had been in Little Bukhara himself. He notes that the men's dress differs little from the Tartar, that they eat with their fingers and wooden spoons, drink tea with milk, salt and butter, and fill half-moon-shaped pastries with chopped meat, which they freeze and carry in a sack on long winter journeys to boil into soup.

They fill a kind of pastry, shaped like a half moon, with it. On long journeys, especially in winter, they take a good supply of these pastries with them and carry them in a sack. When this food has first frozen thoroughly in the air, it is cooked in boiling water, and a fairly good soup is made of it.

Wenn nun diese Speise vorher an der Luft rechtschaffen durchgefroren ist, so wird sie in einem siedenden Wasser gekocht, und eine ziemlich gute Suppe davon gemacht.

'Yusbek': a hundred lords, or 'he is the lord'?

Chardin gives two etymologies for the Uzbeks. Persians derived it from 'yuz' (a hundred) and 'bek' (lord), mocking a land split among a hundred petty princes; the Uzbeks rejected that and said it meant 'he is the lord'. He adds that Tartar and Turkish are one language, Turkish being a dialect of it.

They are called Yusbecks, at least if the Persian Etymology be true, by reason of the great number of Sovereign Princes by which the Tartars are Govern'd; for Yus, as well in the Language of the Tartars as Turks, which is but a Dialect of it, signifies a hundred, and Bek, Lords
