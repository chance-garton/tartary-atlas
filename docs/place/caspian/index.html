Daily lifeUstyurt plateau, between Khiva and the Caspian1840

A Kazakh feast: mutton, broth and thumbs for napkins

At a Kazakh host's tent, the guests ate boiled mutton with no bread or vegetables, the men first and the women afterwards. Abbott is squeamish and scornful, but he records exactly how the meal was served and cleaned up.

It consisted of boiled mutton, soused in its own soup. Bread and vegetables are things quite unknown in these parts. Kuzzauks are exclusively carnivorous. … The bowls were handed to the women, who scraped them clean with their thumbs … The thumb and tongue are the only napkins in Khaurism … The Tartars are right not to eat with their women.

James Abbott

About Kazakhs (Kuzzauks), called Tartars by Abbott

Keep in mind

Abbott's sneering tone is his own; the facts of the meal are what he saw.

The hare, the owl and the fortress of wind

Riccoldo's origin tale: the Tartars lived shut in by mountains whose only pass held an empty fortress that roared with the noise of horses, men and trumpets, a trick of the wind. A hunter chasing a hare followed it in, saw an owl singing on the gate, reasoned that no one lives where a hare runs and an owl sings, and led his people out. So, Riccoldo says, Tartars honour the hare and paint it on arms and tents, and nobles wear owl feathers and owl skins, which Western merchants now sell them for up to twenty pounds each.

'There is no dwelling of men where a hare flees and an owl sings.' And so, entering confidently, he found no one.

Non est habitacio hominum, vbi lepus fugit et bubo cantat. Et sic fiducialiter intrans neminem inuenit.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

The wind that dried the sea for Möngke

In the Chinese biography of Möngke, the Kipchak chief Bachman fled to an island in the sea. When Möngke arrived, a strong wind drove the water off the shallows and his army crossed; Möngke declared that Heaven itself was opening the road. Bachman, captured, refused to kneel, saying 'I was the ruler of a state' and 'am I a camel, that I should kneel?', and warned that the water would soon return. The next sentence says Möngke then marched with Batu against Rus ('Oros').

By good fortune a strong wind drove the sea water off the shallow places, so that it was possible to cross. The khan, delighted, said: 'Heaven itself opens the road for me.' … The khan ordered him to kneel; but Bachman said: 'I was the ruler of a state, and can I value my life? Besides, I am not a camel: why should I kneel?'

Ханъ, обрадовавшись, сказалъ: »Само Небо открываетъ дорогу мнѣ.« … Ханъ приказалъ ему стать на колѣна: но Бацимакъ сказалъ: »Я былъ обладателемъ государства и могу ли дорожить жизнію? Сверхъ сего я не верблюдъ: для чего мнѣ становиться на колѣна?«

Raiders with two horses each

Perry explains why the rich land west of the lower Volga lay empty: raids by the Kuban Tartars, who plundered and burned villages and carried off cattle and people. They came in summer, each man with two horses to change as he rode, with sentries all round, and were gone before the Russians could react. A page later he tells how three to four thousand of them reached his own camp at dawn and drove off about 1400 horses.

The said Tartars when they make their Incursions, it is usually in the Summer time, when there is Grass enough on the Ground; and each Man for Expedition sake takes two Horses, which they change as they ride, sometimes sitting upon one, and sometimes upon another. They always travel with Centinels at a convenient Distance from all Sides of their main Body,

Captain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

Three Tatar nations, one language

In his closing description of the Caucasus, Güldenstädt sorts the Tatars of the eastern Caucasus into three peoples: Kumyks (settled farmers, rice and cotton growers and fishermen), Terekeme or Turkmen Tatars (whose dialect is almost Ottoman Turkish), and Nogais (tent-dwelling nomads). All speak Tatar, one of the main languages of the Caucasus, in dialects that are mutually intelligible at a pinch. For him ‘Tatar’ is a label for Turkic speakers, and he compares their dialects with Turkish as Italian, Spanish and Portuguese compare with each other.

In the eastern Caucasus one finds three kinds of Tatar peoples: 1. the Kumyk, 2. the Terekeme or Turkmen, and 3. the Nogai Tatars. … The similarity of the Turkish, Terekeme, Kumyk and Nogai-Tatar dialects seems to me comparable to the similarity between the Italian, Spanish and Portuguese languages.

Working translation

Original wording

Im ostlichen Kaukasus trift man dreyerley tatarische Völker an, 1 Die Kumükischen. 2. Terekemenischen oder Truchmenischen und 3. Die Nogaischen Tataren. … Mir scheint die Aehnlichkeit des türkischen, des terekemenischen, kumükischen und nogayisch-tatarischen Dialekts mit der Aehnlichkeit zwischen der italienischen, spanischen und portugisischen Sprache vergleichbar.

Johann Anton Güldenstädt (ed. P. S. Pallas, 1787)

'Mogoli' from 'Magogoli': the Tartars and Gog and Magog

Riccoldo weighs the theory that the Tartars were the Lost Ten Tribes of Israel shut behind the Caspian mountains by Alexander, notes that they hate Alexander's name and that their script looks like Chaldean, then argues against it. He reports that they themselves claim descent from Gog and Magog, which is why they are called Mogoli, as if a corruption of 'Magogoli'. It is a textbook example of how Europeans fitted the Mongols into biblical and Alexander legends.

They themselves say they are descended from Gog and Magog. Hence they are called Mogoli, as if from a corruption of the word Magogoli.

Working translation

Original wording

Ipsi vero dicunt se descendisse de Gog et Magog. Vnde ipsi dicuntur Mogoli, quasi corrupto vocabulo Magogoli.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

About Tartars (Mongols)

Keep in mind

A folk etymology. 'Mongol' has nothing to do with Magog; the claim that Mongols themselves said this is Riccoldo's report and is doubtful.

The lost ten tribes, shut in the Caspian mountains by Alexander

Matthew reports the belief that the Tartars were descendants of the ten tribes of Israel who abandoned the Law for golden calves, and whom Alexander the Great tried to wall up in the Caspian mountains with stones cemented with bitumen; when the work proved too great, God made the mountain peaks close together. On the next page he admits a problem: these Tartars do not speak Hebrew or know the Law of Moses, which he explains as divine punishment.

These Tartars, whose memory is detestable, are believed to have come from the ten tribes who went away, abandoning the Law of Moses, after the golden calves; whom Alexander of Macedon first tried to shut up in the steep Caspian mountains with millstones set in bitumen. When he saw that the work was beyond human labour, he called on the help of the God of Israel; and the peaks of the mountains came together

Original wording

Creduntur isti Tartari, quorum memoria est detestabilis, fuisse de decem tribubus, qui abierunt, relicta lege Mosaica, post vitulos aureos; quos etiam Alexander Macedo primo conatus est includere in praeruptis montibus Caspiorum molaribus bituminatis. Quod opus cum videret humanos labores excedere, invocavit auxilium Dei Israel; et coierunt cacumina montium adinvicem

Matthew Paris, Chronica majora, ed. H. R. Luard (vol. 4, 1877), drawing on Peter Comestor's Historia scholastica

The 'Silver Hill': a buried brick fortress

What looked like a hill on the coast turned out to be the sand-covered shell of a huge fired-brick building with a wall about 600 feet long. Muraviev dug for coins and found Muslim-style graves and broken glass. The local Turkmens told him it had been built by the Russians, and a translator's footnote links it to an ancient wall along the Gorgan River.

The Silver Hill is, as above mentioned, only the shell of some huge building or fortress, which has been covered with sand from the eastern side ; nevertheless within those walls I found tombs, and, indeed, got at some human remains, buried after the Mahomedan fashion … The great wall is about 600 feet long and over 12 feet in height. It consists of beautiful burnt bricks

Nikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

About Yomut Turkmens (living around it); builders unknown

Keep in mind

The site lies at the western end of the brick barrier now called the Great Wall of Gorgan, usually dated to the Sasanian period. The claim that Russians built it is local belief, and the footnote rejects it.

Stone towers, idols and Tibetan scrolls by the Caspian

In the same list the author repeats a report that General Bekovich, on his disastrous march to Khiva, found remains near the Caspian made up of four stone towers, stairs, idols and rolled-up 'Tangut' (Tibetan-script) writings. The same page also lists a ruined stone mosque on the Or and a tomb said to have been built by Khan Janibek. The Caspian report is second-hand, but it shows that ruins with idols and Tibetan writings in the steppe were already being reported in the 18th century.

General Bekovich, on his unfortunate march to Khiva, came upon remains near the Caspian Sea which consisted of 4 stone towers, stairs, idols and also rolled-up Tangut writings.

A Russian hut on the old Oxus nobody dares touch

The Turkmens told Muraviev that at the mouth of the dry river bed stood a hut built in the Russian style that no one would pull down out of superstitious fear, and whose builder nobody knew. Muraviev guessed it dated from Prince Bekovich's expedition sent by Peter the Great a century earlier.

Kiat informed me that the mouth of this old river bed, although choked with sand, is still distinguishable on the Caspian, and that by it there stands a hut (isba) constructed on the Russian plan. No one knows who the builder of this was, but the neighbouring Turcomans are restrained from demolishing it by superstitious dread.

Nikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

About Turkmens of the Balkhan region

Keep in mind

Muraviev did not see the hut; the link to Bekovich is his guess.

Scythians who will march for the Antichrist

Right after the Magog passage, Cruz cites a treatise on the Antichrist by one Pedraza, who says these Scythians live beyond the Caspian, are a huge multitude, and will come to help the Antichrist at the end of time. Cruz offers it as support for his own view. It is the old Gog and Magog end-of-the-world legend, repeated from a book.

Of these Pedraza says, in the treatise he made on the Antichrist, that they are Scythians and live beyond the Caspian Sea, and that they are a great multitude, which is to come to the aid of the Antichrist; I mention this to show that it agrees with what I have said.

Turkmen tents like those of the Georgian Tartars

On the Caspian coast Muraviev compared Turkmen dwellings to those of the 'Tartars in Georgia' (Turkic-speaking Muslims of the Caucasus): reed mats on long poles covered with felt. He also notes that Turkmen women did not hide from strangers and wore very tall headdresses edged with gold or silver.

The dwelling-places of the Turcomans are constructed like those of the Tartars in Georgia, that is to say, they consist of rush mats stretched on long poles, the whole being covered with felt. Their women are not kept in privacy

Nikolai Muraviev (translated by W. S. A. Lockhart, 1871)

Could anyone know the name 'Tatar' in the 6th century?

In a note, Frähn discusses a 16th-century Ottoman writer who claimed the Persian king Anushirvan (6th century) built the Caspian Gates for fear of 'Turks and Tatars'. Frähn replies that although Arab writers mention the Tatars earlier than some scholars thought, the name can hardly have been known that early in western Asia. It is a scholarly warning against reading 'Tatar' back into ancient history.

for although Arab writers mention the Tatars earlier than is noted in the excellent Mémoires sur l'Arménie (vol. II, p. 263), one can hardly assume that their name was known so early in western Asia.

Working translation

Original wording

denn erwähnen gleich Arabische Schriftsteller der Tataren früher, als in den trefflichen Mémoires sur l'Arménie T. II. p. 263 angemerkt ist; so früh kann man wohl kaum Bekanntschaft mit ihrem Namen im westl. Asien annehmen.

C. M. Frähn, editor's note (1823)
