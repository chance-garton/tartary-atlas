Daily lifeTobolsk1588

Out hawking: Seydyak, a Kazakh tsarevich and Karacha

In the second year after the building of the town of Tobolsk, from the town of Sibir, from Chuvash, there came out for bird sport the Tatars Prince Sedyak and the tsarevich of the Kazakh horde Saltan and Tsar Kuchum's counsellor, the Tatar Karacha, and with them five hundred Tatars.

Working translation

Original wording

По поставленіи града Тоболска во второе лето, из града Сибири, от Чувашъ вышли для птичной потехи Татара князь Седякъ да царевичь Казачьей орды Салтанъ да царя Кучума думной Татаринъ Карача, с нимъ Татаръ пятьсотъ человекъ.

Editorial context

A year after the Russians founded Tobolsk on the hill opposite the Tobol mouth, the Tatar prince Seydyak, a Kazakh tsarevich named Saltan and Kuchum's counsellor Karacha rode out from the old Tatar capital Sibir 'for bird sport' (falconry) with five hundred Tatars. The voevoda invited them to a feast as if for peace talks; when the guests choked on the wine he had offered, he took it as a sign of treachery, had their men cut down and sent the princes to Moscow.

Esipov Chronicle (short redaction), in Sibirskie letopisi (1907)

The nameIsker (Sibir), near Tobolsk1760s

'Sibir' was not the Tatars' name for their capital

It is commonly believed that Sibir is the Tatar name of the place, meaning a capital or residence. But if one asks the Tobolsk Tatars about this, it is quite unknown to them. They call the place Isker instead

Working translation

Original wording

Man glaubt insgemein, Sibir sey die Tatarische Benennung des Orts, welche soviel, als eine Hauptstadt, oder Residenz, bedeute. Fraget man aber die Tobolskischen Tataren nach diesem Umstande, so ist ihnen solcher ganz unbekannt. Sie nennen vielmehr den Ort Isker

Editorial context

Müller corrects a common belief that 'Sibir' is the Tatar word for the capital. When he asked the Tobolsk Tatars, they did not know the word at all; they called the place Isker. He thinks the name Sibir arose in Russia, taken from the Permians or Zyrians who first brought news of the land, and was only later attached to the Tatar capital.

Gerhard Friedrich Müller (Sammlung Russischer Geschichte, vol. 6, 1761-1762)

Strange talesTobolsk and the Irtyshrecorded 1670 and in the 1730s

The Tatar tale of Chinese who fled when the forest grew

They say that at first all Siberia was a bare steppe without forest, and when at that time the Chinese lived there, forest little by little began to grow in various places, at which the people took fright, because the land became as if horned, and moved to their present dwelling. This tale can still be heard by word of mouth from the Tobolsk Tatars.

Working translation

Original wording

Сказываютъ, якобы сперва вся Сибирь была голая степь безъ лѣсу, а какъ въ оное время Китайцы тамъ жительство свое имѣли, то помалу по разнымъ мѣстамъ началъ лѣсъ расти, чего народъ испужавшись, понеже земля подобно какъ рогатая стала, на нынѣшнее жилище переселился. Сію повѣсть еще и нынѣ у Тобольскихъ Татаръ изустно слышать можно.

Editorial context

Müller reports a story still told orally by the Tobolsk Tatars: Siberia was once a bare steppe where the Chinese lived, but when forest began to grow everywhere the people took fright, 'as the land became as if horned', and moved away to their present home. He says it was also written down in 1670 on the orders of the Tobolsk governor Pyotr Godunov. Müller calls it a false tale that no reasonable person can believe, and notes that forest could easily grow over abandoned sites in 450 years without any miracle.

Gerhard Friedrich Müller, reporting Tobolsk Tatar oral tradition (Russian edition, 1787 issue)

Original wording

Сіе мѣсто есть такъ именуемое старинной Татарской городъ и крѣпость Сибирь, котораго остатки повыше Тобольска … еще и теперь видны. … Подлинное Татарское имя сей старинной крѣпости есть Искеръ, которымъ именемъ Татаре еще и понынѣ оную называютъ.

CitiesTobolsk1675

Tobolsk: churches on the bluff, Russians and Tatars below

And the town of Tobolsk is built on the Irtysh river on a high place, on a bluff, and the place is very beautiful, and there are many churches of God, and houses, and people live in the upper town; and below the hill there is also a great trading quarter (posad), in which live many Russian people and Tatars.

Working translation

Original wording

А городъ Тоболескъ построенъ на рѣкѣ Иртышѣ на высокомъ мѣстѣ, на яру, и мѣсто зѣло красивое, и множество церквей Божіихъ, и дворовъ, и людей живутъ въ верхнемъ городѣ; такъ же и подъ горою посадъ великой, въ которомъ живутъ множество Рускихъ людей и Татаръ.

Editorial context

Spafarii set out from Tobolsk, then the capital of Russian Siberia, in May 1675. He describes a town in two levels: churches, houses and people in the upper town on the high bluff over the Irtysh, and a large trading quarter under the hill where many Russians and Tatars lived. From here, he adds, roads ran to Bukhara, to Kazan and to other states.

Nikolai Spafarii (ed. Yu. V. Arsenyev, 1882)

CitiesIrtysh between Tobolsk and TaraJune 1734

Two villages for every village: summer and winter

It is a general custom among the Tatars here that they have one village for the summer and another for the winter. As soon as the cold sets in they move into their winter village, and as soon as the warm weather comes, into the summer village.

Working translation

Original wording

Dieses ist unter den hiesigen Tataren eine allgemeine Gewohnheit, daß sie ein anderes Dorf für den Sommer, ein anderes für den Winter haben. Sobald die Kälte einfällt, ziehen sie in ihr Winterdorf, und sobald die Wärme kommt, in das Sommerdorf.

Editorial context

Sailing up the Irtysh, Gmelin passed Uttus aul, which was really three villages: two for summer and one for winter. This, he says, was the general custom of the local Tatars, who moved into the winter village when the cold set in. He adds that 'aul' is the Tatar word for village, and that Russians call every Tatar village 'yurty', a Tatar word that simply means 'house'.

Johann Georg Gmelin (German original, Göttingen, 1751)

Horse races before a Tatar wedding

It is an old custom at all Tatar weddings that many horses must first put on this show before the wedding begins. And so that there may always be riders, and people to lend horses for it, various prizes are put up both by the bridegroom's side and by the bride's

Working translation

Original wording

Es ist dieses eine alte hergebrachte Gewohnheit bey allen Tatarischen Hochzeiten, daß viele Pferde, ehe die Hochzeit angehet, vorher dieses Schauspiel machen müssen. Und damit es allezeit Reuter und auch Leute, die die Pferde dazu lehnen, geben möge, so sind sowohl von Seiten des Bräutigams, als der Braut verschiedene Preise ausgesetzt

Editorial context

Gmelin and Müller drove out to a Tatar wedding at Sabanaka near Tobolsk. Before any wedding, he was told, riders race their horses to the bride's house for prizes put up by both families; this time they included red damask, a fox pelt, cotton cloths brought from the Kalmyk lands and a red horsehide. A few pages on, the akhun (Muslim cleric) told the groom his chief duty: not to take another wife without the consent of the one he was marrying.

Johann Georg Gmelin (German original, Göttingen, 1751)

CitiesTobolskc. 1609

Tobolsk, capital of the Siberian cities

From Tinnen one reaches Tobolsca, the capital of the Siberian cities; there is the seat of Siberia and the supreme Viceroy of the Muscovites … Also Tartars from the south come at a great distance from Tartary, and many other nations who gradually arrive there, in proportion as the fame of the country spreads wider.

Editorial context

Tobolsk, founded by the Russians in 1587, is described as the capital of the Siberian towns, seat of the Tsar's viceroy, where tribute from both sides of the Ob is collected and sent to Moscow. Tartars come from the south from far inside Tartary to trade. Massa adds a pointed comparison: would that the 'cruel Spaniards' had taken America as peacefully.

Isaac Massa's description of Siberia, as printed by Hessel Gerritsz in 1612 (English translation by F. J. Millard in the 1878 facsimile edition)

CitiesTobolsk1719

Tobolsk: Russian brick above, Tartar streets below

Formerly the Tartar princes had their abode at a place about thirty verst south from Tobolsky, which is now neglected and ruinous. … Under the hill in the suburbs, along the banks of the river, are several large streets, called the Tartar-streets, occupied by the remains of the ancient inhabitants of these parts.

Editorial context

Bell notes that the Tartar princes of Siberia once lived about thirty versts from Tobolsk at a place now 'neglected and ruinous' (he does not name it; it is probably the old khanate capital Isker, or Kashlyk). The Russian town on the hill has a brick wall, the governor's palace and brick churches, while the Tatars, descendants of the old inhabitants, live in several 'Tartar-streets' in the lower suburb along the river, with freedom of religion and trade.

John Bell (Travels, 1763)
