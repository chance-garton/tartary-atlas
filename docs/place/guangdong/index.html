The viceroy's guard: 'Tartars by nation, not Chinese'

Editorial contextIn the account of the Franciscan friars who entered China in 1579, Mendoza describes the Guangdong viceroy's guard of more than two thousand men in silk livery and shining iron helmets with great plumes, swords on the stairs and lances in the courtyards with an arquebusier between each pair. The whole guard, the friars understood, 'was of men who were Tartars by nation, and not Chinese', and they could not find out why, though they asked.

The viceroy's guard (which was more than two thousand men) was drawn up in a line, all dressed in silk livery, and on their heads very shining iron helmets with very large plumes. ... This whole guard (as they understood) was of men who were Tartars by nation, and not Chinese, without their being able to learn the reason, though they inquired diligently.

Working translation

Original wording

Estaua la guarda del Virey (que passauan de dos mill hombres) puesta en hylera, y todos vestidos de librea de seda, y en las cabezas, zeladas de yerro muy relucientes, con muy grandes plumages. … Toda esta guarda (segun entendieron) era de hombres Tartaros de nacion, y no de Chinos, sin poder sauer la caussa, aunque la inquirieron con diligencia.

Juan González de Mendoza (Historia del gran Reyno de la China, Rome 1585), from the report of the Franciscan mission of 1579 (Pedro de Alfaro and companions)

About Tartar soldiers in Ming service

Keep in mind

The friars saw the guard but could not confirm who the men were; 'Tartars' may mean Mongols or other northern soldiers in Ming service.

Daily lifeGuangdong, on the river north of CantonMarch 1656

A Tartar archer hits a palm-sized mark three times

Editorial contextWhile the embassy camped ashore, the Tartar escort put on a display of arms. One bowman shot three arrows in a row through a target no bigger than a hand at thirty-five paces and was paid a small coin. A small eyewitness scene that matches the Manchus' fame as archers.

The Tartars in the mean time, to show some pastime, exercised their Arms before the Tent of the Embassadours, which was performed by them with much dexterity. Amongst the rest, there was one with a Bow and Arrow, who was so rare a Marks-man, that he shot thrice together through the white, no broader then the Palm of a Hand, at the distance of thirty five Paces

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)
