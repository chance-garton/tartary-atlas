Felt houses on wheels, drawn by twenty oxen

Hammer's chapter on dwellings, food and women pulls together Rubruck, Carpini and Marco Polo. The tents were of felt and set on wheels, like the wagon-houses of Horace's Scythians; the chimney-like top was of white felt whitened with lime or powdered bone to shine from afar against the dark tent; the south-facing door was closed by a felt embroidered with vines, trees, birds or beasts. The largest, rectangular and up to thirty feet long, were drawn by twenty-odd oxen and called chargah; round ones were called tscherke. A footnote adds that Timur's chargah was made of two hundred gilded, jewelled tents.

Their tents were of felt and on wheels, like the tents of the Scythians in Horace; their top, shaped almost like a chimney, was of white felt, whitewashed with lime or with white dust of crushed bone … These tents were of different shape and size; the largest, an oblong rectangle, often up to thirty feet long and drawn by twenty-odd oxen, were called chargah

Twenty-seven days through ruined cities

After leaving the Khazar king, Sallam says, his party crossed a black, foul-smelling land, then travelled twenty (or twenty-seven) days among ruined cities. They were told these were the remains of cities overrun by Gog and Magog. Near the wall they found forts held by Arabic- and Persian-speaking Muslims with schools and mosques, who had never heard of the caliph.

we passed twenty (twenty-seven) days in the midst of ruined cities. We were told these were the remains of cities once invaded and laid waste by the peoples of Gog and Magog. At last we came to fortresses built near the mountains … There we found people who spoke Arabic and Persian. They are Muslims and can read the Koran

About Peoples of the steppe; ruins blamed on Gog and Magog

A ninth-century tale: the ruins are real or imagined, but the explanation (Gog and Magog) is what Sallam was told, not something he could check.

The Horse Lord's people live in felt domes

The preface divides the known world among four 'lords': elephants in the south, treasure in the west, men (China) in the east, and horses in the cold north. The northern horse peoples are fierce, live in felt tents and domed yurts, and move 'like birds' after their herds. It is a learned Buddhist scheme rather than an eyewitness scene, but it is a compact Chinese picture of steppe housing.

In the north is the Horse Lord, whose land is bitterly cold and suited to horses... The ways of the Horse Lord's people: fierce by nature, hardened to killing; they live in felt tents and domed yurts, dwelling like birds and following their herds.
