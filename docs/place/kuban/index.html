BuildingsMadzhary (Kuma River, North Caucasus)About 1600 to 1627

Seven Tatar mosques at Majar on the Kuma

On the right bank of the Kuma the book lists '7 Tatar mosques, the Majar yurt', and another mosque across the river, the 'Chrak-Keshen'. These are the ruins of Majar, a Golden Horde town. The word 'keshen' (keshene, a domed tomb) appears again on the Dnieper, where the book records '7 keshens of Tatar mosques'. The clerks credit the ruins to Tatars.

And along the river Kuma, on the right side, are 7 Tatar mosques, the Majar yurt, and a mosque on the other side of the river Kuma, Chrak-Keshen.

BuildingsMadzhari, Kuma River (Stavropol Krai)September 1772

Madzhari: domed brick houses with glazed tiles

Gmelin rode up the Kuma to the ruins of Madzhari, which he calls 'the splendid Scythian city', a raised square 5 versts across. The best-kept buildings stood round the edge, like the fortified houses of great men. They were square, eight-sided or round, 4 to 9 fathoms high, with pyramid-shaped domes reached by narrow hidden stairs in the walls, vaulted cellar passages, and inlaid tiles glazed blue, green and red in triangles, rhombs, crosses and hearts, just as at Selitrennoe. He notes that the bricks were exactly like those the Astrakhan Tatars still made, and that clay, not lime, was mostly used as mortar.

The bricks are such as the Astrakhan Tatars still make today, namely broader and thicker than our usual bricks. For the masonry, mortar of lime and sand is used only here and there, mostly plain clay; but almost all the rooms are plastered inside with lime, smoothed and whitewashed. … The shape of the buildings still standing is square, octagonal or round.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Ziegel sind so, wie sie die Astrachanischen Tataren noch jetzo machen, nemlich breiter und dicker als unsere gewöhnlichen Backsteine. Zum Mauern ist nur hie und da Mörtel von Kalk und Sand, meistens bloßer Thon gebraucht; fast alle Zimmer aber sind inwendig mit Kalk beworfen, geebnet und übertüncht. … Die Gestalt der noch erhaltenen Gebäude ist viereckig, achteckig oder rund.

Samuel Gottlieb Gmelin (part 4, compiled by P. S. Pallas, 1784)

Daily lifeYegorlyk river, Stavropol region16 July 1773

A camp of two hundred tents on the Yegorlyk

Güldenstädt camped beside a Nogai aul of about 200 kibitkas (felt tents). He admired their horses, broad in croup and chest with short thick necks, and their big greyish-white cattle, but noted that most sheep had died in the war's many marches. The herders had nothing to offer but butter and sour milk, which they eagerly traded for millet, since grain was short until the harvest.

We stood here beside an aul (camp) some 200 kibitkas (tents) strong, which is part of the Onokush aul and belongs to Yedisan. … One saw here among these Nogais a great herd of horses and cattle. … Apart from butter and sour milk nothing could be had from this herding people, which they eagerly brought to us

Original wording

Wir standen hier neben einem an 200 Kibitken starken Aaul, der ein Theil vom Onokusch Aul ist und zu Jedissan gehört. … Man sahe hier bey diesen Nagaijern ein große Heerde von Pferden und Rindvieh. … Außer Butter und saurer Milch konnte man bey diesen Hirten Volke nichts haben, das sie uns begierig zubrachten

Johann Anton Güldenstädt (ed. P. S. Pallas, 1791)

Strange talesKarachay (upper Kuban, North Caucasus)about 1780 (tale); recorded 1807

The long-haired forest goblin who boiled two children

Among the Karachay's many tales of mountain demons, Klaproth records the Ssalmasti, a long-haired female spirit of the woods. A villager caught one and enslaved it by hiding its cut hair, until his children revealed the hiding place; the spirit threw them into the boiling pot and fled back to the forest.

A malignant spirit in female shape, and having very long hair, which they call in their language Ssalmasti, is reported to reside in a certain wood. About twenty-five years ago one of the inhabitants of the village caught this goblin, carried it home with him, and cut off its hair, which he carefully hid, and by which means he rendered the spirit subservient to him.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814), from an Armenian informant who lived among the Karachay

CitiesMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1780s to 1807

Who pulled down Madzhar

When Klaproth arrived only two burial chapels were left. He explains that nearby colonists tore the ruins down for bricks, and that Count Paul Potemkin had most of what remained demolished to build the fortress-town of Yekaterinograd; peasants carried off the rest, and inscribed stones ended up in their yards.

The destruction of these remains of antiquity has been occasioned chiefly by the settlement of several colonies, which have established themselves in this neighbourhood, and have pulled them down for the sake of the serviceable bricks. Their total demolition, however, is to be ascribed more particularly to Count Paul Ssergeitsch Potemkin, who ordered the greatest part of the buildings remaining in his time to be taken down

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

The nameBalkaria and upper Kuban (North Caucasus)1807 (tradition)

Mountain Tartars who remember a lost capital

Klaproth reports the origin tradition of the Turkic mountain peoples the Georgians called Bassiani: their elders say they once lived on the steppe of the Kuma up to the Don, with a magnificent capital, Ckirck Madshar ('forty stone buildings'), which they identify with the ruins at Madzhar, before being driven into the high mountains.

Their elders report that they were long ago settled in the steppe of the Kuma as far as the Don… Their capital, which is said to have been very magnificent, was named Ckirck Madshar, which in their language signifies the forty stone buildings, or the forty four-wheeled waggons… They assert that the ruins of Madshar, which yet subsist, are the remains of this city.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814), reporting the elders' tradition

About Bassian Tartars (Balkars and Karachay)

Keep in mind

An oral tradition; the dates are uncertain even to the tellers.

BuildingsBudyonnovsk (Madzhary), Stavropol KraiSeptember 1793

Madzhary: streets of tombs, not a town

On the Kuma River Pallas visited the famous ruins of Upper Madzhary. In 1780 there were still thirty-two buildings, but settlers had since carted away the fine bricks. The ruins stood in rows, which made 'the ignorant' think they were the streets of a large town; Pallas concludes it was a great cemetery of domed sepulchral mosques. (Madzhar is now known to have been a Golden Horde trading town of the 1300s.)

…they have formerly stood in rows; a circumstance that has induced the ignorant to suppose they were the streets of a large town. But, upon close investigation, it appears that this place was a famous cemetry appropriated to persons of distinction;

Peter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

About a Muslim people 'perhaps of Tartar origin'

Keep in mind

His 'cemetery, not a town' verdict reflects his view that nomads did not build towns.

CitiesBudyonnovsk (ruins of Majar), Kuma riverJuly 1773

Whose town was Madshar?

From inscriptions he could read, Güldenstädt dated the town to about 500 years before his visit. The mosques and Muslim tombs told him the people were Muslims, and the history of the region told him they were Nogais. So, he says, the older idea that the ruins belonged to the Magyars (Hungarians), prompted by the name, should be dropped: if Hungarians ever lived here, the Tatars must have driven them out earlier.

From some inscriptions I recognised that the place … was inhabited about 500 years ago, and the style of the prayer-houses and mausolea shows that the inhabitants were Mohammedans and, according to the history of this region, Nogais. The present-day Madschars or Hungarians therefore lose the claim to these ruins that people had granted them

Working translation

Original wording

Aus einigen Inschrifften habe ich erkannt, daß der Ort … etwan vor 500 Jahren bewohnt gewesen, und die Bauart der Bethäuser und Mausoläen zeigt, daß die Einwohner Mohamedaner, und nach der Geschichte dieser Gegend Nogaier gewesen. Die heutigen Madscharen oder Ungarn verlieren also das Recht, welches man ihnen auf diese Trümmer zugestand

Johann Anton Güldenstädt (ed. P. S. Pallas, 1791)

About Nogai Tatars; Hungarians (Magyars)

Keep in mind

The Nogai attribution is Güldenstädt's inference. The town flourished under the Golden Horde in the 1300s (Ibn Battuta passed through it), before the Nogai Horde became the steppe power.

Strange talesMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1728 to 1807

The myth that Hungarians came from Madzhar

Klaproth traces a legend: in 1728 Gerber saw 'handsome stone houses' at Madzhar and guessed from the name that the Hungarians (Magyars) came from there, a notion copied onto maps. Klaproth calls it absurd: the name is Tatar for stone buildings, and the ruins are Golden Horde.

It is yet called Madshar, an appellation which is given by the Poles and Turks to the Hungarians, and by which the latter commonly distinguish themselves. It is probable that the founders of the kingdom of Hungary were originally from this country. Thus we see that Gerber was the first who broached the absurd opinion that the Hungarians derived their origin from Madshar

Johann Gustav Gerber (1728), quoted by Klaproth, followed by Klaproth's comment (English translation, 1814)

BuildingsSteppe between the Kalaus and Yegorlyk rivers, Stavropol region13 July 1773

A stone woman with a Nogai face

Crossing the steppe north of the Caucasus, Güldenstädt stopped at a crude, very stout female statue of shell conglomerate on a low mound, facing east, hands over the belly, four strings of beads round the neck and a peaked cap on the head. He thought the face and headdress Nogai, and a page later concluded that such statues were raised in memory of noble Nogais buried there. He met more of them all the way to the Donets and the Dnieper.

Here, to the right of the road, stood a shapeless female statue of ordinary human size but, in proportion, extraordinarily stout … four loosely hanging strings of corals surround the short neck; the head is very large and round, the face flat and broad, the nose quite flat … the physiognomy is Nogai

BuildingsMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1772

Madzhar's brick buildings, described in 1772

Klaproth quotes the naturalist S. G. Gmelin, who saw the ruins in 1772 when they were best preserved: a great city on a plateau, with fortified mansions at the edge. The bricks were like those Astrakhan Tatars still made; walls were bonded mostly with clay and plastered and whitewashed inside; buildings were square, octagonal or round, up to nine fathoms high and topped by pyramids or domes.

The bricks resemble those still made by the Tartars of Astrachan, that is to say, they are broader and thicker than ours. In the external walls, a mortar composed of lime and sand is used only here and there, the cement generally employed being clay alone… The figure of the buildings yet preserved is square, octagonal, and circular.

Samuel Gottlieb Gmelin (visit of 1772), in Klaproth's extract (English translation, 1814)

About Golden Horde Tatars

Keep in mind

Gmelin called it 'a magnificent Scythian city'; Klaproth corrects the attribution to the Golden Horde.

BuildingsMadzhary (Kuma River, Stavropol Krai)1780 to 1793 (reports); written 1801

Ruins 'all of Tartar origin', and a palace the Kalmyks would not live in

In his appendix Matthew Guthrie lists brick ruins on the Akhtuba, at Madzhary on the Kuma (32 buildings in 1780, only four by 1793) and near the Terek, which Pallas had drawn, and calls them all of Tartar origin from their shape, construction and inscriptions. He follows Pallas in thinking them tombs or shrines, because Mongols so loved camp life that the Kalmyk princes of his day would not live in a palace Russia built for them.

all of Tartar origin, as is evident from several kinds of proof, viz. shape, construction, and inscriptions… I am of Pallas's opinion with regard to the sepulchral or religious destination of all those ruins, from the known attachment of the Monguls to a camp residence, which to this day prevents their descendants, the Calmuc princes, from inhabiting a palace built for them by the crown of Russia

Matthew Guthrie (editor's Appendix, 1802)

About Golden Horde Tatars; Kalmyks

Keep in mind

Second-hand from Pallas; Klaproth (W056) later argued Madzhary was a real town, not just a cemetery.

BuildingsBudyonnovsk (ruins of Majar), Kuma riverJuly 1773

Fifty brick tombs at Madshar

At the famous ruins of Madshar on the Kuma, Güldenstädt counted about fifty buildings of fired brick. He insists they were not houses but mausolea, each over an underground vault where the coffins stood. West of them were two ruined mosques, one still with its minaret. Of ordinary houses nothing was left, and he guesses they had been built of light timber frame and wattle, like local houses in his own day.

Madshar … contains … about 50 different buildings of brick … They are not dwelling houses but mausolea, all provided with an underground vault … Between the two [mosques] there may well have been dwellings … Presumably they were built in the manner still usual in these parts, of light timber framing and wattle.

BuildingsMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1807 (arguing about the 14th century)

Proof that Madzhar was built by Kipchak Tatars

Klaproth sets out his evidence that Madzhar was a Tatar town: the shape of its buildings and burial chapels, which match a Tatar cemetery near Tbilisi; walls decorated with coloured glazed tiles, a Tatar and Mongol fashion; Arabic gravestones; coins of the Golden Horde; and mentions in Asian histories. He also opened a brick vault himself and found a wooden coffin.

The following facts, however, afford incontestable proofs that Madshar was a town built and inhabited by Ckipdshack Tartars. I. The form of the buildings and sepulchral chapels is characteristic of Southern Asia… The fashion of adorning the walls with tiles, which are glazed on one side with different colours, is also Tartar and Mongol.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

About Kipchak Tatars (Golden Horde)

The stone houses of ruined Madjar on the Kuma

Describing the Kuma steppe, the book says the land once had settled people, as shown by ruined towns. On the left bank of the Kuma lie the ruins that Kalmyks call Madsharinka Balgusun, 'the beautiful city Madjar': a ditched enclosure with house foundations, gravestones with inscriptions and seven stone houses still standing. One Arabic inscription was said to record that the people of Madjar left the city for lack of wood and moved into the mountains.

… houses and various gravestones with inscriptions, and also seven standing stone houses of moderate size. The gravestones are said to have partly Arabic, partly unknown inscriptions. On one with an Arabic inscription it is said to read that the people of Madjar left the city because of a shortage of wood and moved into the mountains.

A Muslim gravestone dated 1346

Klaproth read Arabic gravestones at Madzhar: one for 'Sina, son of Mohammed, son of Chalil' of AH 747 (1346-1347), another for a judge of AH 777 (1375-1376), which he carried away. Here is buried the deceased, who needs the mercy of God in eternity, Sina, son of Mohhammed, the son of Chalil..... in the year of the era seven-and-forty and seven hundred.

CitiesMadzhary; Sarai (lower Volga)1274 to 1408 (coins)

Coins struck at Sarai, capital of the Golden Horde

Klaproth reads the coins found at Madzhar: nearly all were minted at Sarai or New Sarai, the Golden Horde capitals, under khans from Mengu-Timur to Tokhtamysh and Pulad (1270s to 1408), some with names in Mongol script. One is struck at Astrakhan. The latest coin helps date the town's end to about 1400.

The great Ssulthan Ghajats Eddunia (the prop of the world) Toktégu-chan, the Just… Coined at Ssarai, the capital, in the year 715; that is, A.D. 1315.

Julius Klaproth, transcribing coin legends (English translation, 1814)

Were the builders of Madzhari Tatars? Gmelin guesses 'Scythians'

Gmelin admits he knows too little medieval history to say who lived at Madzhari. He reports that in 1735, when Tatars still held the district, the Astrakhan governor Tatishchev sent an armed party to search the ruins and received a writing on blue, very strong paper and some coins, which Tatishchev took for 'Scythian'. Gmelin guesses a 'Scythian tribe called Madshar' lived there and mentions a claim that the Ingush of the Caucasus descend from them. On the previous page he says the Tatar grave mounds around the site probably did not belong to Madzhari, since the Madzharians 'were probably not Tatars'.

The former Astrakhan governor Tatishchev sent some people in 1735, when the Tatars still held this district, … to investigate the Madzhari ruins … They brought him a writing on blue, very strong paper and various coins, which this historian took for Scythian. … Presumably a Scythian tribe called Madshar lived here

Original wording

Strange talesBudyonnovsk (Madzhary), Stavropol KraiSeptember 1793

A city driven out by tarantulas

A story said the people of the 'city' at Madzhary had been driven away by swarms of great tarantulas, which is why the stream is called Byvalla, from the Tartar 'by' (tarantula) and 'valla' (bad). Pallas says there is no basis for it: despite searching diligently, he never found a single tarantula there.

There is no foundation for believing what is related of this supposed city, that its inhabitants have been expelled by great numbers of the large tarantula, or Phalangium araneodes, from which traditionary account the rivulet Byvalla has received its name; By, being the Tartar word for the tarantula, and valla, signifying bad or evil.

Peter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

BuildingsSteppe between the Kuban, Terek, Don and DnieperSeptember 1793

Stone statues on the burial mounds

Along the Tashla Pallas saw the rough-hewn stone statues that stand on burial mounds across the steppe from the Kuban to the Dnieper, always facing east. From their dress and faces he judged that a people related to the Mongols made them. In a footnote on the next page he quotes Rubruck, who credited them to the Cumans, and argues that different monuments on the steppe came from different nations. (Most are now thought to be Cuman (Polovtsian) work of the eleventh to thirteenth centuries.)

If we may judge from the costume and form of the face, they uniformly appear to have been made by a people related to the Mongolians, who formerly inhabited those countries. … All these statues are erected on moderately large sepulchral hills, and their faces are exposed towards the East.

Peter Simon Pallas (English translation, 1812 edition)

The Kuban Tartars, raiders subject to the Turks

Describing the Torghut country between the Volga and the Yaik, Tulishen places to the west the Ho-pang, which Staunton identifies as the Cuban Tartars, also called Mang-vu-te (Mangyt, a Nogai clan name). lies the country of the Ho-pang (Cuban Tartars) otherwise called Mang-vu-te, a nation subject to Kung-ke-eur Khan, king of the Tu-li-ye-se-ko, (Turks). This people are constantly in the practice of coming across the boundary, in order to attack and plunder their Russian and Tourgouth neighbours.

Tulishen (translated from the Chinese by George Thomas Staunton, 1821)

A Nogai murza's fresh grave of cut stone

Opposite the mouth of the Tashla, Güldenstädt found the new grave of a Yedisan Nogai murza buried the previous winter. Blocks of shell conglomerate, dug from the grave pit itself and squared, had been laid loose in a man-sized rectangle about three feet high. A second grave like it lay further on. It shows a living steppe people marking graves with cut stone, and on the same page he suggests the same rock could supply building blocks for the planned Russian colonies.

[the grave] of a Yedisan murza buried here last winter. Shell conglomerates cut into square blocks, taken from the pit of the grave itself, had been laid loose one on another, about 3 feet high, in an oblong rectangle proportioned to a man's size.

Karachay marriage and the 'price of blood'

Klaproth describes the Karachay as usually monogamous and unusually kind to their wives. As among other Tartars, the bride's parents receive a kalym (bride price), here called 'the price of blood'; a rich groom sends the bride a full outfit and she is brought to him at night, followed by three days of feasting and dancing.

In general they take only one wife… so that here, as among the Europeans, the wife is the companion, and not the menial servant, of the husband… Among them, as among the other Tartars, the parents, on the marriage of a daughter, receive a kalim, which is here termed the price of blood.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

About Karachay Tartars

Keep in mind

Klaproth's Karachay material came mainly from one Armenian informant.

The Natukhai, 'originally Tartars'

Vorontsov told Spencer he had been visited at Anapa by a prince of the Natukhai, a tribe 'originally Tartars' who had fled into the Caucasus after Russia conquered the lands of the Kuban and Azov. Spencer calls them Russia's most uncompromising enemies there. The Natukhai are usually counted as a Circassian (Adyghe) tribe.

he had been honoured with a visit from a Circassian prince, chief of the Natouhay tribe, originally Tartars, who had fled into the mountains of the Caucasus upon the conquest of the provinces on the banks of the Kouban and the sea of Azov.

Edmund Spencer, reporting Count Vorontsov

About Natukhai (Circassian tribe said to be of Tatar origin)

Keep in mind

The Tatar origin is a claim reported at second hand.
