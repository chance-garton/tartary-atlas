Northeastern Siberia

What the sources say here: 2 passages

The nameSredne Kolymsk, Sakha Republic1821

Once they had writing, and knew lions and tigers

Near Sredne Kolymsk Wrangel befriended an 82-year-old Yakut who read and wrote Russian. The old man said his people had been more civilised before they split from 'the other Tartar races', and had once had their own writing, since lost. As proof that they once lived far to the south, he quoted old sayings that mention gold, gems, lions and tigers, things Yakuts no longer knew.

He complained of the ignorance of his countrymen, who he said, had been formerly more civilized; and that before they separated from the other Tartar races, to whom they are allied, they had possessed written characters, and consequently means of intellectual cultivation, which they have since lost. He maintained, that his race had once inhabited far distant southern regions

Who Yakuts (Sakha)

Heard itFerdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840), reporting a Yakut elder

Keep in mind. One elder's memory of lost traditions, which he himself says were fading. Old Turkic peoples of the south did use runic writing, but the elder gives no specifics.

Daily lifeKolyma River, Sakha Republic1821

A priest who burned the idols

Wrangel reports that the Kolyma Yakuts were now all baptised. A priest named Slezzow, twenty years earlier, had destroyed every idol and pagan altar he found, by fire or water. Yet shamans still had followers and were consulted, even by Russians, to find stray animals or thieves. A footnote adds that newborn girls had once often been left in baskets hung in trees.

The Jakuti are now all baptized. A priest from Sredne Kolymsk visits these settlements every year. An ecclesiastic named Slezzow, who lived here twenty years ago, showed undaunted and unwearied zeal in the abolition of Schamanism. Wherever he discovered idols or heathen altars, they were destroyed by fire or by water, and none such are now to be met with.

Who Yakuts (Sakha) of the Kolyma

Heard itFerdinand von Wrangel (English translation by Mrs Sabine, 1840)

Keep in mind. Wrangel approves of the conversion; the destruction he describes means few Yakut sacred objects survived to be seen.
