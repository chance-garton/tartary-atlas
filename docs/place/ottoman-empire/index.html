'Their name by no means indicates their origin'

The Tartars are public couriers, much respected for their good conduct and fidelity. Their name by no means indicates their origin, as they are taken indifferently from all the provinces in the empire, and are distinguished by the Tartar calpac, which they wear instead of the turban.

Editorial context

In a footnote Clarke quotes Thomas Thornton's book on Turkey (1809) to explain the couriers: 'Tartar' was the name of the job, not of a nation. The men came from every province and were marked out by the Tartar calpac (fur cap) they wore instead of a turban. A good reminder that 'Tartar' could mean a role or a costume.

Thomas Thornton, The Present State of Turkey (1809), quoted in a footnote by Clarke

The nameIstanbul1821

Turkish as 'a dialect of Tartar'

Since the Turkish language is written and spoken in several provinces of Persia, we think we should insert here some reflections taken from a grammar we composed some time ago … This language, we say, is a dialect of Tartar, brought by the Ottomans to Constantinople in 1453.

Working translation

Original wording

Comme la langue turque est écrite et parlée dans plusieurs provinces de la Perse, nous croyons devoir insérer ici quelques réflexions extraites d'une grammaire que nous avons composée il y a quelque temps … Cette langue, disons-nous, est un dialecte du tartare, apporté par les Ottomans à Constantinople en 1453.

Editorial context

In a note drawn from his own Turkish grammar, Jaubert calls Turkish a dialect of 'Tartar' brought to Constantinople by the Ottomans in 1453, later enriched with Arabic and Persian words. For him 'Tartar' is the name of the whole Turkic language family.

Pierre-Amedee Jaubert (footnote from his Turkish grammar)
