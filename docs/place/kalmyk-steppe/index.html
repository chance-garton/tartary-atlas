Daily lifeKalmyk steppeJune 1823

Left on the steppe for the dogs

Zwick found the body of a Kalmyk woman laid out in furs under a felt, with wooden cups and utensils beside her. This, he says, was the normal way of disposing of commoners, whose bodies were eaten by dogs and vultures; the dogs that fed on them were then driven off as unclean. Princes and Lamas, by contrast, were cremated with ceremony, and their ashes mixed with mortar for a chapel or tomb.

At some distance from our tent, we found the corpse of a Calmuc woman, laid out in a fur dress, and covered with coarse felt. Wooden drinking vessels, and other unimportant utensils, were laid by her side. This is the common way of disposing of the dead, amongst the Calmucs of inferior rank, so that the bodies are usually devoured by dogs and vultures.

Breaking into a Kalmyk tomb

Adèle describes how the party tricked their Kalmyk camel-drivers, broke into a satza (a small tomb-shrine for a high priest's ashes) through a window, 'took possession' of it with a silk handkerchief, and carried off two clay statues and some images. She knows the Kalmyks would see it as the worst sacrilege and treats it as a lark. A startling reminder of how European travellers collected 'curiosities'.

Like generous victors we contented ourselves with taking two small statues, and a few images. According to the notions of the Kalmucks, no sacrilege can compare with that of which we were now guilty. Yet no celestial fire reduced us to ashes

'No Cities nor fix'd Habitations'

Summing up the Kalmyks, who ranged from Mongolia to the Volga, Avril says plainly that they had no cities or fixed homes, only neat, comfortable felt tents, and that no people could strike camp faster. On the same page he calls them idolaters who, like the Mongols, look to the Dalai Lama.

The Calmoucs have no Cities nor fix'd Habitations, but only live in Tents made of Felts, which are very neat, and very commodious; nor is there any Nation so nimble at Camping and Decamping in an instant as they, there being no Nation so much accustom'd to it, in regard they never stay long in a place, and are always making Incursions.

Who Kalmyks (Oirat Mongols)

Heard itPhilippe Avril (English translation, 1693)

Keep in mind. Avril met Kalmyks near Astrakhan, but this general statement comes from Moscow informants.

BuildingsSarpa steppe, Kalmykia1823

How a Kalmyk felt tent is put together

Zwick opens a whole chapter on the Kalmyk tent, called gerr by its owners and kibitka by Russians. He describes a round lattice wall of willow laths, painted in red stripes, that folds flat in seven or eight sections, with poles rising to a wooden crown and the whole covered in large felts tied down with woollen girths. It is one of the most exact eyewitness descriptions of a steppe yurt in English before 1850.

The Calmuc huts or tents, in their own language gerr, and in the Russian kibitka, consist of a number of parts, which are easily put together, and taken to pieces. The tents are round, with a funnel-shaped roof, and a blunt point at the top of it. The frame-work, which is of willow laths about an inch thick, carved and painted in red stripes, forms a circular wall of lattice-work

A whole horde on the move

The missionaries travelled with Erdeni's horde as it moved camp. The column was more than a verst wide: strings of camels carrying the tents, goods and children in baskets, then the herds. No one walked; even mothers with babies rode. Zwick describes the migration as a festive show at which families displayed their best clothes and carpets.

The main body of this moving multitude extended more than a verst in breadth, and consisted of single columns of camels, bearing tents, household goods, and children, who were stowed in baskets; next followed troops of horses, cattle and sheep … Nobody performs a migration on foot … we even saw mothers on horseback with infants, who were hardly out of the cradle

The Kalmyk kibitka: a felt house two camels can carry

A precise description of the felt tent (kibitka) from people who stayed in them: a cylinder as high as a man's shoulder, a conical roof open at the top for smoke, a wooden ring held up by many spars. The whole thing comes apart and can be carried by two camels. On the next page they add that the felts kept out the fiercest storms, as they learned from experience.

Their dwellings are felt tents, called kibitkas by the Russians. They are four or five yards in diameter, cylindrical to the height of a man's shoulder, with a conical top, open at the apex to let the smoke escape. The frame is light, and can be taken asunder for the convenience of carriage.

CitiesSarpa steppe, KalmykiaJune 1823

A prince's camp laid out like a town

The horde's headquarters had a fixed plan. The prince's tent, a tent serving as the hall of justice and the princess's tent stood on one side of the wells, the temple tents and the Lama's huts on the other, with the lesser priests in a semicircle around them and the prince's officials and servants ringed outside. Zwick adds that every door faced the main temple. Erdeni's camp held about 100 tents.

To the north of these were the royal tents, viz. that of Erdeni himself, the hall of justice, and the tent of Princess Mingmer ; to the south, the Churulls (or temples) and the huts of the High Priest, or Lama. Round these, in a wide semicircle, were the tents of the inferior priests (or Gellongs,) and these again were enclosed by the ministers and servants of the Prince.

Kalmyks who live in houses on carts

Evliya says the Kalmyks have no fleas, lice, bedbugs, snakes, scorpions or tarantulas among them, because their round houses stand on carts and they live on the open steppe. He then begins his description of their appearance: small eyes, said to come from the salt mines of the region.

Among them there are no fleas, lice, bedbugs, snakes, tarantulas or scorpions at all, because their round houses are on carts and they are desert-dwellers.

BuildingsSarpa steppe, KalmykiaJune 1823

A chapel mortared with a prince's ashes

The Kalmyks did build in stone and wood, for their dead nobles. Zwick visited a small memorial chapel (zaza) for the late Torgut prince Sandschi-Ubaschi: a wooden room about four paces square on a stone base, reached by steps, with a flat roof and a small tower. He was told that the prince's ashes had been mixed into its mortar and whitewash.

This chapel was erected to the memory of the late Torgud Prince Sandschi-Ubaschi, father of the Prince Zerren Ubaschi, and his ashes, as the Calmucs informed me, are mixed with the mortar which cements and whitens the building. The foundation of this edifice is of stone, upon which is erected a wooden chapel, with steps up to it.

The Kalmyk story of Shakyamuni

In a footnote Strahlenberg records the Kalmyk account of 'Xacamuni', that is the Buddha Shakyamuni: once a prince in India, taken up to heaven alive for his holiness, whose celibate sons explain why lamas may not marry, and one of whom brought the religion to Tibet. It is a garbled but recognisable outline of Buddhist teaching as told to a Swedish captive.

They say that 4000 Years ago, he was only a Soveraign Prince, in India; But on Account of his unparallel'd Sanctity, God had taken him up to Heaven alive; But the Place whither he was translated, none of them pretend to know.

Who Kalmyks (Buddhists)

'Kalmyk' means the ones who stayed behind

Strahlenberg rejects the view, in the French edition of Abulghazi, that 'Kalmyk' was an insult aimed at their religion. He says the name was given to them in the Tatar language and means a people that remained behind, from 'kal' (I stay) and 'umak' (tribe). A similar 'those who remained' explanation is still often repeated today.

received the name Kallmack, which is not a term of abuse on account of their religion, as the author remarks … but it means and signifies a people or tribe that stays behind or remains there, for Kall or Call means 'I stay there or behind', and Amack or Umack a tribe; hence by syncope Kallmack or Kallmuck.

A Kalmyk khan said to eat only from gold

Avril reports that the chief khan of the Kalmyks claimed descent from Tamerlane, forced Russians and Uzbeks to pay him tribute, dressed always in white and ate only from gold plate. On the next page he checks the rumour: a French officer in Moscow who had been in this khan's tent near Astrakhan, with 100,000 Tartars, confirmed his splendour but never saw gold or silver plate.

He is very Potent, and therefore forces the Muscovites and the Yousbecks to pay him a certain Tribute, or else he would be continually Harrassing their Countries. 'Tis also reported that he is very Magnificent, that he goes always clad in White, and never eats but serv'd in Gold Plate.

Who Kalmyks (Oirat Mongols)

Heard itPhilippe Avril (English translation, 1693)

Keep in mind. Rumour, partly contradicted by the eyewitness Avril cites.

Jumping through fire after childbirth

A Mongol or Kalmyk mother, Strahlenberg says, is purified after childbirth by jumping three times through a fire before the lama and her relatives. He adds that they wear or keep a small three-headed idol, made of clay or metal, brought from Tibet and the Dalai Lama. He collects these customs to show a sacred number three.

It is a Custom, to this Day, with the Mungals and Kalmucks, that when a Woman bears a Child, and is afterwards re-admitted into the Community of the People, she must, in the Presence of their Lama, or Priest, and her nearest Relations, jump Three Times thro' a Fire; Which they call the Purification.

Who Mongols and Kalmyks

Heard itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Keep in mind. He does not say he saw this himself.

Kalmyks and the hadith of the Turks

Evliya lists the many spellings of the Kalmyk name and says all historians call them the 'yellow folk' (Bani Asfar). He applies to them a hadith about God's soldiers in the east 'whose name is Turk'. He adds that twenty years earlier the Kalmyks were not on this steppe and 140 Nogay tribes grazed it, sending butter to Taman to be sold cheaply in Istanbul; when the Kalmyks arrived the Nogays were routed and butter became dear.

Kalmuk, Kalmak, Kalmaq, Kalmakh and Kalmagh: all the historians call them the people of Bani Asfar [the 'yellow folk'] … Indeed, twenty years ago this Kalmyk people were not present on this vast steppe, and all the hundred and forty tribes of the Nogay people camped and migrated on the open steppe.

Daily lifeKalmyk steppeJune 1823

Sour milk for the poor, meat for princes, brick tea for all

In summer the Kalmyks lived almost entirely on sour milk: mare's milk (tschigan) for the rich, cow's milk for the poor. Only the prince and great men ate meat, because the herds were their whole wealth. Their tea came from China by way of Siberia as hard cakes of coarse leaves and stalks, boiled with butter or sheep's-tail fat and salt.

None but the Prince, and the great men, had flesh in their kettles, for the whole wealth of the nation consists in their flocks and herds, and they are forced to use them sparingly. In winter, when they are short of milk, they buy meal from the Russians who inhabit the shores of the Volga

Grave mounds and brick foundations on the steppe

Crossing the steppe, Zwick stopped at kurgans, the grave mounds Russians call by that name. He thinks they come from many ages and peoples, but credits most of them to the Tartars of the medieval Volga empire whose ruined cities he names, and on the next page notes two mounds holding the remains of a square brick building.

Such tumuli are every where to be found on the steppes, and most plentifully on the willowy shores of the Volga … They probably belong to different ages, and different races of men, but the majority are undoubtedly to be ascribed to the Tartars … I saw a great number of these hillocks, near the ruins of their principal cities Serag and Tschigis.

Who Golden Horde Tatars (as Zwick supposes), and older peoples

Strange talesKalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)1840s

The white elephant with thirty-three heads

Summarising Kalmyk Buddhist books, the authors describe the world-mountain and its guardian elephant, two leagues long and white as snow, with thirty-three red heads, six trunks each spouting fountains, and a young virgin on every star above them. They note, in a footnote, similar marvels in medieval European cosmography. This is religious myth they report, not something anyone claimed to see.

where dwells an elephant two leagues long, white as snow, and named Gasar Sakitschin Koven (protector of the earth). This fabulous animal has thirty-three red heads, each furnished with six trunks, whence spout forth as many fountains, all surmounted with six stars.

Who Kalmyks (Buddhist cosmology)

Legend or rumourXavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

Keep in mind. A second-hand summary of Kalmyk religious books; the authors do not name the text they used.

A tent bazaar between two princes

Arriving at the 'place of heroes' whips', where two Torgut princes were camped, the missionaries found a market that had grown up in the gap between the two princely tents. Russian, Armenian and Tatar merchants lived there in their own tents and carts, forming the travelling bazaar of the horde.

Their tents were about a quarter of a mile asunder, and between them was a multitude of tents and cars belonging to the Russian, Armenian, and Tartar merchants, forming the market, or rather bazaar, of the horde.

Who Kalmyks; Russian, Armenian and Tatar traders

Stone men by the salt spring

By a stream in the Manych steppe, opposite the salt that the people of Azov gathered, the book marks 'stone men, idols'. These are the carved stone statues ('kamennye baby') that stood on steppe kurgans, set up by earlier nomads such as the Polovtsy. The clerks give no builder, only the landmark.

And along the right side of that stream are stone people, idols, opposite the salt that the Azov people take.

The Kalmyk 'Kaaba' and Alexander's wall

Evliya records what Kalmyks told him about a domed shrine they call their Kaaba, far to the east beyond a sea of ice. He adds his own learned gloss: Alexander built the Wall of Gog and Magog from every kind of metal, the mountains the Kalmyks call the Yildirak Dag, then built this dome with ancient sages and hid his treasures in it. On a following page Kalmyk elders say their ancestors died from a deafening noise behind the metal mountains.

[Alexander] built the Barrier of Alexander, the mountains, out of every kind of metal; it is the Barrier of Gog, which the Kalmyk people call the 'Yildirak Dag' [the glittering mountain]; afterwards Alexander built the dome which they call their Kaaba.
