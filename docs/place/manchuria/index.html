'Manchu' from Manjushri, the bodhisattva of wisdom

Editorial contextThe editors argue that the name Manchu (滿洲) was originally Manju, both syllables level in tone, and link it to the Tibetans' custom of addressing the Qing emperor each year as 'Great Emperor Manjushri'. Manju, they say, means 'wonderful auspiciousness'; the Chinese character zhou (continent) was borrowed because it looked like a place name, but Manchu is the name of a people, not a place.

[Examined in] our national (Manchu) script, Manzhou was originally written Manzhu, both syllables read in the level tone. Since our dynasty arose in the eastern land, every year Tibet presents red-ink letters all addressing 'the Great Emperor Manjushri'. The Fanyi mingyi (a Buddhist glossary) says: Manju in Chinese means 'wonderful auspiciousness'.

Ten thousand Tartars sent to hunt ginseng

Editorial contextJartoux writes that the emperor, wanting Tartars rather than Chinese to profit from ginseng, ordered ten thousand Tartars in 1709 to gather it, each owing him two ounces of the best, with the rest bought at its weight in fine silver. His survey party met these herb-hunters in the wilderness; on the same page he describes their troops of a hundred men advancing in a line through the forest. The letter opens with a Tartar villager near the Korean border bringing him four whole plants in a basket.

The Emperor, wishing the Tartars rather than the Chinese to profit from this trade, had ordered ten thousand Tartars that same year, 1709, to go and gather for themselves all the ginseng they could, on condition that each of them would give His Majesty two ounces of the best, and that the rest would be paid for at the weight of fine silver.

The Chinese, who with good reason are very bitter against the Tartars of Niuche, invent and mock them by saying that they live in caves and underground pits; which is false: for they keep themselves in very fine and handsome tents … of silk stuffs or cloths, so neatly and artfully coated with bright, translucent wax that they glitter and shine in the sun like a mirror

Stone stairs of a palace no Manchu built

Editorial contextNear Ningguta the Jesuits found ruined cities, one reachable only by a narrow causeway through water, with great stone staircases and palace remains finer than anything in Ningguta itself. They concluded these were the work not of the present Manchus but of the twelfth-century Kin (Jurchen), who ruled north China, built towns and palaces, and were then destroyed by the Mongols and the Chinese.

Here are also found great Stone Stair-cases, with some other Remains of a Palace … So that it is very probable, all the Monuments of Antiquity in Eastern Tartary were not the Work of the present Manchews, but of those of the 12th Century, who, under the Name of Kin chau, were Masters of the North of China

A ruined palace city with a giant stone Buddha

Editorial contextQuoting the diary of Gao Shiqi, who accompanied the Kangxi emperor to Manchuria in 1682, the gazetteer describes Huorong city: a walled site over forty li around with an inner forbidden city, the foundations of three halls strewn with broken green-glazed tiles, and outside it a great stone Buddha about three zhang high on a lotus base, with a leaning stone pagoda. Gao took it for the Jin (Jurchen) upper capital; the gazetteer's editors then use the Jin river name Anchuhu (which they equate with the Alchuka) and Song travellers' distances to argue that the Jin capital lay further west, toward the Sungari.

[Gao Shiqi's diary:] Fifteen li southeast of Shalin is Huorong city, the upper capital Huining prefecture of the Jin. It is more than forty li around; in the middle is a forbidden city about a li across, where the foundations of three halls all survive, with broken green tiles scattered over them like chess pieces. Outside the forbidden city is a great stone Buddha about three zhang high, resting on a lotus, and in front of it a stone pagoda leaning a little to the east.

Soon she bore a boy who could speak at birth.

Editorial contextHuc sets out how he uses the word: following the Tartars and Chinese themselves, he divides 'the Tartar people' into Eastern Tartars (Toung-Ta-Dze), the Manchus, and Western Tartars (Si-Ta-Dze), the Mongols. The dog cart is made of wood; it is light and simple, shaped like a boat... and pulled by several dogs.

Once a land with no towns at all

Editorial contextPolo begins his history of the Tartars by saying they originally lived on wide plains near 'Chorcha' (Manchuria) with no towns or villages, only rich pastures, and paid tribute to 'Unc Can', whom Europeans called Prester John.
