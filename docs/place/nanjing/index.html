Nanjing

What the sources say here: 6 passages

BuildingsNanjing1656

The Porcelain Tower: said to be built by Chinese on a Tartar's orders

This wonderful Pile (as they inform us) the Chineses built at their own charges, commanded by the insulting Conquerour the Tartar, to them a Pillar of Honour, to the Subdued of Obloquie, seven hundred years since, when in like manner, as lately, they over-ran all China

Editorial context

Nieuhof admired the nine-storey glazed Porcelain Tower of Nanjing and repeats what he was told: the Chinese built it at their own cost on the orders of a Tartar conqueror seven hundred years before, and the new Tartar conquerors chose to spare it. In fact the tower was built by the Ming Yongle emperor in the early 1400s, so the story he was told is wrong, but it shows buildings being linked to Tartar conquerors in popular memory.

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

About Tartars (an earlier conquest, and the Manchus)

Keep in mind

The dating and the Tartar command are hearsay and do not match the tower's known Ming date.

Daily lifeNanjing1656

A Tartar lady tries on the ambassador's hat

we past by the Gate of the old Imperial Court, where sat a great Tartar Lady, with her servants waiting upon her, about forty years of Age: She very civilly sent to our Interpreter to invite the Embassadours into her House … She was very debonair and free, looked upon our Swords, and much admired their bending without breaking: She took the Embassadours Hat, and put it on her own Head

Editorial context

Riding past the old imperial court at Nanjing, the Dutch were invited in by a Tartar lady of about forty, 'very debonair and free'. She came out to meet the ambassador, admired how their swords bent without breaking, put his hat on her own head and unbuttoned his doublet. She then served them a strong drink made of beans. A lively picture of a Manchu noblewoman's easy manners with strangers.

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

BuildingsNanjing1656

At Nanjing the conquerors live in huts; the Chinese keep the stone city

The Tartars seated themselves near to an Idol Temple called Paolinxi, where they built themselves several Hutts, leaving the Chineses to dwell in the City, and there to drive their Trade. The buildings are all of a hard sort of Stone, which the Natives have most curiously Painted with a Yellow Colour, that when the Sun reflects on them, they shine like Gold.

Editorial context

Describing the ruined Ming imperial palace at Nanjing, Nieuhof notes that the Tartar garrison settled near a temple and built themselves huts, leaving the Chinese to live and trade in the city. The palace buildings, of hard stone painted yellow, were Chinese work. On the same page he says the Tartars destroyed the palace out of hatred for the Ming family.

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

CitiesNanjingc. 1569 (describing the early 1400s)

The court moved to Beijing to face the Tartars

This province has sixteen cities. The king anciently resided in this city, because it is a very fertile, fresh and pleasant land, and moved the court to Paquim [Beijing] in order to attend more closely to the wars of the Tartars.

Working translation

Original wording

Tem esta prouincia dezaseys cidades, residio ãtiguamẽte nesta cidade el Rey por ser terra muy fertil, fresca e apraziuel, e mudou a corte pera ho paquim pera de mais perto acudir aas guerras dos Tartaros.

Editorial context

Describing the province of Nanjing, Cruz says the king once lived there because the land is fertile and pleasant, but moved his court to Beijing ('Paquim') to be nearer the wars against the Tartars. It is an early European statement of why Beijing became the capital.

Gaspar da Cruz, O.P. (Tractado, Évora 1569)

BuildingsNanjingabout 1599 (instruments from the 13th or 14th century)

Bronze star instruments from the Tartar era

Some years later Father Matteo saw similar instruments at Peking, or rather the same, so little did they differ from one another, so that there seems no doubt they were cast by the same craftsman; and it is certain they were cast at the time when the Tartars ruled the Chinese. From this we may reasonably guess that they were made by some foreigner skilled in our sciences.

Working translation

Original wording

Spectavit etiam P. Matthaeus post annos aliquot similes Pechini machinas, aut easdem potius, ita nihil aliae ab aliis discrepabant, nihil ut dubium videatur, ab eodem fusas artifice: & quidem eo tempore fusas constat, quo Tartari Sinis imperitabant. unde non temere coniectare possumus, eas ab aliquo externo perito disciplinarum nostrarum fuisse conflatas.

Editorial context

On a hill at Nanjing, Ricci inspected huge bronze astronomical instruments: armillary spheres, a globe, a gnomon on a great marble base. He later saw identical ones at Peking and concluded they came from the same caster, made when the Tartars ruled China. Because they were so well made, he guessed a foreigner trained in European sciences had built them. They were set for the wrong latitude at Nanjing, which he took as a sign they had been moved.

Matteo Ricci, edited and translated into Latin by Nicolas Trigault (1615)
See the original page (p. 363)

About Instrument makers under Tartar (Yuan) rule

Keep in mind

Ricci's guess about a foreign maker is doubtful: historians usually credit the designs to the Yuan astronomer Guo Shoujing, and the Peking set he saw was a Ming copy of the Yuan originals.

Daily lifeNanjing1695

Two million Muslims 'from Great Tartary'

The inhabitants of Nanking are not all Chinese: there are also many Moors who came from Great Tartary (Father Filippo Grimaldi assuring me that in all China there are two million of them), whose policy is not to marry their daughters except within their own sect

Working translation

Original wording

Gli abitanti, che sono in Nankin, non son tutti Cinesi, ma vi sono ancor molti Mori venuti dalla G. Tartaria (accertandomi il Padre Filippo Grimaldi, ch'in tutta la Cina ve ne siano due milioni), i quali han per politica di non casar le loro figlie, sol che nella propria Setta

Editorial context

In Nanjing Careri notes that not all inhabitants are Chinese: there are many 'Moors come from Great Tartary', two million across China according to Father Grimaldi, who marry their daughters only within their own sect and so multiply 'like locusts'. The royal palace sits in a citadel held by a Tartar (Manchu) garrison that let no one in.

Giovanni Francesco Gemelli Careri (Giro del Mondo, part IV, Naples 1700)
See the original page (p. 78)

About Chinese Muslims (Hui) 'come from Great Tartary'; Manchu garrison

Keep in mind

The figure is Grimaldi's estimate; 'from Great Tartary' reflects the belief that China's Muslims came from Central Asia.
