CitiesSudak, Crimea1222-1223

The Tatars take Sudak, the Crimean trading port

The Tatars advanced as far as the town of Sudak, which belongs to the Qipchaqs and from which they draw their grain; it lies on the Khazar Sea and ships put in there. Cloth is found there, which the Qipchaqs come to buy, giving in exchange slaves of both sexes and fox skins … The Tatars took Sudak, whose inhabitants fled

Working translation

Original wording

Les Tatares s'avancèrent jusqu'à la ville de Soudac, qui appartient aux Kiptchacs, et d'où ils tirent leurs grains; elle est située sur la Mer Khazarienne, et les vaisseaux y abordent. On y trouve des étoffes, que les Kiptchacs viennent acheter, donnant en échange des esclaves des deux sexes et des peaux de renard … Les Tatares s'emparèrent de Soudac, dont les habitants prirent la fuite

Editorial context

In a note d'Ohsson translates the Arab historian Ibn al-Athir on the first Mongol raid into the western steppe. The 'Tatars' reached Sudak, a Qipchaq port on the Black ('Khazar') Sea where ships called and where Qipchaqs traded slaves and furs of fox, beaver and squirrel for cloth. They took the town; its people fled to the mountains or by sea to Seljuk Anatolia. The note then describes the Tatars' feigned retreat before crushing the Russians.

Ibn al-Athir, translated by Constantin d'Ohsson (Note VII)

Keep in mind

Ibn al-Athir wrote in Mosul from reports; d'Ohsson's own footnote explains that Crimea was then called Khazaria.

CitiesCrimea (north of Sudak)June 1253

'No abiding city': entering another world

So on the third day after we left Soldaia we came upon the Tartars, and when I went in among them it truly seemed to me that I was entering another world. I will describe their life and customs to you as best I can. Nowhere do they have an abiding city, and of the city to come they know nothing.

Working translation

Original wording

Postquam ergo recessimus de Soldaia tertia die invenimus Tartaros, inter quos cum intravi visum fuit michi recte quod ingrederer quoddam aliud seculum, quorum vitam et morem vobis describo prout possum. Nusquam habent manentem civitatem, sed futuram ignorant.

Editorial context

Three days after leaving the Crimean port of Soldaia (Sudak), Rubruck met the first Tartars and felt he was entering another world. His opening line about them turns a verse from the Letter to the Hebrews ('here we have no abiding city, but we seek one to come'): the Tartars have no abiding city anywhere, and know nothing of the city to come.

William of Rubruck

BuildingsSudak coast, Crimea1840

Terraced Tatar villages with clay roofs

the terrace tops of their houses, where they regularly carry on their work, and which are formed of stout carpentry covered with a thick bed of clay. Nothing can be more picturesque than the appearance, at evening, of all these terraces rising in gradations one above the other.

Editorial context

In the mountain villages of Toklouk, Kooz and Otouz, Xavier found flat-roofed Tatar houses built against the valley sides, so that the roofs formed terraces where people worked and visited each other. The roofs are stout timber covered with a thick bed of clay. At evening whole villages gathered on them.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

The nameSouth coast of Crimea, west of Sudak1784 to 1785

Ruins the Tatars call the 'Shepherds' Fortress'

On the summit of the headland one still sees the remains of former columns and ruined masonry, which ruins are called by the Tatars Choban-kale, or the Shepherds' Fortress, because they serve as a refuge for the herds grazing there.

Working translation

Original wording

Auf dem Gipfel des Vorgebirges sieht man jetzt noch die Trümmer von ehemaligen Säulen und verfallenes Mauerwerk, welche Ruinen von den Tataren Tschoban-kale oder die Hirten-Festung genannt werden, weil sie den dort weidenden Heerden zum Zufluchtsorte dienen.

Editorial context

On a headland of the Crimean south coast, Hablizl saw the remains of old columns and fallen masonry. The Tatars called the ruins Choban-kale, 'the shepherds' fortress', not after whoever built them but because the herds grazing there used them as a shelter. It is a small example of how Tatar place names often describe present use rather than claim ancient origins.

Karl Ludwig Hablizl (German translation, 1789)
