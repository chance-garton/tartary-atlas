Daily lifeGrand Canal, northern China1816

Milk at court, in memory of Tartar origins

Asking about the 'milk-tea' that was to have been served at the cancelled audience, Ellis learns it is simply milk, served to recall the Manchu dynasty's Tartar origins; likewise the emperor carves meat with a knife rather than chopsticks at state meals. Ellis adds that the conquerors profess to value their ancestors' simple ways but have lost their energy.

This milk-tea, it appears, is simply milk; chaya, or tea, being affixed to the names of many beverages besides that made from the tea plant. The milk is given as a record of the Tartar origin of the reigning family. Another instance of the anxiety to preserve these recollections is, that the Emperor, on state occasions, uses a knife to divide his meat, instead of chop-sticks.

Henry Ellis

The Tatars: one of the oldest Turkic tribes

In his chapter on the tribes, Abu'l Ghazi describes the Tatars as a specific tribe, once 70,000 families under one powerful khan, that split into branches and declined. The main branch lived on the frontier of 'Kitay' (northern China) and was subject to its emperor, rebelling often. This is the historical Tatar confederation that Chinggis Khan later crushed, not the later Volga or Crimean Tatars.

The Tribe of the Tatars is one of the most antient and famous of the Turkish Nations; it was heretofore above 70000 Families strong, and had only one Chan who was very potent: But being afterwards divided into several Branches, its Power by degrees began to decline.

Abu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

The Tatar horde revolts, and the Jin are 'Eastern Tartars'

In the story of young Temüjin, Gaubil writes that 'the horde of the Tatars' rebelled against the Jin emperor, keeping the Tatars as one tribe distinct from the Mongols. His footnote explains that the Jin were 'Eastern Tartars' who ruled the steppe north and north-east of Liaodong and much of northern China, and to whom the petty princes of both Tartaries paid tribute.

The horde of the Tatars revolted against the Emperor of the Kin. … The Emperor of the Kin spoken of here is the Emperor of the Eastern Tartars, who were then masters of the part of Tartary lying north and north-east of Liaodong

'The stinking Tartars': what the Chinese called them

In his 'Observation' on d'Herbelot's article Tatar, Visdelou traces the Chinese names: first Tha-tche, then Tha-tha, commonly Tha-tçe, and in contempt Sao-tha-tçe, 'the stinking Tartars'. He says the Chinese added a character meaning raw hide to show how low they ranked these peoples, and that the Jurchen and Mongols, once they had conquered China, dropped the insulting character. The volume's index adds an older name, Kiuen, 'dog'.

The Chinese first called them Thatche, then Tha-tha, and commonly Tha-tçe; finally, in contempt, Sao-tha-tçe, that is, the stinking Tartars. … So the Jurchen and the Mongols, after conquering China, removed this insulting character

Working translation

Original wording

Les Chinois les ont nommé au commencement Thatche, ensuite Tha-tha, & vulgairement Tha-tçe; enfin par mépris Sao-tha-tçe, c'est-à-dire les puants Tartares. … Aussi les Niou-tchin & les Moumgols, après avoir conquis la Chine, ont retranché cette lettre injurieuse

Claude de Visdelou (Observation on d'Herbelot)

The nameNorth China and Jehol1793

Tartars and Chinese: one government, one language, two characters

In a section headed 'Character of the Tartars', Hüttner says mixed marriages, a single government and a common language had made Tartars and Chinese hard to tell apart in their customs. Yet he found the Tartar, as travellers described him, rough, hard and blunt: sturdier in build, clumsier in manners and less clean in his dwellings than the Chinese, but without the deceitful double-dealing he attributes to his Chinese neighbour. On the next page the two mandarins conducting the embassy blamed the stubbornness and pride of the Tartars for trouble getting supplies in Tartary.

Mixed marriages of Tartars and Chinese, one and the same government, one and the same language naturally produce the same customs. But since a people can never wholly deny its own character, here too some traits shine through that clearly distinguish the Tartar from the Chinese. Travellers describe the former as rough, hard and blunt; and so we really found him.

Working translation

Original wording

Vermischte Ehen der Tartarn und Chinesen, einerlei Regierung, einerlei Sprache bringen natürlicher Weise dieselben Sitten hervor. Da aber ein Volk seinen eigenthümlichen Charakter nie gänzlich verläugnen kann, so leuchten auch hier einige Züge hervor, die den Tartarn merklich von dem Chinesen unterscheiden. Reisende beschreiben den erstern als roh, hart und gerade; und so fanden wir ihn wirklich.

Johann Christian Hüttner (1797)

The Tartar raid of 1550 and a city retaken

Portuguese prisoners in China were told that after some years of truce, the Tartars made a great invasion in 1550 and took a very important city. The Chinese besieged it but could not storm it; on the advice of a low-born man they found a way to kill the Tartars and their horses and recovered the city. Cruz does not name the city or the method. The date matches the great Mongol raid on the Beijing region in 1550.

It was affirmed to the Portuguese who were captives … that for some years there had been truces between the Chinese and the Tartars; and in the year fifty the Tartars made a great incursion into China, in which they took from them a very important city … on the advice of a man of low rank they found a means by which they killed them and their horses, and became masters of the city once more.

Strange talesNorthern China and Tartaryreported 1585

Mass weddings by lot 'toward Tartary'

Mendoza passes on a story that in the provinces toward Tartary, and in Tartary itself, governors set an age by which men and women must either enter religion or marry. At the appointed time all who are to marry gather in a designated city, where twelve elders record their names and wealth; if there are more men than women, they draw lots, and the surplus wait until next year. He introduces it with 'they say'.

They say that in the provinces toward Tartary, and in Tartary itself, a certain very strange manner of marrying is customary, which is that the viceroys or governors have set a time for both men and women, so that on reaching a certain age they are obliged either to enter religion or to marry.

Original wording

Juan González de Mendoza (Historia del gran Reyno de la China, Rome 1585)

Tatar Khan's realm by the powerful town of Dsurdsut

When Oghuz Khan forced his neighbours to accept the true faith, a few refugees fled to Tatar Khan, who lived near 'Dsurdsut'. Abu'l Ghazi calls it a very powerful town ruling several smaller ones, near China's frontier. Dsurdsut is most likely the Jurchen, the people of Manchuria, so here the early Tatars are placed beside a town-dwelling kingdom.

a very few who retir'd into the Dominions of Tatar Chan, who resided in the Neighbourhood of Dsurdsut. The Town of Dsurdsut is very powerful, having several small Towns under its Jurisdiction: 'Tis situate towards the Frontiers of Kitay, being called Zinu in the Indian and Persian Language.

Abu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

Before they ruled, the Turks were ironsmiths

In his addenda Schott explains a note from the Chinese encyclopedias Hoan-jü-ki and Ma Tuan-lin: the chief Tu-lun of the S'en-sen took the title K'o-han (khagan, 'khan') in the time of T'ai-wu-ti (reigned 424 to 451), emperor of the Wei dynasty under which the 'Tatar people Topa' ruled northern China, and the Tu-kiu later followed his example. He adds that the S'en-sen were overthrown by the Tu-kiu, who had first served them as ironsmiths. Schott's point is that the title and these peoples are documented in Chinese records centuries before Chinggis Khan.

Heu-Uei (or Juan-Uei) was the Chinese name under which the Tatar people Topa (Tö-pö) ruled the northern China it had conquered. … The S'en-sen, who according to Chinese history were a tribe of the Hiung-nu, were for a time powerful in eastern Turkistan, and fell to the Tu-kiu, who had at first done forced labour for them (as ironsmiths).

Original wording

Heu-Uei (oder Juan-Uei) war der chinesische Name, unter welchem das tatarische Volk Topa (Tö-pö) über das von ihm eroberte nördliche China herrschte. … Die S'en-sen, von denen die chinesische Geschichte sagt, dass sie ein Stamm der Hiung-nu gewesen, waren eine Zeitlang im östlichen Turkistan mächtig, und unterlagen den Tu-kiu, die ihnen anfänglich Frohndienste (als Eisenschmiede) geleistet hatten.

Horse fairs on the Great Wall

After Altan Khan made peace in 1571, the Ming set rules for border markets: the Mongols would bring gold, silver, cattle, horses, hides and horsetail, and Chinese merchants satin, silk, cloth and iron cooking pots. Each market ran for a month, with three hundred Mongols camped outside the wall and five hundred Chinese troops guarding the marketplace. Iron pots were a key trade good because the Mongols could not easily make them.

One: establish border markets, on the model of the northern tribes' three tributes at the start of Hongzhi. The fan (Mongols) bring gold, silver, cattle, horses, hides, horse tails and such; the merchants bring satin, silk, cloth and iron pots and cauldrons. On market days three hundred of those who come are to camp outside the wall, and five hundred of our soldiers are to be stationed at the marketplace; the market lasts a full month.
