CitiesIli (Yining area, Xinjiang)1760s-1770s

No towns on the Ili until the emperor built one for exiles

The next note says the banks of the Ili had long been the favourite home of the Eleuth kings, but 'there were no towns': they camped in tents, moving from place to place. After the conquest the emperor had a town built there that grew daily, and it had become the place of exile for Chinese and Tartars convicted of crimes.

The banks of the Ili river had long been the favourite residence of the Eleuth kings. There were no towns. They camped there in tents, now in one place, now in another. The Emperor has had a town built there which grows more populous every day. It is there that Chinese and Tartars who have deserved the penalty of exile are now sent.

BuildingsIli valley, XinjiangJanuary 1723

A New Year banquet in a felt tent 14 metres wide

At the Mongol New Year (Tsagan Sar, 'White Month') about 300 nobles feasted in one enormous round felt kibitka, some 20 arshins (about 14 metres) across. The lattice walls were hung with blue cloth and a fire burned on an iron hearth in the middle. Tea, meat and vodka were served and musicians played fiddles, long flutes and a three-stringed instrument.

The kibitka was of felt, very large and round, about 20 arshins across; inside, the lattice walls (on which the roof is fixed) were hung with blue cloth; in the middle was a hearth made of iron, on which wood was burning.

A Swedish prisoner casts cannon for the Dzungar ruler

A Russian soldier freed from Dzungar captivity told Unkovsky that a foreign sub-lieutenant, Renat, captured with the Russians in 1716, had cast seven bronze cannon and three mortars for the ruler, and that other captive officers had taught the Dzungars to make cloth and needles; one was said to be making velvet in Yarkand. It shows a steppe state importing skills through its prisoners.

he heard that the foreigner, sub-lieutenant of artillery Renat, who was taken prisoner with them at Koryakov, cast for the Kontaisha seven bronze cannon and three mortars. And at the same Kontaisha's Urga, Lieutenant Debesh and other officers taught them to make cloth and needles. And now it is said that this Debesh is making velvet in Erken (Yarkand).

Daily lifeIli valley, XinjiangJanuary 1723

Horned masked dancers and stones thrown at China

Before the New Year the lamas performed a masked dance (the Buddhist tsam) before a great tent. Unkovsky describes masked dancers wearing caps with horns, one like a cow's, one like a wild goat's, cymbals, drums and long brass trumpets, and an offering blown up in a fire. At the end the whole crowd, the ruler among them, marched east and hurled stones, sticks and earth toward the east with a great shout, 'as if for victory over the Chinese', their enemy at the time.

Two men came out with covered faces, with two horns each on their caps; one had cow's horns, the other horns like a wild goat's … and all the people, seizing whatever came to hand (a stone or a stick or earth), threw it toward the east with a great shout, as if for victory over the Chinese; and the Kontaisha himself walked with them.

Original wording

Вышли два человѣка, закрытыя лица, на шапкахъ по два рога было; у одного коровей, у другого яко у дикой козы … и весь народъ, ухватя что попало въ руки (камень или палку или земли), съ великимъ крикомъ бросали на востокъ, будто бы въ побѣду на Китайцевъ; притомъ и самъ Контайша шелъ.

BuildingsTalki pass (Sayram Lake to the Ili valley), XinjiangBuilt 1219; crossed October 1221

Forty-eight bridges built by Chagatai

Below the mountain lake that Changchun named the Lake of Heaven (Sayram Lake), the road ran down a gorge of birch and pine, and the diarist credits Chinggis's second son Chagatai with cutting it through the rocks and building forty-eight timber bridges wide enough for two carts abreast. Earlier the cart road over the Altai had likewise been 'planned and constructed' by the third son, Ogedei, where the escort hauled the carts up with ropes and dragged the wheels going down (p.62). Here the Mongols appear as builders of roads and wooden bridges for their armies.

The second prince, who was with the emperor at the time he went to the west (in 1219), first made a way through these mountains, cut through the rocks, and built forty-eight bridges with the wood cut on the mountains. The bridges are so wide that two carts can pass side by side.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Mongol army under Chagatai, Chinggis Khan's second son

Keep in mind

'(in 1219)' is Bretschneider's insertion. The diarist saw the bridges but was told who built them, and he does not say whose labour the prince used.

CitiesAlmaliq (near Huocheng, Xinjiang)1259

Almaliq: running water, fruit, Uighurs and Chinese settlers

Beyond the Iron Pass, guarded by Chinese soldiers, Chang De reached Almaliq, a Mongol-ruled city of the Chagatai realm. Its streets were crossed by running water and its melons, grapes and pomegranates were excellent. Muslim Turks lived mixed with Chinese and had picked up Chinese ways, and a town further south was full of settlers from Shanxi.

Leaving the pass he reached the city of Alimali (Almaliq). Its streets and markets are all crossed by running water. There are all kinds of fruit; the melons, grapes and pomegranates are the best. The Huihe (Muslim Turks) live mixed with the Han Chinese, and their customs have gradually been coloured until they are rather like China's. Further south is the city of Chimur, whose inhabitants are mostly people from Bing and Fen (Shanxi).

BuildingsDzungaria (Ili valley)1750s

The Dzungars live in tents, visiting their villages two months a year

In a note on the emperor's text, Amiot explains that the Eleuths camp in tents and spend only two or three months a year in their hamlets, villages and 'kinds of towns', and only the most important among them bother to go there at all. Every head of a family plants a standard before his tent, larger or smaller by rank.

The Eleuths camp under tents; they are in their hamlets, villages and sorts of towns only about two or three months of the year, and even then only the most important of them deign to withdraw there. Each head of a family raises a standard in front of his tent

CitiesIli valley, Xinjiang (Dzungar headquarters)20 November 1722

A capital of tents laid out like streets

Riding to his first audience, Unkovsky entered the ruler's urga, the moving headquarters camp. He describes rings of Kalmyk and Bukharan tent-camps (kuri) set out 'like streets', which he was led around for about a verst, and a bazaar in front of the ruler's own tent. This is what the capital of a powerful steppe state looked like: a planned city of portable dwellings.

When we rode into the first kuri (tent-rings), which are set out like streets, Kalmyk and Bukharan, they led us among them for about a verst, not straight but around the Urga; and when we came to the front of the Kontaisha's Urga, where the bazaar is held, about a hundred sazhens short of it, Kashka said we must get off our horses here.

Original wording

Какъ въѣхали въ первые кури, которые яко улицами поставлены, калмыцкіе и бухарскіе, и вели насъ между оными около версты не прямо, но вкругъ Урги, и какъ пріѣхали съ лица контайшиной Урги, гдѣ базаръ бываетъ, около ста сажень не доѣхавъ, сказалъ Кашка, что тутъ надлежитъ съ лошадей слезть.

BuildingsKhorgos, Ili valley (Huocheng, Xinjiang)told on 9 November 1722; events c. 1388 by the Bukharans' reckoning

A Muslim saint-khan's tomb, and the wrestling match that converted his people

The Tobolsk Bukharans in Unkovsky's party told him of the tomb of 'Temer khan', a ruler of the Ili who, they said, had worshipped idols until a Muslim merchant threw his strongest wrestler three times. The khan then converted, his people were circumcised and threw the tassels from their caps into the Borotala river, damming it. Muslims built chambers over his grave and still brought offerings. Unkovsky wanted to see it but was not allowed. The tomb is generally identified with the mausoleum of the Chagatai khan Tughluq Timur near Khorgos.

For that reason Temer khan accepted the Muhammadan law, and all the people of his realm were circumcised and threw the tassels from their caps into the river Borotala, and supposedly dammed the river with them. And for this the Muhammadans revere Temer khan as a saint, and chambers have been built over his tomb, and to this day the Muhammadans bring offerings to his tomb, and there are guards at that tomb.

Working translation

Original wording

Того ради Темерханъ принялъ Махометской законъ, и всѣ его владѣнія люди обрѣзались и съ шапокъ залы (или кисти) въ рѣку Борталъ побросали, и будто оными рѣку запрудили. И для вышеозначенного махометане оного Темерхана, святымъ почитаютъ, а надъ гробомъ его полаты построены и приносятъ доднесь махометане приносы на гробъ его, и сторожи при томъ гробѣ имѣются.

Daily lifeVolga to Ili1771

Why the Torghuts left the Volga: conscription, hostages and religion

The emperor gives the Torghuts' reasons for leaving Russia: the Russians kept demanding soldiers, had taken Ubashi's son as a hostage, and had a different religion and no regard for the Lamas' faith the Torghuts followed. On the same page they set out with wives, children and baggage, crossed the Kazakh country and the deserts by Lake Balkhash, and after eight months and more than ten thousand li reached the Ili frontier. The volume's preface puts their number at 500,000.

The Russians never ceasing to demand soldiers from him … and lately having taken his own son from him as a hostage, being moreover of a different religion from his and making no account of the religion of the Lamas which the Torghuts profess, Ubashi and his people at last resolved to shake off a yoke

Original wording

CitiesAlmaliq1339 to 1340

From Ozbeg's horde to a new church at Almaliq

The legates wintered with Ozbeg, khan of the Golden Horde, who fed, clothed and rewarded them and sent them on with his own horses to Almaliq, a city of the Chagatai khanate in the Ili valley (near modern Huocheng, Xinjiang). There, only a year after a bishop and six friars had been killed, they bought a plot, built a church and preached openly. The Tartar khans appear as hosts who moved envoys across their realms at their own cost.

From there we came to the first emperor of the Tartars, Ozbeg, and presented letters, cloth, a warhorse, a 'cytiaca' (the editor guesses a strong liquor) and the Pope's gifts; and after the winter, well fed, clothed and magnificently rewarded, we travelled with his horses and at his expense to Almaliq of the middle empire, where we built a church, bought a plot of ground, made wells (or fonts), sang masses and baptised many, preaching freely and in public

The khan's felt house inside a fence of reeds and willow

Unkovsky had to lift the felt door flap himself. The detail shows the materials of a steppe palace: felt, reed, willow and a little timber.

When we came within about 6 sazhens of the enclosure (kosha), which is woven of reed and willow around the Kontaisha's felt house, they opened the doors, which are joined from six planks.

Daily lifeDzungar khanate (Ili valley and Yarkand)1720s

Nomads turning farmers on the khan's orders

Unkovsky notes that thirty years earlier the Kalmyks had little grain because they did not know how to plough, but now fields were multiplying: not only their subject Bukharans (settled Turkestani farmers) but many Kalmyks had taken up farming by the ruler's order. He lists wheat, millet, barley and rice, and melons, apricots, grapes and pomegranates from Yarkand and the other towns under Dzungar rule.

Some thirty years before this they had little grain, because they did not know how to plough. Now their fields multiply by the hour, and not only the subject Bukharans sow grain, but many Kalmyks are taking up ploughing, because there is an order from the Kontaisha about it.

Kalmyk chiefs who wear deer antlers

From Bukhara merchants Burnes heard that Kalmyk Tartars settled around Ili and Yarkand marked their chiefs and nobles by fixing deer's horns on their skull-caps, the size of the antlers showing rank. He adds a crude rumour about their women, and says the Chinese used Kalmyks to guard the frontier.

There are Calmuk Tartars settled around Eela and Yarkund, who have a singular custom to distinguish their chiefs and grandees, by fixing deer's horns on their skull-caps.

Alexander Burnes (1834)

Strange talesAlmaliq1768

An editor moves Almaliq and Sarai to Japan

Faced with unfamiliar names, the Prague editor Gelasius Dobner trusted a Franciscan martyrology that placed the martyrs 'in Japan' and concluded that Almaliq and Sarai were Japanese islands, Sarai being 'Sayrock Xymo' (probably Kyushu, which Jesuit writers called 'Saicoku' or 'Ximo'). In fact Almaliq lay in the Ili valley of Central Asia and Sarai on the lower Volga. By the eighteenth century the geography of the medieval Tartar empires had become hazy even for scholars.

And since the Martyrology of Saint Francis says they suffered in Japan, Almaliq was most probably one of the islands of Japan, just as Sarai, named just above, is most probably the present-day island of Sayrock Xymo, one of the three chief islands of Japan in the eastern Ocean.

Working translation

Original wording

Et cum Martyrologium S. Francisci Seraphici dicat eos passos in Japonia, verosimillime Armalec una ex insulis fuit Japonie, quemadmodum mox supra nominata Saray verosimillime est hodierna insula Sayrock Xymo una inter tres precipuas Japonie insulas in Oceano orientali.

Gelasius Dobner, editor (footnote, 1768)

A martyr's prophecy against the Tartars of Sarai

Marignolli names the Franciscans killed at Almaliq the year before he arrived and credits one of them, Pascal the Spaniard, with visions and prophecies: that the Tartars of Sarai on the Volga would be destroyed by a flood, that Almaliq would perish, and that the ruler would be killed three days after the martyrdom. He presents the dead friars as holy martyrs 'shining with miracles'.

Brother Pascal the Spaniard, who was a prophet, saw heaven opened and foretold martyrdom for himself and his companions, and that the Tartars of Sarai would be destroyed by a flood, that Almaliq would perish because of their martyrdom, and that that emperor would be killed on the third day after their martyrdom

Daily lifeIli valley, Xinjiang1723

Wrestlers in rolled-up trousers

Invited to watch wrestling, Unkovsky sat near the ruler under awnings on tall poles. He describes bouts between the ruler's side and his son Galdan Tseren's side, with bets and judges who wrote down the results. The wrestlers came out bare but for trousers rolled above the knee, raised their arms, glared to look fearsome, and gripped each other by the trousers. A bout only counted when a man was thrown flat on both shoulders.

And so from both sides the wrestlers come out naked, only in trousers rolled up well above the knee. And when they are about four sazhens from each other they raise their arms, and, rolling their eyes (to make themselves look fearsome), slowly close in; and when they meet they bend forward and seize each other by the trousers

Almaliq: a Muslim ruler beside a Mongol governor

At Almaliq in the Ili valley the local Muslim ruler and the Mongol governor (darughachi, Bretschneider explains) rode out together to greet the sage, a glimpse of how the Mongols ruled a town through its old prince. The city was named for its apples (alma in Turkic). The travellers bought cloth woven from 'vegetable wool', cotton, then little known in China, saw people carrying water in jars on their heads, and heard the locals praise the Chinese as 'very able men' when they saw a Chinese well-bucket (pp.70-71).

The ruler of the realm of P'u-su-man came out of the city, together with the Mongol ta-lu-hua-chi, to meet the master. We stopped at a fruit orchard west (of the city). The people here call a fruit a-li-ma; and as the place is famed for its fruits, the city received the above name.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Daily lifeDzungar khanate (Ili valley)1722 to 1724

Verbal courts and savage punishments

In his closing description of the Dzungar state Unkovsky says the ruler took no great decision without his council of zaisangs, and that the court (zargo) of ten or more zaisangs judged cases orally, not in writing. Capital sentences were carried out by flogging to death or by tying limbs to horses' tails, though he adds that such executions were rare.

They decide cases not in writing but orally. If they condemn someone to death for serious matters, some they kneel down and flog to death with whips; others they tie by the hands and feet to horses' tails, and the horses, running, tear off those people's arms and legs. However, such executions happen rarely among them.

Kalmak Tatars moved to Ili as horse breeders

From merchants' reports Moorcroft learned that many 'Kalmak Tatars' had once lived around Khotan but had been deported by the Chinese to the Ili valley, where some 200,000 families bred cattle, camels and horses and paid one head in a hundred as tax. They brought ten to twenty thousand geldings a year to Ili for sale.

A considerable portion of the population of Khoten consisted formerly of Kalmak Tatars, but it is said that when the Chinese subjugated the province, they deported the Kalmaks to the cities which collectively constitute the modern city of Ila, on the river of the same name, and to the adjacent districts.

William Moorcroft (edited by H. H. Wilson, 1841)

CitiesIli (Yining)about 1813

Ili: the old Kalmyk capital becomes a Chinese garrison

In the route copied from a Kashgar man's journal, the note on Ili says the old city by the river, once the Kalmaks' capital, is deserted, and that the name now covers two towns: Gulja, mostly Muslim, and Kura, the Chinese garrison and seat of the governor-general. It also mentions the grave of Tughluk Timur, the first Chaghatai khan to become Muslim, and a burning stone (coal) found nearby.

Ila is properly the name of the river on the borders of which stood the city, thence so called, and which is now deserted. There is the grave of Tughluk Timor, the first of the Chaghatai Sultans who adopted Mohammedanism.

Mir Izzet Ullah, from Mullah Nazr of Kashgar's route notes (translated by H. H. Wilson)
