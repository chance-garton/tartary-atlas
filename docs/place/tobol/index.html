BuildingsKazakh steppe between the Ui and Tobol1770

A brick 'Tatar prayer-house' whose builders were forgotten

Pallas rode out into the steppe beyond the Orenburg line to see a building the Russians called the Tatar Koshena. It was a square brick shrine with a pointed Gothic portal and a twelve-sided funnel dome, standing in a Kazakh cemetery. He thought it was not very old, yet neither the Kazakhs nor the Bashkirs remembered who had built it. On a following page he notes it is all of red brick, much like ordinary European brick.

It is still in perfectly good condition and, at least to judge by the woodwork inside, seems to be of no extraordinary antiquity. Nevertheless the memory of its building has been lost among the Kirgis as well as the Bashkirs.

BuildingsBetween the Tobol and Ishim rivers, south of Tobolsk1720

Stone statues and ruined cities he never saw

In a footnote Strahlenberg records that Russian regiments going up the Irtysh in 1720 found antiquities and 'Temples of Idols', and that Tobolsk Tatars and Russians told him of many stone figures of men and beasts and the ruins of several cities in the little-visited steppe between the Tobol and Ishim. He links them to obelisks said to have been set up by Tamerlane and to the seat of Oghuz Khan, and regrets he could never go to see them.

…and I have also been told, by the Tobolskian Tartars and Russians, that, from this River, farther towards the West, South, and South-west from the City of Tobolsky, between the Sources of the Rivers Toboll and Ischim, whither very few People frequented, there were to be found great Numbers of Images, cut in Stone, of Men and Beasts; and that the Ruins of several Cities were discernible in those Desarts.

Who Tobolsk Tatars and Russians (informants)

Heard itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg, author's footnote (English translation, 1738)

Keep in mind. Pure hearsay: he says himself he never had the chance to visit. The Tamerlane and Oghuz Khan links come from his reading.

The nameWestern Siberia (Tobol, Irtysh, Ob)1636 (Esipov's description)

Tatars keep the law of Muhammad; the Kalmyks, 'I do not know'

Esipov opens with the peoples living along the Tobol, Irtysh and Ob: Tatars, Kalmyks, Mongols, the 'Piebald Horde' (Selkups), Ostyaks and Samoyeds. The Tatars, he says, keep Muhammad's law; as for the Kalmyks, he honestly does not know their faith, since he found nothing written and could not find out. The Ostyaks and Samoyeds, he adds, have no law and worship idols.

Along these rivers live many peoples: Tatars, Kalmyks, Mongols, the Piebald Horde, Ostyaks, Samoyeds and other peoples. The Tatars keep the law of Muhammad; which law or tradition of their fathers the Kalmyks keep, I do not know, since I found nothing written about it and could not find out.

Daily lifeTobol river, between Tobolsk and Tyumen1714

Tatar boat-haulers flogged by Russian soldiers

Going up the Tobol river from Tobolsk to Tyumen, the embassy's boats were towed the whole way by Tatars. In Tulishen's own Chinese words, there was no towpath, the men waded through mud until their legs bled, and the Russian soldiers kept whipping them until the envoy, unable to watch, told them to stop. It is one of the few moments where the author shows personal feeling.

All along the way the Ta-ta-la (Tatar) people hauled the towropes. The banks were thick with trees and there was no towpath, so they all walked in mud and water; the skin of their legs and feet was torn and blood ran down, yet the Russian soldiers still flogged them on. I could not bear to watch, and only when I rebuked them did they stop.

Daily lifeKazakh steppe between the Ui and Tobol1770

Horsehair and bullets left at a holy place

At the same brick shrine birch poles were hung with tufts of horsehair and rags, and the ledges held pebbles, lead bullets, an arrow and gunpowder. Pallas explains that the Kazakhs believe leaving hair from a horse's tail at a holy place helps the horse, and that leaving some small thing is a general Tatar custom. The Tatars of his own escort added offerings after praying.

For the Kirgis believe that it is good for their horse if some hair from its tail is left at so holy a place; and in general it is a Tatar custom, at such prayer-houses or other holy places they visit out of devotion, to leave behind something they carry with them.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Kirgisen nehmlich glauben, daß es ihrem Pferde zuträglich ist, wenn etwas Haar von deßen Schweif an einem so heiligen Ort zurück gelassen wird, und überhaupt ist es eine tatarische Gewohnheit bey solchen Bethäusern, oder andern heiligen Plätzen, die sie der Andacht wegen besuchen, irgend etwas, so sie bey sich führen, zurück zu lassen.

Who Kazakhs (Kirgis) and Tatars of Pallas's escort

The nameWestern Siberia (Tura, Tobol, Irtysh, Ob)c. 1580 (narrated c. 1700)

Tatars by the rivers, reindeer people to the north, camel herders on the steppe

What Yermak is said to have heard about Siberia before setting out: a map in words that sorts the peoples. The Tatars live along the Tura, Tobol and Irtysh and travel by boat and horse; the Ostyaks and Samoyeds of the Ob ride reindeer and dogs and live on fish; Kalmyks, Mongols and the Kazakh horde ride camels on the steppe. 'Tatar' here is one people among many, not a label for all Siberians.

and along the Tura and the Tobol live Tatars, who travel in boats and on horses. The Tobol falls into the Irtysh; and on the Irtysh near its mouth is a tsardom and many Tatars; the Irtysh falls into the Ob, and the Ob falls into the sea by two mouths, and along it live Ostyaks and Samoyeds, who ride reindeer and dogs and feed on fish; and on the steppe the Kalmyks and Mongols and the Kazakh horde, who ride camels
