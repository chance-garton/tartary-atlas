CitiesBaghdadc. 1290

Baghdad forty years on: mostly ruined, still crowded, ruled by Tartars

In that city, although it is for the most part destroyed, there is nevertheless a very great multitude of people. For besides Christians and Jews, who are many thousands there, there are more than two hundred thousand Saracens, as is plausibly believed; yet all are under the lordship of the Tartars.

Working translation

Original wording

In ipsa quidem ciuitate, licet sit pro maiori parte destructa, est tamen maxima multitudo populi in ea. Nam preter Christianos et Iudeos, qui sunt ibi multa milia, sunt ibi vltra ducenta milia Sarracenorum, vt probabiliter creditur; omnes tamen sunt sub dominio Tartarorum.

Editorial context

Riccoldo lived in Baghdad and describes it after the Mongol conquest: the city is 'for the most part destroyed' yet still full of people, with many thousands of Christians and Jews and over two hundred thousand Muslims, all under Tartar lordship. He also found its Muslim colleges and Sufi houses thriving and admired their scholars.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Keep in mind

Population figures are his estimate ('as is plausibly believed').

CitiesBaghdad1401

Baghdad razed, except mosques, colleges and hospitals

Afterwards Timur gave orders that there shou'd not remain one single house in the city unraz'd ; but that the mosques, colleges and hospitals shou'd be spar'd. Accordingly they ruin'd the markets, caravanseras, hermitages, cells, monasterys, palaces, and other edifices.

Editorial context

After Baghdad fell by storm, each Tartar soldier was ordered to bring a head, and towers of heads were built as a warning. Timur then ordered every house razed but the mosques, colleges and hospitals spared. A footnote from the translator adds Ibn Arabshah's figures: 90,000 killed and 120 towers of heads.

Sharaf al-Din Ali Yazdi (English of 1723, via Pétis de la Croix)
See the original page (p. 215)

About Timur's Tartar soldiers

Keep in mind

The official history presents the massacre as justified; the casualty figures in the footnote come from Ibn Arabshah, a hostile writer.

CitiesBaghdad1258

Baghdad taken, the caliph and his treasure

The caliph was brought alive before Hulegu, and so much wealth was found in the city of Baghdad that it was a great marvel to behold. … And the caliph answered that he thought old women alone were enough to defend the land.

Working translation

Original wording

Le calif fu amenez vif devant Haloon, e tantes richesses furent trovées en la cité de Baldac que ce fut grant merveille à regarder. … E le calif respondi que il quidoit que veisles fammes soulement estoient soufisables à defendre la terre.

Editorial context

Hayton's account of the Mongol sack of Baghdad: Hulegu took the city by force, put men and women to the sword, and found such riches that it was a marvel to see. Asked why he had not spent his treasure on an army, the caliph answered that he thought old women alone were enough to defend his land. Hulegu then shut him in a room with his treasure to eat it, and 'never again was there a caliph in Baghdad'.

Hayton of Corycus, La Flor des estoires de la terre d'Orient (French text, 1307), ed. Ch. Kohler (1906)
See the original page (p. 169)

About Hulegu's Tartars; the Abbasid caliph

Keep in mind

Hayton says his knowledge of Hulegu's reign came from his uncle King Het'um; the starving-among-treasure ending is a widely told story, not a verified fact.

CitiesBaghdad1258 (text: dingsi year)

The fall of Baghdad

In the dingsi year they took the country of Baoda (Baghdad), two thousand li from north to south. Its ruler was called the Helifa (caliph). The city has an east city and a west city, with a great river between. The west city has no ramparts; the east city is fortified with brick, painted very splendidly on top. When the imperial army reached the city, in a single battle it broke picked troops more than 400,000 strong; the west city fell and all its people were slaughtered.

Working translation

Original wording

丁巳歳取報逹國南北二千里其主曰合里法其城有東西城中有大河西城無壁壘東城固之以甓繪其上甚盛王師至城不一交戰破勝兵四十餘萬西城䧟皆尽屠其民

Editorial context

Chang De's report describes Baghdad as two cities, east and west, divided by a great river, the western one without walls and the eastern one fortified with painted brick. The Mongol army broke the caliph's troops in one battle and slaughtered the people of the western city; the eastern city fell after a six-day siege (the page goes on). It is one of the very few Chinese accounts of the sack.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 094-7a)

About Mongols of Hulegu; the Abbasid caliph

Keep in mind

The dingsi year is 1257; Baghdad fell in February 1258. This text reads 城不 where the Siku copy has 城下 ('below the city'); the translation follows the Siku reading. The troop figure is an exaggeration.

Strange talesBaghdad, IraqJanuary 1258

Tatars who take the dam of Gog and Magog for a cobweb

The army of the Tatars, who come on like the floods of the sea, flies over the mountain tops like flocks of eagles; since they regard the dam of Gog and Magog as a cobweb, what else should rise from their hooves on the heights of Hamrin but dust, what else should come out of the storm in which they ride but fire and plunder

Working translation

Original wording

Das Heer der Tataren, die wie die Meeresfluthen einherfahren, überfliegt der Berge Gipfel wie der Adler Schaaren; da sie den Damm von Gog und Magog für Spinngeweb' ansehen, was soll auf des Hamrin's Höhen aus ihren Hufen wohl anders erstehen, als Staub, was soll aus dem Sturme, in dem sie daherfahren, wohl anders aufgehen, als Feuer und Raub

Editorial context

As Hulagu closed on Baghdad, the last Abbasid caliph, told that the Mongol vanguard had crossed the Hamrin hills, asked how that could be. The answer, in Wassaf's ornate rhymed prose (rendered by Hammer in rhyme), paints the Tatars as a sea flood flying over peaks like eagles, for whom the legendary dam of Gog and Magog, Alexander's barrier against the peoples of the north, is no more than a cobweb. Hammer then describes how the Mongols ringed Baghdad with a hastily raised earthen rampart.

Wassaf (Persian court historian, early 14th century), translated by Hammer-Purgstall (1842)
See the original page (p. 149)

About Hulagu's Mongol army ('the Tatars'); the caliph al-Musta'sim

Keep in mind

Literary rhetoric written decades later, not a record of what was said to the caliph.

CitiesBaghdad1258

The caliph of Baghdad made to eat his gold

When he fell silent they ordered gold to be melted and poured into his mouth, and said to him: 'You thirsted for gold, drink gold!' And they killed his people for forty days on end.

Working translation

Original wording

Quo tacente mandauerunt, quod liquaretur aurum et colaretur in os eius, et dixerunt ei: Aurum sitiisti, aurum bibe! Populum autem eius per quadraginta dies continuos occiderunt.

Editorial context

Riccoldo's version of the fall of Baghdad, told a generation later by a man living in the city. The Tartars found the caliph in a marvellous palace heaped with treasure, starved him for three days, set gold, silver and jewels before him, and when he said he had only kept what his ancestors gathered, poured molten gold into his mouth. They then killed the people for forty days.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)
See the original page

About Tartars under Hulegu; the Abbasid caliph

Keep in mind

The molten-gold death is a legend; Persian sources say the caliph was rolled in a carpet and trampled, and Hayton (W229) has him starved among his treasure.
