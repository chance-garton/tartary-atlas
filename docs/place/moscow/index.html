Moscow shrunk after the Tartar fire of 1571

Possevino, who saw Moscow himself, says it had been larger and more crowded under the same tsar until the Tartars burned it 'in the seventieth year of this century', killing most of the inhabitants. Traces of the old, larger circuit were still visible, and the city now filled barely five miles round. He means the Crimean Tatar sack of Moscow in 1571.

And Moscow was indeed grander and more populous under this same prince, but in the seventieth year of the present century it was burned down by the Tartars, most of its inhabitants consumed by the fire, and everything was reduced to narrower bounds. Traces of a larger circuit remain, so that what was perhaps eight or nine miles round now scarcely fills five thousand paces.

CitiesMoscowAugust 1382

Moscow boasts of its stone walls and iron gates

When Tokhtamysh marched on Moscow, the townspeople burned their own suburbs so no timber or fence was left for the attackers. Some broke into the boyars' cellars, got drunk on mead and jeered from the walls that the Tatars could not stay long before 'a stone city with iron gates'. The Kremlin walls of white stone had been built in 1367. Four days later the Tatars took the city by a false peace.

And they carried the lords' mead out of the cellars and drank themselves drunk, and reeling about drunk they said: 'We shall not fear the Tatar invasion, for we have a strong stone city and iron gates; and the Tatars will not bear to stand long under our city

CitiesMoscow1571

The Crimean Tatars burn Moscow, 1571

Writing to Lord Burghley from Kholmogory in August 1571, Jenkinson lists Russia's woes: famine, the Tsar's executions, plague, and then the Crimean Tatars, who in late May routed the Tsar's army and set Moscow on fire. He reports that not one house was left standing and that more than 300,000 were said to have been burned or carried off as captives. The figures are hearsay, but the event is real.

a valyante natyon of Tartars, in the latter ende of May laste, inuadid this realme, gaue the Prynce an ouerthrowe in the feildes, cawsed him to retyre, burnte and consumed all the countrye before theim, and came to the cyttye of Musko, sett fyer one the same, not leauinge one house standinge

Anthony Jenkinson, letter to Lord Burghley, 8 August 1571 (ed. E. Delmar Morgan and C. H. Coote, 1886)

About Crimean Tatars under Khan Devlet Giray

Keep in mind

Jenkinson arrived after the fire and repeats reported numbers; he calls it 'a juste punyshment of God for such a wycked natyon' (the Russians).

CitiesMoscow1571 (Perry writes 1671)

When the Crimean Tartars burned Moscow

Perry recalls that the Crimean Tartars once penetrated to the heart of Russia and burned Moscow, and that the tsars had been forced to pay tribute and even to agree to hold the khan's stirrup if they met. Perry's date is wrong by a century: Khan Devlet Giray burned Moscow in 1571. The tribute to the Crimean khans was indeed paid until the 1680s to 1700.

The said Tartars were formerly victorious against the Russes. They have often penetrated into the Heart of Russia, and in Anno 1671, they burnt the City of Mosco, and by the Articles of Peace … the Russes were obliged to submit to very scandalous Terms; not only to be tributary to the said Tartars, but even … that the Czar's of Russia should hold the Stirrup to the Great Cham,

Captain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

Perry's year 1671 is a mistake for 1571. The stirrup clause is what he was told.

Daily lifeMoscow; Crimean khanate1588

Two horses each: one to ride, one to eat

Tartar soldiers, says Fletcher, bring two horses, one to ride and one to eat, and horseflesh is their main food. He adds something he saw himself: when the Crimean prince Kiriach Murza came to Moscow with 300 Tartars, he was welcomed with two fat horses, ready skinned, for supper.

every man with his two horse at the least, the one to ride on, the other to kill, when it commeth to his turne to have his horse eate. For their chiefe vittaile is horse flesh, which they eate without bread or any other thing with it.

Giles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

Daily lifeMoscowAugust 1382

Tatar archers who never miss

The account of the 1382 siege describes Tatar archery: their arrows fell on the walls like rain, some shot standing, others ran back and forth, others galloped and shot to both sides, forward and back, 'without a miss'. The defenders answered with crossbows, stone-throwers, boiling water and even the first cannon mentioned in Moscow.

Their assault on the city was very fierce; some of them shot standing, and others ran swiftly here and there, so well were they trained, and shot without a miss; and others, galloping very fast on horses, shot swiftly to both hands, and again forward and backward, without a miss.

CitiesMoscowMay 1571

Horsey's eyewitness of the great fire

Horsey describes the same disaster: the khan's men fired a church, a storm spread the flames, and the timber city burned in six hours. People drowned in the river with their valuables, and for a year afterwards survivors dredged for jewels. The khan watched from the Simonov monastery.

The enyme fiers St. Johns church high steple : at which instant happened a wounderfull stormye wynd, through which all the churches, howses and palaces, within the cittie and suberbs 30 miells compas, built most of firr and oak tymber, was sett one fier and burnt within six howers space

Sir Jerome Horsey (memoir; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

About Crimean Tartars under Devlet Giray

Keep in mind

Horsey arrived in Russia in 1573, two years after the fire, so despite the vivid detail this is what he was told.

The Tsar feeding the khan's horse from his cap

How Witsen gathered his Tartary

Witsen answers that the map grew out of a visit almost 28 years earlier to the country of the Tsar, where he talked with local people and 'Tartars of all sorts'. Since then he had kept up letters with Moscow, Astrakhan, Georgia, Isfahan, Poland, Constantinople and Peking, and collected journals and drawings made to his order. He then admits his biggest doubts are about the sea coasts, which he drew in pale colour to show the uncertainty.

I not only Conversed with the Inhabitants of those Countreys, but with Tartars of all sorts … I have maintain'd a constant Correspondence in Mosco, Astracan, Georgia, Ispahan, Polonia, and Constantinople, I have had Letters every year from Peking the chief City of China, I have gathered Volumes of Journals and Registers

Nicolaas Witsen (letter printed in Philosophical Transactions, 1686)

About 'Tartars of all sorts' met in Muscovy

The Tartar embassy house inside the Kremlin

Even the mighty Ivan III, Herberstein says, had to go out and stand before seated Tartar envoys. The Tartars kept a house inside the Moscow citadel. His Byzantine wife Sophia, claiming a dream, persuaded the Tartar queen to give the plot for a church, and the Tartars were never given a house in the fortress again.

There was within the citadel of Moscow a house in which the Tartars lodged for the purpose of learning what was going on at Moscow, and as this also gave great offence to his wife, she sent messengers with liberal presents to the queen of the Tartars, begging her to give up that house to her

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

Whirlwinds of fire: three hours that ended a city

A second account, printed by the chronicler John Stow and apparently drawn from a letter of the English survivor Thomas Glover, describes the fire as a storm of 'whirl wynds' that died down after three hours, leaving streets full of dead men and horses. Twenty-five people died in the English Company's house. The 'Tartar dog', the Crimean khan, retreated across the Oka that same night.

This crewell and sodayne destructyon cam vpon ye moscow in great and wonderfull whirl wynds, and at ye ende of thre owrs very calme agayne, and fayre wether, that men myght walke and se ye dede bodyes of men & horses to no small nomber … ye same daye that the tartar dogge had done this myscheffe, the nyght following he, with all his power, fled ouer the ryuar oca

CitiesMoscow1571

Moscow burned by the Crimean khan, 1571

Fletcher tells how the Crimean khan reached Moscow unopposed in 1571 and fired the suburbs. Because the city was built of wood, the blaze consumed most of it in about four hours, and crowds crushed each other at the gates. He gives the rumoured death toll of 800,000, which is far too high.

(which is all of wood, without any stone, brick, or lime, save certein out roomes) … consumed the greatest part of the citie almost within the space of foure houres, being of thirty miles or more of compasse. Then might you have seene a lamentable spectacle

Giles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

About Crimean Tartars under Devlet Giray

Keep in mind

The khan's statue in Moscow

Olearius retells the great Crimean raid of 1521: the khan besieged Moscow and forced the Grand Duke to sign himself a tributary. To prove it, the story goes, the khan set up a statue of himself in Moscow before which the Grand Duke had to bow to the ground each time he paid tribute; when the charter was later recovered, the Muscovites smashed the statue.

Mendligeri, to make it appear he was Sovereign Lord of Moscou, caused a Statue of his to be erected in the heart of the City, and would needs oblige the Great Duke, to express his subjection, to smite the ground with his head, before that Statue, as often as he paid Tribute

Adam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

Strange talesMoscow1554 (legend reported 1843)

The cathedral for Kazan and the blinded architect

St Basil's Cathedral on Red Square was built to celebrate Ivan the Terrible's capture of Kazan, the Tatar capital, in 1552. Haxthausen compares it to a rainbow-coloured dragon and repeats the tale that Ivan had the architect blinded so he could never build anything finer. He clearly marks it as hearsay ('is said to').

Ivan Vasilievitch ordered it to be erected in 1554 by an Italian, to commemorate the conquest of Kazan. After it was completed, he is said to have asked the architect whether he would undertake to make a plan for a still more singular building ; and when the latter in his vanity answered in the affirmative, he ordered his eyes to be put out

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Ivan IV; the conquered Kazan Tatars

Keep in mind

Russian tradition names the builders as the Russian masters Barma and Postnik, not an Italian, and the blinding story is a legend with no contemporary evidence.

BuildingsMoscowlate 1830s

Moscow's mosque, rebuilt by a Tatar merchant

Kohl visited Moscow's small mosque in the Tatar quarter. Its predecessor burned in the fire of 1812, and the poor congregation of about 120 Tatars could not rebuild until a wealthy Tatar paid for new walls. When Kohl saw it, it still had only a temporary roof and bare walls, and Russians had stolen one of its prized carpets.

The little building erected here to the honour of Allah … had a predecessor which in 1812 met with the same fate from the French fire, in which so many Russian churches shared. The flock were long unable to get together the necessary funds for a new temple, till about twelve years ago, when a wealthy Tartar erected the walls that are now standing.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

CitiesMoscow13th to 16th centuries, written late 1830s

Moscow 'singed' by the Tartars twelve times

Kohl says no European city except Constantinople had burned so often as Moscow, and that the Tartars alone had set it alight more than twelve times. He adds that about two-thirds of its 12,000 houses were still wooden, the rest built of soft local limestone and brick.

Constantinople excepted, there is, perhaps, no city in Europe that has been so often burnt to the ground. By the Tartars alone it was well singed more than twelve times.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

CitiesMoscow and Crimea1730

'Kremlin' as a Tartar word for fortress

In his alphabetical glossary Strahlenberg claims that 'Kremlin' is a Tartar word meaning fortress, and that the Tatars call the Crimea 'Crimm' because of the ancient rampart and ditch at Perekop. He adds that western Tartars and Uzbeks call the Great Wall of China the 'white fortress'. This is his own word-guessing, but it shows how 'Tartar' origins were read into Russian names early on.

Cremmelin, or Crimmelin; the Name of the Czar's Palace, in the City of Muscow. This is a Tartarian Word, and signifies a Fortress, But not a Flint, as Kremm does in the Sclavonian Language. Thus the Precopensian Tartary is call'd, by the Tartars, Crimm or Cremm, on Account of a Rampart and a Mote, which was there in ancient Times,

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Keep in mind

800,000 captives and old men given to boys

After the 1521 raid the Crimean khan is said to have driven off more than 800,000 prisoners, selling many to the Turks at Kaffa. Herberstein repeats the lurid claim that old captives, worth little at market, were handed to Tartar youths for practice in killing. He flags the numbers himself ('scarcely be considered credible', 'they say').

But he took with him from Moscow so great a multitude of prisoners as would scarcely be considered credible ; they say that the number exceeded eight hundred thousand, part of whom he sold in Kaffa to the Turks, and part he slew.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

BuildingsMoscow1581 to 1582

Walls rebuilt after the Tartar fire, a Kremlin by Italians

Possevino classifies Muscovite fortresses: some of stone and brick, some of earth banks woven with wicker and rammed hard, some of squared timbers filled with earth. Moscow's outer wall of the wattle and earth kind was put up after 'the Tartar fire', while the two stone citadels of Moscow were built for Ivan's father Vasily by a Milanese architect with Italian workmen. Here the Tartars burn, and Italians build.

Some are of stones and bricks, like the two that adjoin the city of Moscow ... Others are of earth banks woven with wicker and pressed hard: such is Smolensk, and the circuit of the walls of Moscow itself, raised after the Tartar fire. ... The two citadels of Moscow were ordered built by this prince's father Vasily, with a Milanese architect and Italian workmen.

Daily lifeMoscowNovember 1539 (7048)

Nogai horses for sale below Simonov

Nogai envoys arrived with letters from their mirzas asking for friendship. The grand prince lodged them at the 'Nogai Court' in Moscow and let them sell their horses on the meadow below the Simonov monastery. That year the horses were cheap: early snow had left no grazing, and many barely arrived alive or died. Steppe horse herds for Moscow's cavalry were the Nogais' great export.

And the grand prince favoured them, and wishes to keep his friendship with them, and ordered them lodged at the Nogai Court, and to trade their horses on the meadow below Simonov. And the horses were cheap, they barely arrived alive, and many others died, because snow fell early, for lack of fodder.

Crimeans, Nogais and 'the whole nation of the Tartarians'

An English visitor to Ivan IV's court lists the Tsar's usual enemies: Lithuania, Poland, Sweden, Denmark, Livonia, and three kinds of Tatars. He separates the Crimeans and the Nogais from 'the whole nation of the Tartarians' and praises them all as tough fighters.

His enemies, with whom he hath warres for the most, are these : Litto, Poland, Sweden, Denmarke, Liefland, the Crimmes, Nagaians, and the whole nation of the Tartarians, which are a stoute and a hardie people as any vnder the Sunne.

Three hundred Tartars drowned in a forced baptism

Fletcher reports that Russians offered captured Tartars their lives if they accepted baptism, but few agreed. The year after the 1571 fire, a captured Tartar noble and 300 of his men refused, and were 'baptized' by being knocked on the head and thrust into the river.

The yere after Mosko was fired by the Chrim Tartar, there was taken a Dwoymorsey, one of the chiefe in that exploit, with 300 Tartars more : who had all their lives offered them, if they would be baptized after the Russe manner. Which they refused all to doo … they were all baptized after a violent manner, being thrust downe with a knock on the head into the water.

Giles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

CitiesMoscowJuly 1521

Moscow in panic as the Crimean khan approaches

In 1521 the Crimean khan Mehmed Giray and his brother, the khan of Kazan, raided up to Moscow. Herberstein, drawing on witnesses including German gunners, describes the Grand Duke fleeing (reportedly hiding under a haystack) and crowds crushing each other at the fortress gates. The city bought off the khan with gifts, above all mead, and a written promise of tribute.

On the 29th of July the Tartars made a farther advance, and devastated the country with fire in all directions ; and such was the terror which they inspired amongst the people of Moscow, that they had little confidence in their security even in the city and the fortress.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

Moscow as a 'Tartar Rome'

Kohl opens his chapter on Moscow's churches with Madame de Staël's famous exclamation on first seeing the city: 'Voilà Rome Tatare'. It shows how Western Europeans used 'Tartar' as shorthand for Moscow's un-European look.

'Voilà Rome Tatare' means 'There is a Tartar Rome'.

Where the Chuvash smear fat on the shrine

Captain Woodroofe's journal calls the Chuvash 'the poorest and most wretched of all mortals', tributary to Russia and heavily taxed. He could not learn their beliefs, but saw that they stick part of their food over doors and windows, and that when they kill an animal the priest eats some of the fat, greases himself and smears their place of worship with it. He adds that the neighbouring Tartars eat horse meat.

We could not discover what notions the chuvashins have of religion, but observed when they dress victuals, they stick part of it over their doors and windows; and when they kill an animal, … their priest takes out the fat, and after eating some of it and greasing himself, he smears over their place of worship,

Captain Thomas Woodroofe, his 1740 journal as printed by Jonas Hanway (1753)
