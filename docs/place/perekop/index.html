Mud and dung huts in winter, round houses on carts in summer

Editorial contextBroniewski, the Polish envoy who spent nine months in the Crimean khanate, describes Tatar winter villages called 'ulus': huts of very thin wood daubed with mud and animal dung and roofed with reed. In April the Tatars leave them with wives, children and slaves, carrying round felt-covered houses that barely hold four or five people on two-wheeled carts drawn by camels or oxen, and return in October.

Tartar huts, which the barbarians commonly call 'vlnsi' (evidently ulus, a misprint), made like villages of very thin wood, plastered with mud or muck and the dung of cattle or animals, and roofed with reed ... But in summer and autumn the Tartars do not live in these huts, for in April, with wives, children, household and slaves, they are carried with their houses, which are round, covered with penulae (felt coverings), and scarcely roomy enough for four or five people to live in, on carts resting on two wheels ...

Felt tents on carts, as in Herodotus

Editorial contextLeaving the Crimea, the party came on a few nomad tents: round, about four feet high and eight feet across, covered in rushes and thick felt, with a smoke-hole and a banner marking the horde. She notes they ride on carts called kibitkas, just like the cart-dwelling Scythians of Herodotus, and that women live in separate tents.

the very tents that I have been describing, called Kabitkies, are placed on carts of the same name, and drawn with their inhabitants from one grazing ground to another, as the difference of season, or a want of pasture for their numerous flocks, demands a change of place. The women are kept in separate tents pitched near those of the men

Maria Guthrie (letter, translated by Matthew Guthrie, 1802)

Perekop: a stone town and a ditch across the isthmus

Editorial contextAt the neck of the Crimea stood Perekop, 'a stone town' where Crimean Tatars and Turks lived, with artillery. A ditch ran from sea to sea across six versts, two sazhens wide and three deep, with stone towers along it. Beyond the ditch people ploughed fields. A precise official description of a Crimean Tatar fortress.

And in Perekop there is a stone town, and Crimean Tatars and Turks live in it, and there is artillery in the town. And Perekop runs from sea to sea for 6 versts, and across Perekop [the ditch] is some two slanting sazhens, and three sazhens deep, and along the ditch stand stone towers too

Daily lifePerekop1829

Nogay herdsmen, 'inhabitants of waggons'

Editorial contextCrossing the sandy steppe on the way to the Crimea, Alexander passed herds of horses guarded by Nogay Tartars and their dogs. He likens them to the wagon-dwellers Herodotus described among the Scythians, notes their two- and four-wheeled carts drawn by horses, oxen or camels, and describes their sheepskin caps, grey coats and sandals of leather or linden bark.

passed several taboons of horses guarded by wild-looking Nogay Tartars and their dogs: these are the Hamaxobitii, or inhabitants of waggons, of Herodotus : their arbas are two or four-wheeled, and drawn by horses, oxen, or camels. The dress of these nomade tribes harmonises well with the Scythian deserts which they frequent ; a rough sheep-skin cap, a grayish surcoat reaching below the knees

James Edward Alexander (1830)

Daily lifePerekopc. 1540

A Jewish toll-keeper asks if anyone is left in Lithuania

Editorial contextAt Perekop, the single gate into the Crimea, Michalo met a Jewish toll collector who watched endless columns of Lithuanian captives being brought in and asked whether there were still any people left in their lands, and where so many came from. Michalo then describes how the stronger male captives were mutilated, branded and chained to work by day and locked up by night, while young women were kept for pleasure.

So a certain Jew there in Taurica, in charge of the toll at its one and only gate, and always seeing an innumerable multitude of our people brought in as captives, asked us whether there were still any people left in our lands or not, and where so great a supply of mortals came from.

Michalo the Lithuanian, De moribus Tartarorum, Lituanorum et Moschorum, fragment 1 (c. 1550; Basel, 1615)

BuildingsNorthern Crimea, between Perekop and KarasubazarApril 1786

Underground huts and a village in a circle

Editorial contextSouth of Perekop the post stations were sometimes in deserted Tatar villages, and sometimes the stable-keeper lived in a hut dug underground, which she calls common in this treeless, sun-baked country. At sunset she reached a Tatar village of huts straggling in a circle with no fence, while herds of horses, cows and sheep drifted home across the downs: 'a simple and majestic landscape'. A few lines earlier she had called Perekop's Tatar village the dirtiest place she ever saw.

The posts were sometimes in a deserted Tartarian village, and sometimes the only habitation for the stable-keeper was a hut made under ground, a common habitation in this country, where the sun is so extremely hot, and there is no shade of any sort.

Elizabeth Craven (letters published London, 1789)

Barrels of gold, some of them fake

Editorial contextPerry repeats the story of why Prince Vasily Golitsyn's army turned back from the Crimea in 1689: the Tatars brought him barrels of gold ducats, 'amongst which, as 'tis said, there were many Counters', and he made peace in the field. On the page before, he reports that the Crimean khan would inherit the Ottoman throne if the sultan's male line failed.

…being tempted by a great Sum of Ducats of Gold, which they brought to him in Barrels (amongst which, as 'tis said, there were many Counters) he concluded Conditions of Peace in the Field, and withdrew his Army.

Captain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

Four hundred ungreased wheels

Editorial contextAt the entrance to the Crimea Webster met a Tatar caravan of about a hundred wagons, drawn by oxen and camels, whose ungreased wheels made an 'excruciating' noise. The Tatars in their black lambskin caps, the camels and the bustards on the plain struck him as a novel eastern scene.

These people never grease the wheels. The creaking of one is sufficiently offensive; but the noise of four hundred was absolutely excruciating. The Tartars, too, were singing, which, with them, is another term for howling … The Tartars, in their black lamb-skin caps and eastern dresses, the camels grazing on each side of the road

James Webster

The Nogays: a fragment of the Golden Horde

Editorial contextAlexander describes the Nogays' faces and then explains who they are: a part of the Golden Horde scattered by Timur (Tamerlane), whose wars shattered the Horde in the 1390s. It is a short, fairly accurate piece of steppe history attached to a first-hand description.

Their noses are short, eyes elongated, and beards scanty : such are the Nogays, a party of the Golden Horde dispersed by Timoorlung (Tamarlane).

James Edward Alexander (1830)

A Nogai prince in a little clay town

Editorial contextAn Italian merchant told her he had crossed the Nogai steppe and was welcomed by the Nogai chief Beyazet Beg, who lived in a small clay-built town and ate mostly milk, koumis and mutton. Trying to please a European, the prince served an 'Italian' feast so strange that the guest slipped out to be sick, and promised a French one next day.

He said, he found their chief named Beyazet Beg, i. e. Prince Bajazet, dwelling in a little town built with clay, on the lake and rivulet Bolotchik… This Tartar prince, who lives like his Scythian ancestors… principally on milk and its preparation koumis (which constitutes the food, wine, and brandy of the Tartars), with occasionally the flesh of his flocks

Maria Guthrie (letter, translated by Matthew Guthrie, 1802), reporting an Italian merchant of Kherson

Dung fuel, millet and a whole sheep at one sitting

Editorial contextCrossing treeless plains, de Tott explains that the Nogais burn cakes of dung kneaded with sandy earth. He reports (as hearsay) that they had uprooted the old forests so no enemy could surprise them. Their everyday diet is millet and mare's milk, yet a Nogai could eat a whole sheep for a wager.

each family carefully collects the dung of the cattle, which they knead with a sort of sandy earth, and produce a turf which unfortunately smokes the Tartars more than it warms them. No people live more soberly. Millet and mares milk are their usual diet… A Noguais might lay a wager that he eat a whole sheep, and win his wager, without having an indigestion.

Baron de Tott (English translation, 1785)

About Nogai Tatars

Keep in mind

The claim that the Nogais tore up the forests is something he was 'assured' of, not something he saw.
