Bearded 'Alimenes' guarding the Tartar passes

Cruz reports that the king of China keeps salaried soldiers to guard the weak passes and walls facing the Tartars: big, bearded men in cut breeches and caps, with curved swords. A Portuguese captive heard the Chinese call them 'Alimenes', which Cruz takes to mean Germans (Alemanes), concluding that China must border Upper Germany. He tells the same story on an earlier page (n25). The identification is his own leap.

the Chinese say that the King of China has many salaried men-at-arms who guard for him the weak passes and the walls on the side of the Tartars; they say they are big, heavily bearded men who wear cut breeches, caps and curved swords; and a Portuguese who had been taken captive inland told me that he had heard the Chinese call these people Alimenes.

CitiesInner Mongolia (between the Nen River and the Great Wall)1693

A ruined city of the Tartar king 'Utai-chan'

On 10 October 1693 the caravan reached a large, old, deserted city called Taimingzinz, with two sealed towers, fallen masonry, and life-size stone statues of people and idols and huge stone lions, all carved so true to life that, Brand says, few such are found in Europe. The Chinese told him a Tartar king named Utai-chan had ruled there centuries before, until a Chinese king took it and drove the Tartar out; stone towers on the hills, they said, marked Tartar graves.

On 10 October we came to a great, old and ruined city called Taimingzinz, in which stand two towers … The Chinese told us that this place, many hundreds of years ago, when a Tartar king called Utaichan reigned there, was conquered by a Chinese king and the Tartar driven out

Daily lifeHami to Jiayuguan (Gansu corridor)1605

Tartar raiders on the road to the Great Wall

The Portuguese lay brother Bento de Goes travelled overland from India towards China in 1603 to 1605. On the last stretch he found the country between Cialis and the Chinese wall 'notorious for its Tartar raids': merchants crossed it by night in silence, and the bodies of lone Muslim travellers lay by the road. Goes adds a telling detail (in the sentence that follows this excerpt): the Tartars did not usually kill the local people, whom they treated as their servants and herdsmen and occasionally robbed of their flocks.

"The country between Cialis and the Chinese wall," Goes writes, "is notorious for its Tartar raids, so that traders always cross it in the greatest fear, sometimes even under cover of night and in the strictest silence. One often comes across the dead bodies of Mohammedans who have attempted the journey unaccompanied, though on the other hand the Tartars are not wont to kill the native population

Bento de Goes, as recorded in Matteo Ricci's Commentari and translated by C. Wessels (1924)

Daily lifeGubeikou, Great Wall1793

'No Chinese may punish me on the Tartar side of the wall'

Although Chinese and Tartars had one master, Staunton found the Great Wall still treated as the boundary between two peoples. Just past it, a Tartar attendant facing punishment by Chinese mandarins loudly refused, claiming they had no authority over him on the Tartar side. On the next page a Chinese mandarin dares not sit before a Tartar officer of equal rank.

The line it has traced, continues still to be considered as a demarkation between the respective nations of Chinese and Tartars. … one of the Tartar attendants was about to be punished for some misdemeanour, by order of the Chinese mandarines; but he resisted with much vehemence, loudly exclaiming, that no Chinese had a right of exercising authority over him on the Tartar side of the great wall.

George Leonard Staunton

The nameGreat Wall of China1654

Why the Chinese say 'Tata'

Martini defines his subject: the Tartars are simply the people living north of the Great Wall, which for 300 German leagues has served as a rampart against them. He adds that the Chinese, lacking the letter R, have always called them 'Tata', and that the name covers both the Eastern Tartars, unknown in Europe, and the Western ones stretching from Kashgar to the sea above Japan.

I call that Nation Tartars, which inhabiting the Northern parts, behind that famous Wall which stretching out above 300. German Leagues from East to West, hath ever served for a Rampart to hinder their irruptions into the said Empire. This Country the Chineses having a defect of the letter R. antiently called Tata:

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

BuildingsGreat Wall of China1556 to 1569

A hundred leagues of wall, with mountains in the gaps

Cruz reports that the Chinese have a wall of a hundred leagues (some say more) between themselves and the Tartars, always garrisoned against Tartar raids. He reasons that it cannot be continuous but must use mountains to fill the gaps, because a Persian lord told him Persia had similar walls broken by hills. The Wall is credited plainly to the Chinese, built against the Tartars.

as I have said, the Chinese have a hundred leagues of wall (others say it is more) between themselves and them, where there are always garrisons to defend against the incursions of the Tartars. It may be believed that this wall is not continuous, but that some mountains or ranges come in between, because a lord of Persia assured me that there were similar works in some parts of Persia, with hills or ranges coming in between.

A Mongol nun, who with a rosary in her hand kept moving her lips continually, was asked whom she worshipped; she answered: the god who was thrust out of heaven by our God, and her god would come back into heaven and throw our God down from heaven again

A 500-league wall against the Tartars, mostly mountains

Mendoza reports a wall 500 leagues long running west to east, built by a king 'Tzintzom' (Qin Shi Huang) to protect himself from the Tartars, 'and so the wall takes in the whole frontier of Tartary'. But, he adds, 400 of those leagues are made by nature, very high closed mountains; only the remaining hundred were built by the king, of very strong stone, seven fathoms wide at the base.

There is in this kingdom an enclosure or wall five hundred leagues long ... A king called Tzintzom made it to protect himself from the Tartars, with whom he was at war, and so the wall takes in the whole frontier of Tartary. But it must be understood that 400 of the leagues of the said wall are made by nature, for they are very high, very close-set mountains.

BuildingsInner Mongolia, north of the Great Wall1688

A Mongol tent 'like the roof of a round tower'

On his first journey beyond the Great Wall with the Qing embassy to the Russians, the Jesuit Gerbillon went into a Mongol tent 'the better to know how they were built'. He describes a round cage of small sticks about 13 or 14 feet across and 8 or 9 feet high, with a pointed roof, covered with felt ('woollen stuff, not woven, but mill'd'). Inside: a plank bed, a bench, a cupboard and wooden bowls.

It is a sort of Cage, made of pretty small Sticks, round, and about 13 or 14 Foot Diameter. … the Roofing begins at about four Foot from the Ground, and ends in a Point like the Roof of a Round Tower, or Pigeon-house: They are covered with Pieces of Woollen Stuff, not woven, but mill'd.

Jean-François Gerbillon, Jesuit (journal compiled by Du Halde, English translation, 1738)

BuildingsGreat Wall of China1683

The Great Wall, built by a Chinese emperor against the Tartars

Verbiest crossed the Great Wall several times and says all seven wonders of the world together could not match it. He credits it to an ancient Chinese emperor who built it as a bulwark against the Western Tartars, and was amazed that water, brick and mortar had been hauled up dry mountains. He measured one stretch with an instrument at 1,037 geometrical feet above the horizon.

'twas to make a Bulwork against their Incursions, that a Chinese Emperor in Antient times caused this great Wall to be Built, which seperates China from their Countrey. I have passed it several times, and have considered it very attentively, And I can say without Hyperbolizing, that all the seven Wonders of the World put together, are not comparable to this worke.

Ferdinand Verbiest (English translation, 1686)

BuildingsGreat Wall of China (north of Beijing)1696 (visit); wall credited to Qin Shi Huang

The Great Wall is brick, built against the Tartars

Careri rode out from Beijing and walked four miles up the mountains to see the Wall himself. It stood 15 to 20 feet high, higher and wider in the valleys, room for six horses abreast, built 'of large fire-baked bricks with few stones', with strong square towers two bowshots apart running to the sea. He credits the emperor 'Xi-hoamti' with building it more than 1800 years earlier 'as a defence against the raids of the Tartars'.

That wall is 15 feet high in some parts and 20 in others; but in the valleys it is much higher and wider, so that six horses can comfortably go abreast on it. The construction is of large bricks baked in the fire, with few stones, and from time to time it has its towers, very strong and square, one as far from the next as two bowshots

Three khans, one people

Bell names the three great 'Tartar princes' between the Volga and China: Ayuka Khan of the Volga Kalmyks, the Kontaysha of the Dzungars and the Tushetu Khan of the Khalkha Mongols. He treats all their subjects as one Kalmyk nation with the same looks, religion and language, and marvels at how far a traveller could go with that one language.

From the river Volga, to the wall of China, there are three great Tartar princes; the Ayuka-Chan, the Kontaysha, and the Tush-du-Chan. These three mighty nations have almost the same features, religion, and language; and live in the same manner. … whose subjects go by the general name of Kalmuks.

John Bell (Travels, 1763)

Strange talesGreat Wall of China1696

A wall up a cliff no Tartar horse could climb

Careri found the Wall one of the greatest and 'most senseless' works ever made. What made him laugh was seeing it carried to the top of a precipitous mountain where 'the Tartar cavalry' could never have climbed; and if the Chinese thought raiders could scramble up the cliffs, it was foolish to think so low a wall would stop them. A rare eyewitness who was unimpressed.

But what struck me as altogether laughable was to see the wall carried even to the peaks of a very high and precipitous mountain, where hardly even woodpeckers could cling, let alone the Tartar cavalry climb up to enter the country.

Where China ends and 'Tartary' begins: the Wall at Gubeikou

On the road north the embassy slept every night in the emperor's own travel palaces, so Hüttner skips the small places, but not the little town on the famous Great Wall 'which forms the border between China and Tartary', called Chu-pa-ku, 'between or at the wall' (Gubeikou). For him the Wall is the line where Tartary starts. He adds that the old Wall had fallen in many places and its towers had no garrisons, and the embassy took away pieces of its stone and mortar as curiosities.

since we slept every night in the imperial palaces in which the emperor himself is accustomed to stay … One small town, however, is too remarkable for me not to mention it. It lies on the famous Chinese Wall, which forms the border between China and Tartary, and is called Chu-pa-ku, that is, between or at the wall.

BuildingsGubeikou, Great WallSeptember 1793

Soldiers pocketing bricks from the Great Wall

Holmes reports what the soldiers were told on the spot: the Wall was built about 1,500 years earlier against Tartar raids on the northern provinces and ran about 1,400 miles. They measured it at 25 feet high and 36 paces thick at the gate, saw it was built of brick, and everyone from the ambassador to the privates collected pieces as souvenirs.

That part of it which we measured and examined, with uncommon pains, was in height 25 feet, and in breadth, through the gate, 36 regular paces, which breadth it carried to the summit. … It was built of brick; many pieces of which were as anxiously collected as if they were wedges of the most precious metal, by all ranks, from his Excellency to the private soldier

Samuel Holmes (diary printed 1798)

BuildingsGubeikou, Great Wall1793 (reported 1836)

The Great Wall measured: earth, brick and blue bricks that were really burned

Using Captain Parish's survey for Macartney's embassy, Davis gives the wall's make-up: an earthen core faced on both sides with masonry and brick, a platform of square bricks, 20 feet high including the parapet, 25 feet thick at the base. The bricks are bluish, which made people doubt they were fired, until old kilns were found and Dr Abel showed in 1816 that Chinese clay burns blue. The wall, he says, was a useful barrier until Chinggis Khan overran it.

The body of the wall consists of an earthen mound, retained on each side by walls of masonry and brick, and terraced by a platform of square bricks. The total height, including a parapet of five feet, is twenty feet, on a basis of stone projecting two feet under the brick-work,

John Francis Davis, summarising Captain Parish's survey (Macartney embassy)

Marco Polo's lost city of Campion

Strahlenberg says he asked every nation in Tartary about Marco Polo's city of Campion, shown on old maps, and found no one who had heard of it. He concludes the name was mispronounced and that it must be the city at the western entrance of the Chinese Wall, which the Tatars call Camtzi, where all caravans must stop and wait for permission to enter China.

although I made considerable inquiry among all the nations of Tartary about the name of the city of Campion, as Marco Polo and other old maps set it before us, I nevertheless found not a single one who could inform me about it from old or recent times … [the city] which lies in the mountains at the entrance of the Chinese Wall on the western side, which the Tatars call Camtzi instead of Campion

Kangxi kills a bear with a half-pike

Davis says the emperors Kangxi and Qianlong kept up 'the hardy and warlike habits of the Manchows' with great ring hunts north of the Wall, led by a small army with falconers. He quotes Gerbillon: Kangxi shot a bear with an arrow from across a valley, then dismounted and finished it with a half-pike 'used by the Manchows against tigers'. Davis contrasts this with the later emperors who kept cancelling the hunt.

The Emperor, upon this, alighting from his horse took a half-pike, used by the Manchows against tigers, and, accompanied by four of the ablest hunters armed in the same way, he approached the bear and killed him outright with a stab of his half-pike.

Jean-François Gerbillon, quoted by John Francis Davis

Tartary: herds on the hills, few houses

From the Wall it was four days' hard travel through hills and valleys to Jehol. Holmes found the country fertile and good pasture for immense herds of sheep and cattle, but thinly peopled, with only scattered houses in the valleys and no place of note until Jehol.
