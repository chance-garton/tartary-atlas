Strange talesMongolia1840s

Royal tombs with poisoned children and arrow traps

Huc passes on a lurid report about Tartar kings' burials: a brick building with statues, a central cavern full of treasure, children killed with mercury to keep their faces fresh and set up around the corpse holding the king's pipe and snuff, and a spring-loaded battery of bows to shoot any grave robber. He says bow-makers sold such traps and that Chinese sometimes bought them to guard their houses.

These monstrous interments sometimes cost the lives of a great number of slaves. They take children of both sexes, remarkable for their beauty, and make them swallow mercury till they are suffocated; in this way they preserve, they say, the freshness and ruddiness of their countenance, so as to make them appear still alive.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Wiped out by the Tatars: the Mongols' escape from Ergenekun

Hammer retells the Mongol origin legend: long before Chinggis Khan the Mongols were exterminated by their enemies the Tatars, all but two men, Tegus and Kijan, who fled into a valley walled by steep rocks. Over centuries their descendants multiplied and, tired of their mountain prison and their mining, broke out by driving the flames of great wood piles with seventy bellows against the ore wall until it melted (p. 14). All true Mongols claimed descent from these two. Hammer suspects the story hides the real subjugation of the Xiongnu, who mined for their conquerors in the Altai, and notes that Persian poets placed this mountain prison behind the dam of Gog and Magog.

Tatars wanted to be Turks, Turks wanted to be Tatars

Hammer notes that the Black Tatars near Baikal and the Water Tatars on the Kerulen appeared at the end of the 12th century under the name Mongols. When Chinggis Khan raised this small people to world rule, Turkic and Tatar peoples claimed the name for themselves, just as earlier, while Turks and Tatars ruled, each had wanted the other's name. Only later, once that power had faded, did Mongols and Kalmyks refuse to be called Tatars and Ottomans refuse to be called Turks; his note cites Rubruck's remark that the Mongols did not want to be called Tartars. On p. 22 he adds that the Turkic peoples of Russia are called Tatars, 'though the name is incorrect'.

When the small people living south-east of Lake Baikal … was raised by Chinggis Khan to be the ruling tribe of world-conquering armies, Turkic and Tatar peoples laid claim to this name, just as, when the Turks and Tatars held power, Turks wanted to be called Tatars and Tatars Turks. Only in more recent times … do the Mongols and Kalmyks no longer want to be called Tatars, nor the Ottomans Turks.

Bone skates, but no monsters

From informants at Mongke's court Rubruck learned of northern peoples: the Orengai, who tied polished bones under their feet and sped over snow and ice fast enough to catch birds and beasts. He also asked about the monstrous races described by Isidore and Solinus; his informants said they had never seen any such thing, which, he writes, 'we much wonder whether it is true'.

There are also the Orengai, who tie polished bones under their feet and push themselves over the frozen snow and over the ice so fast that they catch birds and beasts. … I asked about the monsters, or monstrous men, that Isidore and Solinus tell of. They told me they had never seen such things, at which we much wonder whether it is true.

Daily lifeCentral Mongolia (upper Kerulun to the Tuul)June 1221

Furs, milk and the tall bark headdress

West of the Kerulun bend the country was crowded with carts and tents, and the people raised cattle, hunted, wore furs and skins and lived on milk and meat. Married women wore a two-foot headdress of tree bark covered in wool or, among the rich, red silk, with a long 'tail' called yu-yu that looked like a goose or duck, and they entered tents backwards with heads bent so as not to knock it (next page). Bretschneider matches it with the tall headdress described by Carpini and by Rubruck, who calls it bocca. On the same page, Mongols who had waited a year for the sage brought him millet and were amazed by the jujubes he gave them, a fruit they had never seen.

They dress in furs and skins, and live upon milk and flesh-meat. The men and unmarried young women plait their hair so that it hangs down over their ears. The married women put on their heads a thing made of the bark of trees, two feet high, which they sometimes cover with woollen cloth, or, as the rich used to do, with red silk stuff.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Grand Cham of the Tartars, named after the river Tata

At Genghis Khan's proclamation, Pétis says, the new emperor praised the 'Soumoguls', called Tartars after the river Tata that ran through their land, and added 'Grand Cham of the Tartars' to his title to honour them. A footnote explains that this is how all the Scythian nations under Genghis came to be called Tartars, and that the Chinese, lacking an r, said 'Tata'.

And having much cause to praise the Soumoguls (who are particularly called Tartars, by reason of the River Tata that waters their Country) he declared in the Mogul Language, that he would add to his Title of Emperor of the Moguls that of Grand Cham of the Tartars, both to do that Nation Honour

Daily lifeMongoliac. 1245 (written c. 1321)

One god, many idols, and the sin of a knife in the fire

Sanudo's chapter on 'the customs of the Tartars' says they believe in one God, maker of all, but do not pray to him; they worship idols that give them answers. They are 'too superstitious': it is a sin to stick a knife in the fire, touch fire with a knife, take meat from the cauldron with it, or chop with an axe near the fire, 'for they think the head is thus cut off the fire'. Killing and invading other lands, he adds, they do not count as sin.

The Tartars worship one God, maker of all things, giver of good and of punishments in this world; yet they do not worship him with prayers or praises or in any other way, but idols from which they get answers. ... They are too superstitious: for they think it a sin to stick a knife in the fire, or touch it in any way with a knife, or take meat out of the cauldron with it, or chop with an axe beside the fire: for they think that in this way the head is taken off the fire.

From Yuan emperors back to 'Dada'

The history of the Mongol domains says the Yuan's ancestors, the Mongols, came from the far northwest, conquered the world and unified it. After 1368 the Yuan heir fled north and kept his dynastic title, but a later usurper, Guilichi, called himself khan, dropped the dynastic name and 'again called [them] Dada', Tatars. The Qing record thus treats Tatar as the name the Mongols fell back on after losing China.

The forebears of the Yuan were called Menggu (Mongols); they lived on the far northwestern frontier, took possession of all under heaven and so unified it. At the start of the Ming, Ayushiridara fled back to the northern desert and resumed his old title... Guolegi (Guilichi) usurped the throne, styled himself khan, abolished the dynastic name and again called [them] Dada (Tatars).

BuildingsMongolian steppec. 1275-1292

Round houses of wands and felt, doors to the south

Polo describes the classic Mongol dwelling: a round frame of light, tightly bound wands covered with felt, carried from camp to camp, always pitched with the door facing south. Families also had felt-covered wagons, rainproof and drawn by oxen and camels, in which women and children travelled. Yule's note compares the Kirghiz yurts of his own day.

Their houses are circular, and are made of wands covered with felts. These are carried along with them whithersoever they go ; for the wands are so strongly bound together, as well as so well combined, that the frame can be made very light. Whenever they erect these huts the door is always to the south.

Marco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

The Mongols do not call themselves Tartars

Strahlenberg insists that the Mongols and Kalmyks, whom Europeans call Tartars, do not use the name and dislike it. In his view the name belonged to the 'original Tartars' near China and was stretched to cover the Mongols because they allied with them long ago. He adds that the Chinese used 'Tata' much as the Romans used 'barbarian'.

But to return to the Mungal Nations: We must know, that these are the People whom the Chinese, with Respect to their Situation, call West Tartars, and others West Mungals: But they do not reckon themselves Tartars, neither would they, by any Means, be thought so by others.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Chen-hai's town of Chinese craftsmen

North of the snowy mountains lay T'ien Chen-hai ba-la-ho-sun (balgasun, the diarist explains, means city), a granary town nicknamed 'the head of the magazines', where Chinese artisans and two concubines of the Jin emperor Zhangzong turned out to greet the sage, and Changchun was surprised to see farming in the desert. Bretschneider's note, from the Yuan shi, says Chinggis had founded a military colony and built a city here, settling over 300 families from Western Asia to weave gold brocade and 300 from Kaifeng to make woollen cloth, under Chen-hai, the chancellor Chinqai whom Carpini later met. Before moving on, the sage's followers built a monastery there in under a month, 'the rich with money and the workmen with their labour' (p.61).

a number of Chinamen, artisans and workmen, who lived there … came in procession to see the master. They were all ravished; met him with exclamations of joy, bowed before him, and accompanied him with variegated umbrellas and fragrant flowers. There were also two concubines of the Kin emperor, Chang tsung, and the mother of a Chinese princess, who met the master with exclamations and tears.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

'Not so much as a single house' in all Mongolia

Staunton footnotes the embassy's entry into Mongol lands with John Bell's description of the Mongols. Bell says that in all Mongolia there was not a single house: everyone, even the prince and the high priest at Urga, lived in tents and moved with their herds. It is very remarkable that in all the vast dominions of Mongalia, there is not so much as a single house to be seen. All the people, even the prince and high priest, live constantly in tents, and remove all their cattle from place to place as conveniency requires.

The Xiongnu town that was never built

The Chinese annals tell how a Chinese defector at the Xiongnu court, Wei Lü, advised the young shanyu to dig wells, build a town and two-storey granaries, and give the grain to Chinese captives to keep. Hundreds of wells were dug and thousands of logs felled, but others argued that the Xiongnu could not defend towns and the grain would only fall to the Chinese, so the plan was dropped. A vivid example of steppe rulers weighing fixed buildings against mobility.

Wei Lü advised the shanyu to dig wells, build a town and two-storey storehouses to keep grain, and to entrust the keeping of the grain to 'Qin Chinese'. Then, even if Chinese troops came, they could do nothing. Several hundred wells had already been dug and several thousand logs cut, when some pointed out that the Xiongnu cannot defend towns

Prester John was a mishearing
