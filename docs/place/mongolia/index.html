Strange talesMongolia1840s

Royal tombs with poisoned children and arrow traps

Huc passes on a lurid report about Tartar kings' burials: a brick building with statues, a central cavern full of treasure, children killed with mercury to keep their faces fresh and set up around the corpse holding the king's pipe and snuff, and a spring-loaded battery of bows to shoot any grave robber. He says bow-makers sold such traps and that Chinese sometimes bought them to guard their houses.

These monstrous interments sometimes cost the lives of a great number of slaves. They take children of both sexes, remarkable for their beauty, and make them swallow mercury till they are suffocated; in this way they preserve, they say, the freshness and ruddiness of their countenance, so as to make them appear still alive.

Who Mongol princes (reported)

Legend or rumourÉvariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Keep in mind. Huc did not see this; 'they say' marks it as hearsay, and it echoes older Chinese tomb legends.

The Naiman ruler Tayang plans to attack the upstart people to his east, and his mother Gurbesu sneers that they stink and wear dirty dark clothes, so only their good-looking women are worth fetching to milk the Naiman herds. In the Mongolian original these people are the Mongols; the Ming Chinese summary calls them Dada, the Chinese word for Tatars, while it keeps a different spelling for the actual Tatar tribe. The same summary has To'oril ask 'how can you Dada be without an emperor?' of Chinggis's people.

Tayang said: 'Over here in the east there are some Dada (Tatars)... Let us go now and take those Dada.' His mother Gurbesu said: 'Those Dada people have a bad smell and their clothes are dark and grimy. What would we do bringing them here? Let them stay far away.'

Daily lifeMongoliac. 1245 (written c. 1321)

One god, many idols, and the sin of a knife in the fire

Sanudo's chapter on 'the customs of the Tartars' says they believe in one God, maker of all, but do not pray to him; they worship idols that give them answers. They are 'too superstitious': it is a sin to stick a knife in the fire, touch fire with a knife, take meat from the cauldron with it, or chop with an axe near the fire, 'for they think the head is thus cut off the fire'. Killing and invading other lands, he adds, they do not count as sin.

The Tartars worship one God, maker of all things, giver of good and of punishments in this world; yet they do not worship him with prayers or praises or in any other way, but idols from which they get answers. ... They are too superstitious: for they think it a sin to stick a knife in the fire, or touch it in any way with a knife, or take meat out of the cauldron with it, or chop with an axe beside the fire: for they think that in this way the head is taken off the fire.

The nameMongolia13th to 15th centuries (compiled to 1842)

From Yuan emperors back to 'Dada'

The history of the Mongol domains says the Yuan's ancestors, the Mongols, came from the far northwest, conquered the world and unified it. After 1368 the Yuan heir fled north and kept his dynastic title, but a later usurper, Guilichi, called himself khan, dropped the dynastic name and 'again called [them] Dada', Tatars. The Qing record thus treats Tatar as the name the Mongols fell back on after losing China.

The forebears of the Yuan were called Menggu (Mongols); they lived on the far northwestern frontier, took possession of all under heaven and so unified it. At the start of the Ming, Ayushiridara fled back to the northern desert and resumed his old title... Guolegi (Guilichi) usurped the throne, styled himself khan, abolished the dynastic name and again called [them] Dada (Tatars).

BuildingsMongolian steppec. 1275-1292

Round houses of wands and felt, doors to the south

Polo describes the classic Mongol dwelling: a round frame of light, tightly bound wands covered with felt, carried from camp to camp, always pitched with the door facing south. Families also had felt-covered wagons, rainproof and drawn by oxen and camels, in which women and children travelled. Yule's note compares the Kirghiz yurts of his own day.

Their houses are circular, and are made of wands covered with felts. These are carried along with them whithersoever they go ; for the wands are so strongly bound together, as well as so well combined, that the frame can be made very light. Whenever they erect these huts the door is always to the south.

Who Tartars (Mongols)

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Keep in mind. Yule notes the manuscripts differ on 'felts' (one reads 'ropes'); he chose felt as the right reading.

The nameMongolia1711 to 1730

The Mongols do not call themselves Tartars

Strahlenberg insists that the Mongols and Kalmyks, whom Europeans call Tartars, do not use the name and dislike it. In his view the name belonged to the 'original Tartars' near China and was stretched to cover the Mongols because they allied with them long ago. He adds that the Chinese used 'Tata' much as the Romans used 'barbarian'.

But to return to the Mungal Nations: We must know, that these are the People whom the Chinese, with Respect to their Situation, call West Tartars, and others West Mungals: But they do not reckon themselves Tartars, neither would they, by any Means, be thought so by others.

Who Mongols and Kalmyks (Oirats)

Heard itPhilipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

Keep in mind. His explanation of how the name spread rests on his own reading of Abulghazi and Chinese sources, and on guesswork about Oghuz Khan.

Staunton footnotes the embassy's entry into Mongol lands with John Bell's description of the Mongols. The Xiongnu town that was never built

The Chinese annals tell how a Chinese defector at the Xiongnu court, Wei Lü, advised the young shanyu to dig wells, build a town and two-storey granaries, and give the grain to Chinese captives to keep. Hundreds of wells were dug and thousands of logs felled, but others argued that the Xiongnu could not defend towns and the grain would only fall to the Chinese, so the plan was dropped. A vivid example of steppe rulers weighing fixed buildings against mobility.

Wei Lü advised the shanyu to dig wells, build a town and two-storey storehouses to keep grain, and to entrust the keeping of the grain to 'Qin Chinese'. How the map of Tartary was put together

The atlas foreword, summarising du Halde, says the Jesuits' manuscript maps passed through d'Anville's hands before engraving.
