Raiding the Land of Darkness on mares with foals

Far to the north, Polo says, lies a Land of Darkness where no sun, moon or stars are seen. Tartars raid it riding mares whose foals they leave at the border, trusting the mares to find the way back to their young. The pale inhabitants hunt sables, ermine and black fox for the fur trade. Yule suggests the story garbles the Arctic winter night.

The Tartars however sometimes visit the country, and they do it in this way. They enter the region riding mares that have foals, and these foals they leave behind. After taking all the plunder that they can get, they find their way back by help of the mares, which are all eager to get back to their foals

Legend or rumourMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Tartars (Golden Horde raiders) and northern fur hunters

Keep in mind

Pure hearsay; the mare-and-foal trick is an old travellers' tale also told of Alexander the Great.

Old maps of Tartary drawn before even the Russians knew it

In his preface Gerritsz attacks a German doctor, Helisaeus Röslin, who claimed ships could sail round Nova Zembla to Cathay. Röslin's 'absurdities', he says, rest on maps of Tartary drawn in very old times, when the Russians themselves did not know the country. A sharp early warning that the big 'Tartary' of old maps was guesswork.

This was also done by a certain Heliseus Röslin, a doctor of Hanau … in which (after many absurdities which he grounds on the maps drawn of Tartary from very old times, when the Russians themselves were unacquainted with it) he says: that the nearer the pole the warmer it is in summer time

Read itHessel Gerritsz, preface to the 1612 Dutch tracts (English translation by F. J. Millard in the 1878 facsimile edition)

Daily lifeGolden Horde steppe near Tana (Azov), Russia1430s

The nomad Horde sows its grain

From the Venetian Josafa Barbaro, who lived at Tana, Hammer reports that Ulu Muhammad kept his camp moving for eleven years without letting the Horde sow in March and wait for the harvest, so it suffered grain shortage and lived on meat and milk alone. Normally, in February the camp announced the sowing day; those who wanted to grow grain drove out with wagons and seed to fields a day or two from the camp. The harvest was rich: fifty-fold for grain and a hundred-fold for millet. Nomads, in other words, also farmed.

Otherwise it was the custom that in the month of February the day on which sowing should take place in March was proclaimed in the camp. On that day those who wished to grow grain drove with their wagons and seed to the fields, usually one or two days' journey from the court camp, and tilled them. The harvest was plentiful, for the soil gave fifty-fold of the grain

Sonst war es der Gebrauch, dass im Monathe Februar im Lager der Tag, an welchem im März gesäet werden sollte, ausgerufen ward. Die Ernte war ergiebig, denn der Boden gab das Fünfzigfache des Kornes

Olla Billa: the Tartar war cry

Fletcher describes Tartar raiders setting dummy riders on spare horses to look more numerous, and charging with the cry 'Olla Billa' (Allah, bi-llah), which he translates 'God help us'. He contrasts their refusal to surrender with the behaviour of Russian and Turkish soldiers.

When they make any onset their manner is to make a great shoote, crying all out together, Olla Billa, Olla Billa, God helpe us, God helpe us. They contemne death so much as that they chuse rather to die then to yeeld to their enimie, and are seene when they are slaine to bite the very weapon

Heard itGiles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

Pigs as a border defence

Russian borderers, Fletcher says, kept almost nothing but pigs, because Muslim Tartar raiders would not touch or drive them off. He also notes that the Crimean khan was now largely picked by the Ottoman sultan and paid him a tenth of his war booty.

The Russe borders (being used to their invasions lightly every yeere in the sommer) keepe fewe other cattel on the border parts save swine onely, which the Tartar will not touch nor drive away with him ; for that he is of the Turkish religion, and will eate no swines flesh.

Heard itGiles Fletcher (1591; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

Sober Tatars who still enjoy a drink

Commenting on Ibn Fadlan's horror at Rus drinking, Frähn notes that not all Muslims keep the ban on wine. Among the Tatars of Russia, he says, 'sober as this people is', he met many who did not refuse wine and strong drink, though he never saw them drink too much. It is a small firsthand note on real Tatars by a scholar who knew them.

And so among the Tatars of Russia too, sober as this people is, I have found not a few who did not scorn wine and such strong drinks, though I never saw them exceed moderation.

Original wording

Kasimov: a Tatar prince's town on the Oka

When 'wind-swift' Tatars of Sayyid Ahmad's horde raided Muscovy, Sultan Kasim drove them off, and in gratitude the grand prince granted him a yurt (here, a domain) on the Oka in the Murom district, and 'there he built a town'. Hammer adds that the district town that still bears his name, on a height at the mouth of the Babinka, ringed by an earthen rampart, was in his day home to about five hundred Tatars with their own quarter and mosque. A rare case of a Tatar town founded inside Russia with Russian consent.

Out of gratitude the grand prince granted Kasim a yurt on the Oka, in the district of Murom. There he built a town … His name is still borne today by the district town at the mouth of the Babinka into the Oka, which, lying on a height and surrounded by an earthen rampart, and still inhabited today by half a thousand Tatars, preserves the memory of its origin through its Tatar quarter and mosque.

Working translation

Original wording

Aus Dankbarkeit dafür räumte der Grossfürst dem Kasim einen Jurt an der Okka, im Gebiethe von Murom, ein. Dort baute er eine Stadt … Seinen Nahmen erhält noch heute die am Einflusse der Babinka in die Okka gelegene Kreisstadt, welche, auf einer Anhöhe gelegen, mit einem Erdwall umgeben, noch heute von einem halben Tausend Tataren bewohnt, durch tatarische Slobode und Moschee das Andenken ihres Ursprunges bewahrt.

A stone that 'burns like a candle' in Witsen's Tartary book

Among the rocks of the district Schrenk lists a black, combustible shale soaked with bitumen from the Ukhta, known locally as Domanik, and in a footnote he notes that the naphtha springs there were already known to Witsen. He translates the passage from the first edition of Witsen's 'Noord en Oost Tartarye' (Amsterdam 1692): a shallow in the river drives a black oil to the surface, and a stone found there burns like a candle with a black light. Schrenk calls it the oldest report of the Domanik, a real oil-bearing rock, and goes on to later Russian accounts of an old timber enclosure built over the oil spring.

the river Oegta [Ukhta] is a day's journey from the Pechora; on this river … there is a shallow in it (drooghte die daer in is) which drives a fattiness onto the water, which is black naphtha; which stone is also found there, and burns like a candle, giving off a black light.

Working translation

Original wording

der Fluss Oegta [Uhta] ist eine Tagereise vom Petschorastrom entfernt; an diesem Flusse … findet sich darin eine Untiefe (drooghte die daer in is), die eine Fettigkeit aufs Wasser treibt, welche schwarze Naphta ist; welcher Stein dort auch gefunden wird, und brennt wie eine Kerze, ein schwarzes Licht von sich gebend.

Read itNicolaes Witsen (Noord en Oost Tartarye, 1692), quoted in German translation in a footnote by Alexander Gustav von Schrenk
Keep in mind

The 'black light' is Witsen's wording, and his source for it is not given here. Schrenk confirms that the shale exists and burns, but not Witsen's exact description.

Strange talesSouthern frontier of Russia1570s

'Away with those new devils and their thundering puffs'

Horsey claims he persuaded Ivan IV to arm captured Scottish mercenaries and send them against the Crimean Tartars. Twelve hundred Scots with guns, he boasts, did better than 12,000 Russian archers, and the Tartars, unused to firearms, fled crying out against the 'new devils'.

Twelve hundred of them did better service against the Tartor then 12 thowsand Russes, with their shortte bowe and arowes. The Crim, not knowinge then the use of peece and pistolls, stroken dead of their horses with shott they sawe not, cried : " Awaye with those new divells that com with their thunderinge puffs ;"

Heard itSir Jerome Horsey (memoir; ed. E. A. Bond, 1856)

About Crimean Tartars

Keep in mind

A boastful anecdote; the Crimean khanate had firearms and Ottoman gunners by this time.
