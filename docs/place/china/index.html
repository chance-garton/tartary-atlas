The nameChina13th century (written about 1610)

Was China's Tartar conqueror Tamerlane?

Ricci writes that China had never been wholly conquered by foreigners until a Tartar leader invaded; his line ruled until 1368, when the Chinese threw off the yoke and the Ming founder Hongwu expelled the Tartars. Some Jesuits guessed this conqueror was Tamerlane, because the Chinese called him 'Tiemor'. Ricci gives up and simply calls him 'the Tartar'. The Chinese name was almost certainly Tiemuzhen, that is Temüjin (Chinggis Khan), not Timur, who lived a century later and never reached China.

yet we do not read that it was ever wholly conquered by foreign peoples until, in the year of our salvation …, some leader from Tartary, a conqueror of kingdoms, invaded this kingdom with his victorious army. Some of our men, on conjectures that in my judgement are not slight, think this leader was Tamerlane or one of his successors, for the Chinese call him Tiemor … Whoever he was, I shall call him the Tartar.

A town of leather tents, with streets and markets

When the trumpet sounds at night, every soldier pulls his tent from the baggage wagons. Palafox says the tents are made of strong leather or raw hides sewn together, big enough to live in, and pitched in order with streets and market places like a great town. Daily lifeChina1644 onward

The queue: 'the most open and degrading mark of conquest'

After the Manchus took Peking, Davis writes, the Chinese were forced to shave their long hair and adopt 'the Tartar fashion of a long plaited tress, or tail', and Tartar dress, on pain of death. He reflects that changing the whole nation's costume touches every single person and is the plainest possible sign of conquest. The old Chinese costume, he notes, now survives only on the stage.

In other respects, too, they were commanded to adopt the Tartar habit on pain of death; and many are said to have died in preference to submission… an entire alteration in the national costume affects every individual equally, from the highest to the lowest, and is perhaps, of all others, the most open and degrading mark of conquest.

Read itJohn Francis Davis (The Chinese, 1836)

Bearded 'Alimenes' guarding the Tartar passes

Cruz reports that the king of China keeps salaried soldiers to guard the weak passes and walls facing the Tartars: big, bearded men in cut breeches and caps, with curved swords. A Portuguese captive heard the Chinese call them 'Alimenes', which Cruz takes to mean Germans (Alemanes), concluding that China must border Upper Germany. He tells the same story on an earlier page (n25). The identification is his own leap.

the Chinese say that the King of China has many salaried men-at-arms who guard for him the weak passes and the walls on the side of the Tartars; they say they are big, heavily bearded men who wear cut breeches, caps and curved swords; and a Portuguese who had been taken captive inland told me that he had heard the Chinese call these people Alimenes.

Tartary: the Chinese name for everything beyond the Wall

Discussing a Siberian Tatar story that Chinese once lived in Siberia, Müller suggests that at most the Chinese may have built some towns on the Daurian and Mongol frontiers and toward Liaodong, because in China all lands beyond the Wall are called Tartary. By all the circumstances one may judge that they perhaps meant certain towns built on the Daurian and Mongol borders and toward Liaotung, of which there are examples in ancient times, because in China all the lands lying beyond the Wall are called by the general name Tartary.

The 'genuine Tartars' are going native

After praising Tartar warfare, Polo adds a striking remark: in his day the Mongols were changing. Those settled in China had taken up the religion and ways of the Chinese, and those in the Middle East had adopted Muslim customs. It is an eyewitness observation that the conquerors were being absorbed by the settled peoples they ruled.

But I must add also that in these days they are greatly degenerated ; for those who are settled in Cathay have taken up the practices of the Idolaters of the country, and have abandoned their own institutions ; whilst those who have settled in the Levant have adopted the customs of the Saracens.

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

The 'Tartar ceremony': nine knocks of the head

The embassy's quarrel was over the 'Tartar ceremony' demanded before the emperor. Ellis notes that the English word kowtow is loose: the court rite is properly 'thrice kneeling and nine times bowing the head', a Manchu court protocol.

The Tartar ceremonial has perhaps improperly been rendered by the Chinese word ko-tou, which more strictly signifies three simple genuflexions, a mode of salutation practised even in private life. The court ceremony has a different name, implying thrice kneeling and nine times bowing the head, San-kwei-keu-kou.

Heard itHenry Ellis (footnote)

The appended East Indies volume includes a history of the Tartar conquests of China. Why the Chinese say 'Tata'

Martini defines his subject: the Tartars are simply the people living north of the Great Wall, which for 300 German leagues has served as a rampart against them. He adds that the Chinese, lacking the letter R, have always called them 'Tata', and that the name covers both the Eastern Tartars, unknown in Europe, and the Western ones stretching from Kashgar to the sea above Japan.

I call that Nation Tartars, which inhabiting the Northern parts, behind that famous Wall which stretching out above 300. German Leagues from East to West, hath ever served for a Rampart to hinder their irruptions into the said Empire. This Country the Chineses having a defect of the letter R. antiently called Tata:

Cruz reports that the Chinese have a wall of a hundred leagues (some say more) between themselves and the Tartars, always garrisoned against Tartar raids. He reasons that it cannot be continuous but must use mountains to fill the gaps, because a Persian lord told him Persia had similar walls broken by hills. as I have said, the Chinese have a hundred leagues of wall (others say it is more) between themselves and them, where there are always garrisons to defend against the incursions of the Tartars. A khan buried under a hill with slaves and impaled horses

Ibn Battuta reports that a Mongol khan was killed by rebel kinsmen and buried with about a hundred relatives. The khan was laid on a couch in an underground chamber with his weapons, gold and silver vessels, four slave women and six favourite servants, and earth was heaped over it into a hill. Four horses were impaled on stakes on the mound. Everyone wore white hoods for mourning.

a large sepulchre was dug for him under the earth, in which a most beautiful couch was spread, and the Khan was with his weapons laid upon it. With him they placed all the gold and silver vessels he had in his house, together with four female slaves, and six of his favourite Mamluks … and the earth heaped upon them to the height of a large hill.
