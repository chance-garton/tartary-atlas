A Qing envoy's journey to the Torghut khan on the Volga, 1712 to 1715

A Manchu official sent by the Kangxi emperor records crossing Siberia and Russia to visit Ayuki Khan of the Torghut Mongols near the Caspian.

Catalogue title Narrative of the Chinese Embassy to the Khan of the Tourgouth Tartars

Tulišen (Tulishen)

Tulishen, a Manchu official, led the Kangxi emperor's embassy of 1712 to 1715 through Mongolia, Siberia and European Russia to Ayuki Khan of the Torghuts (Kalmyks) on the lower Volga. His report, known in Chinese as Yiyu lu, was published by imperial authority in 1723. It describes Russian towns such as Irkutsk, Tobolsk and Kazan, the Tatars living among the Russians, and a long, formal conversation with Ayuki Khan. This corpus copy is George Thomas Staunton's 1821 English translation, which adds many footnotes quoting John Bell's Russian embassy of 1719 to 1722; the corpus also holds the Chinese text (T1072).

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Several groups: the Manchus (Staunton's 'Mantchoo Tartars', including the author himself); the Mongols and the Torghut Kalmyks ('Tourgouth Tartars'); and the Turkic-speaking Tatars of Tobolsk and Kazan, whom the Chinese text calls Ta-ta-la (搭搭拉), after the Russian name.
Why read it
A rare view of Siberia, Russia and the Kalmyks through Chinese and Manchu eyes, with sober descriptions of towns and a telling moment when a Kalmyk khan asks whether Manchus and Mongols share one origin.
Watch out
Staunton's footnotes quote John Bell at length; those passages are Bell, not Tulishen. The narrative follows Qing court conventions (every foreign ruler 'receives an edict kneeling'), and some place names are garbled Chinese renderings of Russian ones.

CitiesKazan1714

Kazan in 1714: a wooden city of Russians and Tatars

Tulishen describes Kazan, the old Tatar khanate capital, as a Russian provincial seat: no stone walls but wooden palisades with eight gates, a ditch and wooden spikes, three public buildings of wood, five brick churches, and two-storey wooden houses for about 5000 families. The people were a mix of Russians, Tatars, Cheremis and Torghuts.

Instead of regular walls, Cazan is surrounded with wooden palisades. It has 8 gates … Within the city there is a market, three public edifices of wood, and five churches built of brick. The population consists of about 5000 families. The dwelling houses are all built of wood, and raised to the height of two stories. The inhabitants are a mixture of Russians, Tartars, Tzeremish, and Tourgouths.

CitiesLower Volga steppe (Kalmyk lands east of the Volga)June 1714

Arriving at Ayuki Khan's camp by Lake Manuto

After ten days' ride from Saratov the envoys reached the headquarters of Ayuki Khan, not a town but a camp 'at present' on the shore of a lake. The Torghut nobles, priests and chiefs lined the road with their followers, displayed their flocks and herds, set out tables of food, and knelt to offer provisions. The next day the envoys were received in the khan's 'great tent of ceremony'.

we at length arrived at the head-quarters of A-yu-ke, the Khan of the Tourgouth nation, which are situated at present upon the banks of the lake Ma-nu-to. … Their flocks and herds were also collected and displayed, and tables, covered with provisions, laid out before us. All the people had come out to a very considerable distance to meet us

CitiesTobolsk1713

Tobolsk: a timber capital with two brick buildings

The capital of Russian Siberia, as Tulishen saw it, had no walls; about a thousand families lived in houses of large timber, with two big brick public buildings and wooden government offices, and a lower town of about 2000 families by the river. It is a plain eyewitness picture of Tobolsk as a Russian timber town seven years after its first stone kremlin was begun.

It contains above a thousand families, who live in houses constructed of large timber. There are also two large public buildings of brick, and several Government offices built of wood in the usual manner. On the low ground, along the banks of the river, there is another portion of the town, built also of wood, and containing above 2000 families.

BuildingsMongoliaabout 1720

'Not so much as a single house' in all Mongolia

Staunton footnotes the embassy's entry into Mongol lands with John Bell's description of the Mongols. Bell says that in all Mongolia there was not a single house: everyone, even the prince and the high priest at Urga, lived in tents and moved with their herds. It is a clear eyewitness statement, from a Scot who crossed the region, that the Mongol heartland had no built settlements in his day.

It is very remarkable that in all the vast dominions of Mongalia, there is not so much as a single house to be seen. All the people, even the prince and high priest, live constantly in tents, and remove all their cattle from place to place as conveniency requires.

Daily lifeTobol river, between Tobolsk and Tyumen1714

Tatar boat-haulers flogged by Russian soldiers

Going up the Tobol river from Tobolsk to Tyumen, the embassy's boats were towed the whole way by Tatars. In Tulishen's own Chinese words, there was no towpath, the men waded through mud until their legs bled, and the Russian soldiers kept whipping them until the envoy, unable to watch, told them to stop. It is one of the few moments where the author shows personal feeling.

All along the way the Ta-ta-la (Tatar) people hauled the towropes. The banks were thick with trees and there was no towpath, so they all walked in mud and water; the skin of their legs and feet was torn and blood ran down, yet the Russian soldiers still flogged them on. I could not bear to watch, and only when I rebuked them did they stop.

Daily lifeDzungaria (lands of the Kontaysha)about 1720

Kalmyks who pity people tied to one place

Another footnote quotes Bell on the Dzungar Kalmyks of Tsewang Rabdan (the 'Kontaysha'), who could field 100,000 horsemen. Bell, relying on Russians who had visited, says they lived in tents all year and found it amusing to pity settled people who had to work for a living, which they considered 'the greatest slavery'.

These Tartars live in tents, all the year, removing from place to place, as called by necessity or inclination. This is the most ancient and pleasant manner of life. It is entertaining to hear them commiserate those who are confined to one place of abode, and obliged to support themselves by labour, which they reckon the greatest slavery.

The nameTobolsk1713

The Tartars of Tobolsk, once subjects of Kuchum Khan

At Tobolsk the envoys asked about a people the Russians called Tartars (Ta-ta-la), who lived from the Irtysh to Kazan. Russians told them these people had once been subjects of Kuchum Khan (Ku-tching Khan), the last khan of Sibir, and had spread out after Tobolsk became Russian. Tulishen noted that those who had not become Orthodox Christians looked like Chinese Muslims (Hui): short hair, small caps, no pork.

the Russians said: "This people was formerly subject to the Ku-tching Khan, otherwise called Ho-tung. … Some among them have conformed to our religion, but others have not." Such of this people as have not conformed to the religion of the Russians, have the appearance of being (Hoei-tse) Mahometans. They wear small caps, and their hair cut short. They eat no pork

The nameLower Volga steppe (Ayuki Khan's camp)June 1714

A Kalmyk khan asks if Manchus and Mongols were once one people

In his long interview Ayuki Khan asked the envoys a question about origins: Manchus and Mongols look so alike that they must spring from one source, so how did they become separate nations? He asked them to put it to the emperor. The envoys promised to do so. He went on to ask about Manchu writing and why there were 'old' and 'new' Manchus.

"The Mantchoos and the Mongals bear a great resemblance to each other: I imagine therefore that they sprang anciently from the same origin. But how has it happened that they have since formed separate states and nations? The causes of this, are no doubt well known to your Emperor.

The nameManchuria (Yehe)1712

The envoy himself: a man 'from Ye-ho in Tartary'

Tulishen opens by introducing himself as coming originally from Ye-ho in Tartary, meaning the Yehe lands of the Manchu (Jurchen) confederation north-east of China. John Bell, who met him in 1720, called him 'a Mandshu Tartar by birth'. In this book the Chinese court's envoy to the 'Tartar' Kalmyks was himself a 'Tartar', a reminder of how loosely Europeans used the word.

am originally from the country of Ye-ho in Tartary. My ancestor, O-yen … was a man honorably distinguished in the country of Ye-ho by his fidelity and exertions.

The nameKuban (north-west Caucasus)1714

The Kuban Tartars, raiders subject to the Turks

Describing the Torghut country between the Volga and the Yaik, Tulishen places to the west the Ho-pang, which Staunton identifies as the Cuban Tartars, also called Mang-vu-te (Mangyt, a Nogai clan name). He says they were subjects of the Turkish sultan and constantly raided both Russians and Torghuts. It shows a Chinese writer mapping the Nogai Tatars by their own clan name.

lies the country of the Ho-pang (Cuban Tartars) otherwise called Mang-vu-te, a nation subject to Kung-ke-eur Khan, king of the Tu-li-ye-se-ko, (Turks). This people are constantly in the practice of coming across the boundary, in order to attack and plunder their Russian and Tourgouth neighbours.

Strange talesYeniseisk region, Siberia1713

The mammoth: a giant beast that lives under the earth

Near Yeniseisk the envoys heard of a huge animal in the coldest north that burrowed underground and died if exposed to sun and air. Its white, ivory-like bones were dug from river mud and carved by Russians into cups and combs, and its flesh was eaten against fever. The foreign name was Ma-mien-tou-va (mamont), the Chinese called it kee-shoo (the 'hidden rat'). Staunton notes that John Bell heard the same tale and that frozen mammoth flesh had since been found.

In the very coldest parts of this northern country, a species of animal is found which burrows under the earth, and which dies if it is at all exposed at any time to the sun and air. Its bones are very white and shining, like ivory. … The Russians collect the bones of this animal, in order to make cups, saucers, combs

Strange talesLower Volga steppe (Ayuki Khan's camp)June 1714

A khan who 'received the edict kneeling'

Tulishen writes that Ayuki Khan knelt to receive the Chinese emperor's letter and turned north to wish him well. The translator Staunton adds a sharp footnote: it is very unlikely that an independent prince would humble himself so in his own court; the Chinese claim to rule the whole world simply required every letter from the emperor to be described as received on bended knee.

Nothing certainly can be more improbable than that an independent prince, in the midst of his own court, should have voluntarily subjected himself to such a humiliation; … but it seems that the assumed attribute of universal sovereignty, which occasions every communication from the Emperor of China to claim the authority of an Edict, also requires that such an edict should be supposed to have been received
