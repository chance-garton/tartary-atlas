Joseph Wolff's mission to Bukhara to find two missing British officers

An eccentric missionary's account of his dangerous 1843 to 1845 journey to the Emir of Bukhara, mixing eyewitness scenes, desert tales and borrowed statistics.

Catalogue title Narrative of a Mission to Bokhara

Joseph Wolff

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

Joseph Wolff, a German-born Jewish convert who became an Anglican clergyman, had already visited Bukhara in 1832. In 1843 he set out again, alone and in clerical robes, to learn the fate of Colonel Stoddart and Captain Conolly, two British officers held by the Emir of Bukhara. He found they had been executed and was himself held for months before being released. The book mixes his own experiences in Persia, the Turkmen desert and Bukhara with his earlier travels, long religious digressions, Turkmen and dervish tales, and descriptions of Khiva, Kokand and Bukhara copied from Burnes and others. This corpus copy is the enlarged London edition of 1846, which calls itself the fourth edition (p. 353).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Wolff uses 'Tatar' in several senses: the Uzbeks ('Usbeck Tatars'), the Nogai Tatars of Russia, Turkic people from Yarkand in 'Chinese Tartary', the historic Tatars of Genghis Khan and Timur, and even the Ottoman courier called a 'tatar' who guided him in Turkey.
Why read it
A lively picture of Bukhara's crowded streets and colleges, plus rare glimpses of how local people talked about Genghis Khan, Timur and Russia, including a Turkmen tale of Timur's pyramid of skulls and dervishes calling Russia Gog and Magog.
Watch out
Wolff was a zealous missionary with strong religious opinions, a love of legends and a taste for self-dramatisation. Many figures about Khiva, Kokand and Bukhara are copied from Alexander Burnes and other writers rather than observed.

CitiesBukhara1844

Bukhara: eleven gates and 360 mosques

Wolff gives a thumbnail sketch of Bukhara: a walled city of fifteen miles' circuit with eleven gates, hundreds of mosques, caravanserais, baths and about a hundred colleges, and the old citadel (the Ark), which he says was built a thousand years earlier by Arslan Khan.

It has eleven gates, and a circumference of fifteen English miles; three hundred and sixty mosques, twenty-two caravanserays, many baths and bazaars, and the old palace called Ark, built by Arslan Khan one thousand years ago, and has about one hundred splendid colleges. The houses have neither roofs nor windows.

Joseph Wolff

About Uzbek emirate of Bukhara

Keep in mind

The number 360 is a traditional round figure, and the date and builder of the Ark are local tradition. 'Neither roofs nor windows' probably means flat roofs and no outward-facing windows.

CitiesBukhara1844

A street full of 'Tatars' of every kind

As Wolff rode into Bukhara in his clerical robes, crowds lined the streets and rooftops. His list of onlookers shows how many different peoples could be called 'Tatar' in one city: Nogai Tatars from Russia, Kazakhs and Kirghiz from the steppe, and a 'Tatar' from Yarkand in Chinese Turkestan.

It was a most astonishing sight ; people from the roofs of the houses, the Nogay Tatars of Russia, the Cassacks and Girghese from the deserts, the Tatar from Yarkand or Chinese Tartary, the merchant of Cashmeer, the Serkerdeha or grandees of the king on horseback, the Affghauns, the numerous water-carriers, stopped still and looked at me

Joseph Wolff

CitiesSamarkand1844

Samarkand, 'Ornament of the Face of the Earth'

Wolff describes Samarkand, Timur's old capital, as a clay-walled town of 40,000 people with a palace, marble houses, mosques, colleges and Timur's tomb. He adds stories that Chinese once lived there making paper from silk, and that the city took its name from a conqueror called Samar.

It has a wall of clay, and forty thousand inhabitants; a beautiful palace, and many houses of marble; many mosques and colleges. It was formerly inhabited by Chinese, who manufactured paper of silk; and it once had the name of Bokhara-Tsheen, but received the present name from the conqueror Samar, after Christ 643. Oologh Beyk erected there an observatory.

Joseph Wolff

About Emirate of Bukhara; Timur

Keep in mind

Wolff did not describe visiting Samarkand on this journey; the name story and the Chinese paper-makers are traditions he repeats.

CitiesBukhara1219 to 1220

Genghis Khan takes Bukhara

In a later addition to the book Wolff notes that Genghis Khan besieged Bukhara in 1219 and took it in 1220, and repeats a tradition that thousands of Jews then fled to China. He adds that the city's houses were mostly of mud.

The town of Bokhara is situated one day's journey to the north of the river Jehoon or Amoo ; Ghengis Khan besieged it in 1219, and took it in 1220. Thousands of Jews emigrated at that time to China. The houses are mostly built of mud.

Joseph Wolff

About Mongols under Genghis Khan; Jews of Bukhara

Keep in mind

The Jewish migration to China is Wolff's claim, probably from Bukharan Jewish tradition, not a documented event.

BuildingsKhiva1840s

Khiva and Kokand: towns of mud

Drawing on Alexander Burnes and other authors, Wolff describes the capital of Khiva as a town of mud huts with three stone mosques and a mud palace, living largely by the slave trade, and says Kokand was likewise built of mud except for three stone bazaars.

The capital of Khiva is a town of mud huts, with three stone mosques, and a mud palace. Its chief trade is in slaves, of whom there are about forty thousand in the Khanat ; and it is said there are many captured Russians, who are slaves in Khiva. … It is, like Khiva, built of mud, with the exception of three stone bazaars.

Joseph Wolff, summarising Alexander Burnes and other writers

About Uzbek khanates of Khiva and Kokand

Keep in mind

Second-hand; Wolff did not visit Khiva or Kokand on this journey.

BuildingsBukhara1830s to 1840s

Bukhara's sun-dried brick and arched bazaars

Quoting Burnes's figures, Wolff describes Bukhara's massive buildings, the Khan's fortress-palace, houses of sun-dried brick, narrow dirty streets and lofty arched bazaars where each trade had its own section.

It has massive buildings, three hundred and sixty mosques, three hundred schools, the Khan's palace, or rather fortress ; houses built of bricks dried in the sun ; very narrow and dirty streets ; lofty arched bazaars, in which the different trades have their distinct sections ; and ponds and fountains for the supply of water.

Joseph Wolff, summarising Alexander Burnes

Daily lifeMerv1832

Turkmens pray without mosques

On his first journey Wolff noted that the Turkmens had no mosques, praying alone in the open or in their tents and gathering twice a year in the desert for common prayer. He also records a claim by the Jews of Turkestan that the Turkmens descend from the biblical Togarmah.

All the Jews of Turkistaun assert that the Turkomauns are the descendants of Togarmah, one of the sons of Gomer, mentioned in Genesis x. 3. The Turkomauns have no mosque; they pray apart from each other, either in the field or the tent. Twice in the year they assemble in the desert, and prefer their prayer.

Joseph Wolff

Keep in mind

Daily lifeMerv to Bukhara1844

Turkmen elders who could advise but not punish

Wolff describes his Turkmen guides as truly 'masterless': their greybeards (aqsaqals) could give advice and lead raids, but had no power to punish anyone.

The Turkomauns my guides were, in the strictest sense of the word, masterless : for their Aga Sakals, ' lords with the beard,' have only a right to give advice, and to conduct them on their plundering expeditions, but they have no power to punish.

Joseph Wolff

The nameTabriz1844

Twin brothers named Mogul and Tatar

In a Tabriz prison Wolff met a captive chieftain who wrote out his family tree for him. It calls him a 'Tatar of the family of Genghis Khan' and gives a traditional origin story: an ancestor had twin sons, Mogul Khan and Tatar Khan, from whom the Moguls and the Tatars descend.

Muhammed, son of Izhak Khan Kerahe, Tatar of the family of Ghengis Khan. The ancestors of Ghengis Khan were Oolinjah Khan and Olamgoo, a Mogul, who had twins : the name of the one was Mogul Khan, and the other Tatar Khan, from whom all the Tatars descend, as the Moguls do from Mogul Khan.

Muhammad Khan Qarai (in Wolff's translation of his autograph)

About Muhammad Khan Qarai, a Khorasani chief claiming descent from Genghis Khan

Keep in mind

A traditional genealogy (a version of the Mongol origin story found in Persian and Turkic histories), not history.

The nameBukhara1498 onward

Uzbeks replace the 'Turks or Tatars'

Wolff says that after the 'Turks or Tatars' were driven out, the Uzbeks became the ruling people, brought to the Oxus by Shaybani Khan with their first centre at Urgench in Khiva. He treats 'Turk' and 'Tatar' as one name for the earlier rulers.

Since the expulsion of the Turks or Tatars, the Usbecks are the dominant people. Sheybek Khan brought them to the country of the river Amoo in the year 1498, when they had the centre of their empire at Organtsh, in Khiva.

Joseph Wolff

About Uzbeks under Shaybani Khan

Keep in mind

Wolff's date is approximate; Shaybani Khan's conquest of Transoxiana is usually dated to about 1500 to 1507.

Strange talesKonye-Urgench1844 (telling); 1370s to 1380s (events)

Timur's pyramid of skulls, as the Turkmens told it

Snowed in at a desert camp, Wolff listened to Turkmens talk of Alexander and Timur as if they had lived yesterday. One told how Timur punished the Khan of Khwarazm by building a pyramid of human skulls cemented with clay at Urgench, sparing only dervishes, scholars and poets.

It is remarkable to hear these Turkomauns speak of the exploits of Alexander and Timur, exactly as if of modern occurrence. … He made a pyramid at Organtsh entirely of skulls of men, cemented with clay. He spared the lives of none, except those of holy derveeshes, of the learned, and of poets, around whose houses he placed Karawl, i. e. guards.

A Turkmen in the Merv desert, as reported by Joseph Wolff

About Turkmens of the Merv desert; Timur (Tamerlane)

Keep in mind

Oral tradition. Timur's skull towers are recorded elsewhere (Wolff also mentions one at Sabzevar), but this telling is folk memory.

Strange talesDesert between Bukhara and Merv1845

Russia is Gog and Magog

In a caravan leaving Bukhara, a Turkmen told Wolff that Russia would conquer the world, having built a fort in the sea near Khiva. A dervish added that the great mullahs of Samarkand said Russia was Gog and Magog, the destroying peoples of the end times.

A Turkomaun present said, "The Russians shall be the conquerors of the world. They have now built a strong castle almost in the midst of the sea, not far from Khiva. … A derveesh sitting among us made the following remark ; "The great mullahs of Samarcand assert that Russia is the Jaaj-Majooj, i. e. Gog and Magog

A Turkmen and a dervish, as reported by Joseph Wolff

About Dervishes and Turkmens

Keep in mind

A prophecy repeated in conversation, reflecting fear of Russian expansion.

Strange talesBukhara1844

The rumour that Wolff had been to Tartarus

Wolff found a rumour running through Bukhara that he knew 72 languages and 72 religions, had spoken with 72 nations, and had even visited the 'land of darkness', which he glosses as Tartarus. The word play between Tartary and Tartarus here comes from Wolff's own gloss.

A report was spread abroad, I found, throughout the whole country of Bokhara, that Mullah Joseph Wolff, the grand derveesh from England, was acquainted with seventy-two languages, with seventy-two religions, and had conversed with seventy-two nations of the earth. That, further, I had been in Sulmistaun, i. e. the ' land of darkness," Tartarus

Joseph Wolff

About People of Bukhara

Keep in mind

Gossip about Wolff himself; the 'Tartarus' gloss is his.

Strange talesNew York1837 to 1838

Were the Mohicans Tatars?

On a trip to America Wolff thought he saw signs that Native Americans were of 'Tatar race': a shared word for 'great', a fondness for the number nine, and a custom of playing a mournful flute outside a sweetheart's tent like the Turkmens. It is a striking example of how far the Tatar idea could stretch.

Many of their customs, besides words in their language, and their physiognomy, rather seem to me to betray a Tatar race. Thus, for instance, they have the word kelaun, great, which is also used in the same sense at Bokhara. They have nine as a favourite number, which the Tatars also have.

Joseph Wolff

About Mohican people of North America, compared with Tatars and Turkmens

Keep in mind

Wolff's own speculation from a few coincidences.
