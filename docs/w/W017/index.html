Two French priests cross Mongolia on the way to Tibet, 1844 to 1846

Lazarist missionaries Huc and Gabet travel by camel through Inner Mongolia, living in tents among Mongols, on their famous journey to Lhasa.

Full title Travels in Tartary, Thibet, and China

Évariste Régis Huc; Joseph Gabet

Read the 18 passagesOpen the original book

Évariste Huc and Joseph Gabet, French Lazarist missionaries based north of the Great Wall, set out in 1844 dressed as lamas and travelled by camel across Inner Mongolia, the Ordos and Kokonor to Lhasa, arriving in 1846. Huc's lively account, published in French in 1850, describes Mongol tents, food, marriage, burial, lamaseries, towns, ruins and the pressure of Chinese settlers on Mongol pasture. This corpus copy is volume I of William Hazlitt's English translation (London, National Illustrated Library, 1852), which covers the Mongolian part of the journey.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Chiefly the Mongols ('Tartar-Mongols', 'Western Tartars'), but Huc also calls the Manchus 'Eastern Tartars' and follows Chinese usage (Ta-Dze) for both.
Why read it
The most vivid close-up of Mongol daily life in the 1840s, and it includes a half-buried abandoned city, cave-houses dug by Chinese settlers, and a Mongol herder's own view of tents versus houses.
Watch out
Huc is a sharp observer but a storyteller who sometimes embroiders, and he writes as a Catholic missionary with strong opinions about lamas and Chinese. Some chapters mix what he saw with what he read in Chinese or European books.

CitiesInner Mongolia (between Tolon-Noor and the Blue Town)1844

An abandoned city half buried in the soil

On the road to the Blue Town (Hohhot) the priests came upon a large abandoned walled city with towers and four gates facing the cardinal points. It was three-quarters buried by wind-blown soil and covered in turf, so that streets and buildings showed only as bumps in the ground. The only person there, a young Mongol shepherd, knew nothing except that it was called the Old Town.

we came, in the solitude, upon an imposing and majestic monument of antiquity, … a large city utterly abandoned. Its turreted ramparts, its watch towers, its four great gates, facing the four cardinal points, were all there perfect, in preservation, except that, besides being three-fourths buried in the soil, they were covered with a thick coating of turf.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

About Mongols (a local shepherd)

Keep in mind

Huc blames 'gradual accumulations of wind-borne soil' for the burial; he does not suggest any flood or catastrophe.

CitiesInner Mongolia13th to 15th century

Who built the lost cities of Mongolia

Reflecting on the Old Town, Huc says such ruined cities are common in Mongolia and that their story is unknown locally. His best guess, from Chinese annals, is that the Mongols built many large cities in 'Northern Tartary' while they ruled China, and that after their expulsion the Ming emperor Yongle burned their towns in three campaigns deep into the desert.

the probabilities are, that they date no earlier back than the 13th century, the period when the Mongols rendered themselves masters of the Chinese empire … During their domination, say the Chinese annals, they erected in Northern Tartary many large and powerful cities. … the Emperor Young-Lo, who desired to exterminate the Tartars, invaded their country, and burned their towns

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

About Mongols (Yuan dynasty); Ming Chinese

Keep in mind

Huc's explanation is an educated guess from Chinese histories, not local tradition.

CitiesUlaanbaatar (Urga)1840s

The Great Kouren: a monastery city of 30,000 lamas

Huc describes the Great Kouren (Urga, today's Ulaanbaatar) on the Tula river: temples with gilded roofs, 30,000 lamas, and a plain always covered with pilgrims' tents from every people of the Qing north, including 'Fish-skin Tartars' from the Amur and Torghuts. The next page adds a fortified Chinese trading post of wooden and mud huts, where everything was priced in bricks of tea.

Thirty thousand Lamas dwell in the Lamasery itself, or in smaller Lamaseries erected about it. The plain adjoining it is always covered with the tents of the pilgrims who resort hither from all parts to worship Buddha. Here you find the U-Pi-Ta-Dze, or "Fish-skin Tartars," encamped beside the Torgot Tartars

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

CitiesYinchuan (Ningxia)10th century to 1227

Ningxia, a Tartar capital ruined by Genghis Khan

Passing through the half-deserted city of Ning-Hia, Huc gives its history: a prince 'of Tartar race' from Tou-Pa founded a small state near the Yellow River in the tenth century with Ning-Hia as its capital (the Tangut Xi Xia kingdom). It flourished for two centuries until 1227, when it was ruined by Genghis Khan. Here 'Tartar' covers the Tanguts too.

In the tenth century, a prince of Tartar race, a native of Tou-Pa, … having induced a few hordes to follow him, came, and formed, despite the Chinese, a small state not far from the banks of the Yellow River. He chose for his capital, Hia-Tcheou, which afterwards came to be called Ning-Hia. … in 1227, it was involved in the common ruin, by the victories of Tchinggiskhan

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Chakhar, Red Banner)1844

How a Mongol tent is built

Huc visited Mongol families and describes their tent exactly: a cylinder about three feet high topped by a cone, a folding lattice of crossed bars below, a ring of poles meeting at the top like umbrella ribs, a thick covering, and a low folding door with a beam across the bottom. The next page describes the smoke-hole flap of felt and the men's and women's sides inside.

The Mongol tent, for about three feet from the ground, is cylindrical in form. It then becomes conical, like a pointed hat. The woodwork of the tent is composed below of a trellis-work of crossed bars, which fold up and expand at pleasure. Above these, a circle of poles, fixed in the trellis-work, meets at the top, like the sticks of an umbrella.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Chakhar)1844

'No towns, no edifices' in the land of grass

Crossing the prairies of the Chakhar, Huc sums up what makes Tartary unlike anywhere else: no towns, no buildings, no crafts, no farming and no forests, only endless grassland, lakes, rivers and mountains. In the next paragraph he describes encampments of tents appearing and vanishing as herders moved to fresh pasture.

There are no towns, no edifices, no arts, no industry, no cultivation, no forests; everywhere it is prairie, sometimes interrupted by immense lakes, by majestic rivers, by rugged and imposing mountains; sometimes spreading out into vast limitless plains.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Keep in mind

A literary generalisation; elsewhere Huc describes lamaseries, trading towns and ruins in the same region.

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Gechekten)1844

A Mongol: 'We Mongols are formed for living in tents'

In Gechekten, where Chinese settlers had turned grassland into poor sandy fields, the priests asked a Mongol rider why his people did not farm. He answered that Mongols were made for tents and herds; since they had started farming and building houses they had grown poor, and the Chinese (Kitat) had taken their land, herds and houses. It is a Mongol's own verdict on houses versus tents.

"We Mongols are formed for living in tents, and pasturing cattle. So long as we kept to that in the kingdom of Gechekten, we were rich and happy. Now, ever since the Mongols have set themselves to cultivating the land, and building houses, they have become poor. The Kitats (Chinese) have taken possession of the country; flocks, herds, lands, houses, all have passed into their hands.

A Mongol horseman, as reported by Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

About Mongols of Gechekten (Hexigten)

BuildingsInner Mongolia (Kingdom of Efe)18th century

A city built for a Mongol prince by imperial slaves

The Qianlong emperor married a daughter to a Khalkha prince, who hated court life and longed for his tents. As a compromise the emperor carved out land in the Chakhar, 'built for him' a small handsome city, and gave him a hundred families of slaves skilled in Chinese arts and crafts. The princess got her court; the prince could go back to the tent. Huc credits the city to the emperor and Chinese craftsmen, not to the Mongols.

He dismembered a portion of the Tchakar, and assigned it to the Mongol prince; he built for him, amid these solitudes, a small but handsome city, and presented to him a hundred families of slaves skilled in the arts and manufactures of China.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

BuildingsOrdos1844

Cave houses dug by Chinese settlers

Caught in a freezing storm in the Ordos, the priests found a ravine lined with man-made caves, neatly plastered, with windows, stoves and heated beds (kang). Huc explains that Chinese settlers in Tartary dig such homes into hard hillsides. A Mongol sheltering in the next cave told them he had known the Chinese families who dug them, and that the local Mongols had driven them out two years before for encroaching and stealing.

not simple caves formed by nature, but fine, spacious apartments excavated by the hand of man. … When the Chinese establish themselves anywhere in Tartary, if they find mountains, the earth of which is hard and solid, they excavate caverns in their sides. These habitations are cheaper than houses, and less exposed to the irregularity of the seasons.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Daily lifeInner Mongolia (Chakhar)1844

A minstrel's song: 'Return, O Timour!'

At a feast for the Moon festival a Mongol bard (toolholos) played a three-stringed fiddle and, at the priests' request, sang the 'Invocation to Timour', a lament for lost greatness that begs a hero to return and make the Mongols warriors again. Huc reproduces it in translation. It shows nineteenth-century Mongols remembering their imperial past in song.

"When the divine Timour dwelt within our tents, the Mongol nation was redoubtable and warlike; its least movements made the earth bend; its mere look froze with fear the ten thousand peoples upon whom the sun shines.

A Mongol minstrel, as rendered by Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Daily lifeInner Mongolia (Chakhar)1844

Women do all the work, men ride

Huc is blunt about the division of labour: Mongol women milk the cows, make butter and cheese, fetch water, gather and dry dung fuel, make clothes, tan skins and full cloth. The men's work is herding on horseback, which Huc calls more amusement than labour. The page opens with the Chinese nickname Tsao-Ta-Dze, 'Stinking Tartars'.

Among the Tartars, household and family cares rest entirely upon the woman; it is she who milks the cows, and prepares the butter, cheese, &c.; who goes, no matter how far, to draw water; who collects the argol fuel, dries it, and piles it around the tent. The making of clothes, the tanning of skins, the fulling of cloth, all appertains to her

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Daily lifeInner Mongolia1840s

Marriage as a contract of sale

Huc says Mongol marriages were arranged by parents through go-betweens, and that the bride's family was paid in horses, oxen, sheep, cloth, butter, brandy and flour. The Mongols themselves spoke of 'buying' and 'selling' a daughter. He goes on to describe a mock fight at the bride's door, a gallop to the new tent, polygamy and easy divorce.

The thing is indeed very clearly expressed in their language; they say, "I have bought for my son the daughter of so and so." "We have sold our daughter to such and such a family." The marriage contract is thus simply a contract of sale.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Daily lifeInner Mongolia1844

Brick tea, 'the grand luxury of the Tartars'

Huc explains 'Tartar tea': coarse tea leaves pressed into bricks, broken off, boiled until red, salted and mixed with milk. He says the Tartars (with the Russians) were its only consumers. Elsewhere he notes that at Urga bricks of tea served as money.

the coarser leaves, with which are mixed up the smaller tendrils, are pressed together in a mould, in the form and of the size of the ordinary house brick. Thus prepared, it becomes an article of considerable commerce, under the designation of Tartar-tea, the Tartars being its exclusive consumers, with the exception of the Russians

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Daily lifeInner Mongolia1840s

Burning the dead in an earthen pyramid

Huc describes several Mongol funerals: nomads left the dead on hilltops or in ravines for eagles and wolves, while rich families built a pyramid-shaped earth furnace around the standing body, burned it, and had the Grand Lama knead the powdered bones with meal into cakes that were stacked and placed in a small tower.

The richer Tartars sometimes burn their dead with great solemnity. A large furnace of earth is constructed in a pyramidical form. Just before it is completed, the body is placed inside, standing, surrounded with combustibles.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

The nameManchuria and Mongolia1840s

Eastern Tartars and Western Tartars

Huc sets out how he uses the word: following the Tartars and Chinese themselves, he divides 'the Tartar people' into Eastern Tartars (Toung-Ta-Dze), the Manchus, and Western Tartars (Si-Ta-Dze), the Mongols. He dismisses European map labels such as 'Kalmoukia' and 'Great and Little Bukharia' as 'purely imaginary'.

we will divide the Tartar people into Eastern Tartars (Toung-Ta-Dze), or Mantchous, and Western Tartars (Si-Ta-Dze), or Mongols. … Great and Little Boukaria, Kalmoukia, Great and Little Thibet … all these denominations seem to us purely imaginary.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

The nameHohhot (Koukou-Khoton)1844

Manchu soldiers who no longer speak Manchu

At the garrison town of Koukou-Khoton (Hohhot), Huc found that the 10,000 'Mantchou Tartars' stationed there hardly spoke their own language. Two centuries after conquering China, he says, the Manchus had become Chinese in manners, language and even homeland.

The soldiers of the New Town of Koukou Khoton are Mantchou Tartars; but if you did not previously know the fact, you would scarcely suspect it from hearing them speak. Amongst them there is perhaps not a single man who understands the language of his own country.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Strange talesMongolia1840s

Royal tombs with poisoned children and arrow traps

Huc passes on a lurid report about Tartar kings' burials: a brick building with statues, a central cavern full of treasure, children killed with mercury to keep their faces fresh and set up around the corpse holding the king's pipe and snuff, and a spring-loaded battery of bows to shoot any grave robber. He says bow-makers sold such traps and that Chinese sometimes bought them to guard their houses.

These monstrous interments sometimes cost the lives of a great number of slaves. They take children of both sexes, remarkable for their beauty, and make them swallow mercury till they are suffocated; in this way they preserve, they say, the freshness and ruddiness of their countenance, so as to make them appear still alive.

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

About Mongol princes (reported)

Keep in mind

Huc did not see this; 'they say' marks it as hearsay, and it echoes older Chinese tomb legends.

Strange talesChina1368

The mooncake massacre of 1368

Huc tells the Chinese tradition behind the Moon festival: in about 1368 the Chinese rose against the Mongol dynasty, passing the signal in letters hidden in the festival cakes, and killed the Mongol soldiers billeted in their homes. When the priests told this to a Mongol family, the old man wept and said a day of vengeance would come. The massacre story is a legend, not established history.

The signal was given by a letter concealed in the cakes which, as we have stated, are on that day, mutually interchanged throughout the country. The massacre was effected, and the Tartar army dispersed in the houses of the Chinese, utterly annihilated. This catastrophe put an end to the Mongol domination

Évariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

About Mongols (Yuan dynasty) and Chinese

Keep in mind

A Chinese folk tradition that Huc repeats as history.
