James Baillie Fraser's journey into Khorasan and the Turkmen frontier

A Scottish traveller's 1821 to 1822 journey through Persia to Mashhad and the Turkmen camps of the Gorgan plain, with long appendices on Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv and Khiva gathered from informants.

Full title Narrative of a Journey into Khorasan

James Baillie Fraser

Read the 18 passagesOpen the original book

James Baillie Fraser, a Scottish traveller and artist, crossed Persia in 1821 to 1822 hoping to reach Bukhara and Samarkand. Disguised in Persian dress, he got as far as the shrine city of Mashhad but could not go on, and returned west through the camps of the Goklen and Yomut Turkmens on the Gorgan plain to Astarabad. He describes Turkmen tents, dress, music and customs from his own stay, and retells the Mongol destruction of Nishapur from Persian histories. A long appendix on Merv, Khiva, Bukhara and Samarkand is compiled from informants, including an exiled Bukharan prince living in Mashhad, and from earlier travellers. This corpus copy is the 1825 first edition (London, Longman).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
For Fraser 'Tartary' is the steppe and desert north of Persia ('Oozbeck Tartary' for the lands of Bukhara), and 'Tartars' are the Turkic and Mongol peoples there: the Mongols of Genghis Khan who sacked Nishapur, the Turkmen and Uzbek 'Tartar tribes', and people with 'Tartar features'. He notes that the Mughals (Moghuls) were properly one tribe of Tartars.
Why read it
One of the most exact early descriptions of how a Turkmen felt tent is built, plus first-hand scenes of Turkmen camp life and a clear, sceptical retelling of what the Mongols did to the cities of Khorasan.
Watch out
Fraser is openly hostile to the Turkmens as raiders and slavers. Everything about Bukhara, Samarkand, Khiva and Merv is second-hand, from informants and books; he says so himself.

What it says: 18 passages

CitiesNishapur1221

Nishapur levelled by the Tartars

the Tartars having found means to enter the place after a siege of only three days, spent a day and night in plundering it, and in massacring and reducing to slavery its wretched and defenceless inhabitants; the city was razed, and the ground on which it stood levelled so completely, that it is said a horse might have ridden over the whole site without stumbling

Editorial context

Fraser retells, from Persian histories via Pétis de la Croix, how Genghis Khan's son Tolui took Nishapur after three days, plundered it, killed or enslaved the people, and levelled the site so that a horse could ride over it without stumbling. He openly doubts the historians' death toll of 1,747,000.

James Baillie Fraser, following historians quoted by Pétis de la Croix

CitiesBukhara1220

Bukhara burned: wood gone, brick left standing

as the greater part of its habitations were constructed of wood, its destruction was complete ; the sun, which rose upon its rich and crowded bazars, and thickly inhabited edifices, went down at night upon a waste of smoking ashes, among which, there was not one house standing, except some mosques and public buildings, that being built of brick, remained unburned.

Editorial context

In his history of Bukhara, Fraser writes that Genghis Khan ordered the city burned when he found enemy officers hidden there. Because most houses were of wood, he says, nothing survived except mosques and public buildings of brick. He adds that Genghis's son Ögedei rebuilt the city.

James Baillie Fraser

CitiesSamarkandabout 1812 to 1822

Samarkand: Timur's capital in ruins

This venerable city, often the seat of empire, once the favourite capital of the great Timoor, is now little better than a mass of ruins … In this place may yet be seen the Mausoleum of the Ameer Timoor ; it is a large building with a very lofty dome, which once was covered with gold ; but that was taken off by Shah Muraud.

Editorial context

Relying on the Indian traveller Mir Izzatullah and other reports, Fraser says Samarkand had been almost deserted until Shah Murad of Bukhara repopulated it. It had a mud wall and citadel but stone public buildings, and Timur's tomb still stood under a tall dome whose gold covering Shah Murad had removed.

James Baillie Fraser, drawing on Mir Izzatullah

BuildingsGorgan plain, northeastern Iran1822

How a Turkmen tent is built

The frame is curiously constructed of light wood, disposed in laths of about an inch broad, by three quarters thick, crossing one another diagonally … and pinned at each crossing with thongs of raw hide, so as to be moveable ; … over which is thrown a covering of black numud, leaving in the center a large hole to give vent to the smoke, and light to the dwelling

Editorial context

Fraser says no one had yet described exactly how the 'portable wooden houses' of the Turkmens are made, and does so: a folding lattice of thin laths pinned with rawhide that opens out like a child's toy, curved roof poles, black felt (numud) coverings, a reed screen outside and a broad woven band holding it all together.

James Baillie Fraser

BuildingsBukhara regionabout 1822

Uzbek 'black houses' of felt

The tents, or rather houses, of the Oozbecks resemble those of the Toorkomans, of which a description has already been given, a frame work of thin laths covered with thick, black felts, and they are called karaooees (black houses) or kirghahs. The floors of the rich are spread with carpets and numuds, those of the poor with a blanket or mat of camels' hair

Editorial context

In his appendix on Bukhara, Fraser reports that the tents of the country Uzbeks were like those of the Turkmens: a lath frame under thick black felts, called kara-ui ('black house') or kirgah, with a lattice smoke hole that could be covered with felt when it rained.

James Baillie Fraser

BuildingsBukharaabout 1822

Bukhara's houses of raw brick and timber

The houses in Bockhara are in general two and even three stories high, built of raw brick, and often strengthened by wooden frame work ; those of inferior description are constructed of the same frames of timber, filled up with mud and fragments of brick.

Editorial context

Fraser's informants described Bukhara's houses as two or three storeys of raw (unfired) brick strengthened with timber frames, with poorer houses of timber filled with mud and broken brick. The city's roughly eighty colleges, by contrast, were mostly of stone and lime.

James Baillie Fraser

BuildingsMervabout 1822

At Merv, only the burnt brick survived

there are no ruins to prove the existence of the marble palaces, and baths, and tombs, which have been vaguely attributed to it ; the chief remains are those of a fine large mosque, with a medressa, built by Timoor Shah ; of the mausoleum of Sooltaun Sanjer, and of a fine arsenal, all of which having been constructed of burnt brick, have partly escaped

Editorial context

Fraser doubts the tales of marble palaces at Merv. His informants said the main surviving ruins were a mosque and college credited to Timur, the tomb of the Seljuk sultan Sanjar and an arsenal, which lasted because they were built of fired brick.

James Baillie Fraser

BuildingsGonbad-e Kavus1822

A brick tower among the Turkmen pastures

It has been built of the finest fire-baked bricks, large, square, and about two inches and a half thick, with which also, placed flat, the roof has been covered ; and so well have they stood, that not above one or two of the whole have been yet displaced. The whole is cemented with lime, and of the most excellent masonry

Editorial context

Riding through Goklen Turkmen pastures, Fraser reached the Gonbad-e Kavus, a hollow brick tower on a mound that he guessed at 150 feet high. He admired its fired bricks and lime mortar, and judged it to be of early Islamic ('Arabic') origin, though he could not read its inscription. The nomads grazing around it were not credited with building it.

James Baillie Fraser
See the original page

About Goklen Turkmens (camped around it); builders unknown to Fraser

Keep in mind

Fraser could not read the date. The tower is now known to be the tomb of the Ziyarid ruler Qabus, built around 1006 to 1007.

Daily lifeGorgan plain and Karakum desert1822

Turkmens: no chiefs, no nobles

But they have no governors, chiefs, or nobles among them : and if any one should attempt to arrogate superior consideration to himself, or openly aim at power or authority, it would be the signal for his destruction. … Such an event can only occur, when some individual supereminently endowed with talents and courage, like a Chengiz, or a Timour, arises to force into union substances naturally repellent

Editorial context

Fraser says Turkmen camps of 30 to 200 families followed an elder but had no rulers or nobles, and anyone who tried to seize power would be destroyed. Only a rare figure like Genghis Khan or Timur, he adds, could force such people into a single powerful union.

James Baillie Fraser

Daily lifeGorgan plain1822

A Turkmen singer like an Aeolian harp

the measure was always closed by a single line or chorus that died away in a very sweet and singular cadence, infinitely more agreeable than any music I ever heard in Persia, for the singer did not strain his voice in the way usual in that country, but taught it most curiously to follow the inflections of the tarr, imitating the sound and undulations of the wire

Editorial context

After dinner in a Goklen chief's tent, two musicians played until past midnight. Fraser found the singing far sweeter than Persian music: the singer's voice imitated the vibrating string of the tar, and the audience rocked and shouted with delight.

James Baillie Fraser

Daily lifeGorgan plain1822

Guests seized by the reins

When any stranger (who is not an acknowledged enemy) enters a camp, he is saluted at the first tent he approaches by its inhabitants, who run out, seize his reins, and insist on his alighting, and becoming their guest; even should the tent contain but a single

Editorial context

Turkmen hospitality, Fraser was told, was so insistent that people ran out of the first tent a stranger reached, grabbed his horse and made him their guest; going on to another tent was an insult. In a footnote he warns that his informant made the tribes sound more trustworthy than they were.

James Baillie Fraser, quoting informants

Daily lifeGorgan plain1822

Unveiled women in coin-covered caps

The Toorkoman women are not shut up, or concealed like those of most Mahometan countries, nor do they even wear veils … most of them wear a lofty cap, with a broad crown resembling that sort of soldier’s cap called a shako … The front of this is covered with ornaments of silver or gold, in various shapes ; most frequently gold coins, mohrs or tomauns, strung in rows

Editorial context

Turkmen women did not hide from strangers or wear full veils, only a cloth over the lower face. Fraser describes their tall shako-like caps covered in gold coins, silver bells and set stones, which he thought looked more like horse trappings than jewellery.

James Baillie Fraser

Daily lifeGorgan plain1822

A pole on a mound where a Turkmen died

When one of these Toorkomans dies, they wash the body on the spot where he breathed his last, or as near it as possible; and on that spot they raise a little mound, by digging a circular trench, two or three feet wide, throwing the earth up in the centre; and in this mound they plant a tree, or pole, to mark the place.

Editorial context

Fraser records that when a Turkmen died, the body was washed on the spot and a small mound with a tree or pole was raised there, while the body itself was buried further out on the plain. He saw the plain dotted with these markers.

James Baillie Fraser

Daily lifeBukhara regionabout 1822

Kimmiz and horse flesh among the Uzbeks

it is said they are fond of horse flesh, which they prefer to beef : I have heard this denied ; but I do not know which statement is the true one. There is no doubt of their fondness for the national and intoxicating drink, called kimmiz, prepared from mares’ milk, by shaking it violently in a skin for several hours.

Editorial context

Fraser's informants told him the Uzbeks were fond of kimmiz (kumiss), fermented mare's milk shaken in a skin for hours, and often got drunk on it in season. He also heard, and heard denied, that they preferred horse meat to beef.

James Baillie Fraser

The nameGorgan plain1822

Turkmens with 'Tartar' faces

The Tuckehs have a great deal of the Tartar physiognomy; many of the men were tall, stout and well made, with scanty beards, eyes small, and drawn up at the corners ; high cheek bones, and small flat noses: some on the contrary had handsome features, more resembling those of Europeans than Asiatics.

Editorial context

Fraser sorts the Turkmen tribes by how 'Tartar' they looked: the Teke most, the Goklen less, the Yomut least, with big differences even within one family. It shows 'Tartar' being used as a physical type rather than a nation.

James Baillie Fraser

The nameHerat1825

'Moghuls are a tribe of Tartars'

Moghuls are, in truth, a certain tribe of Tartars, very few of whom could possibly be in Herat as part of its permanent population. Probably Captain Christie inserted this word in his journal somewhat loosely, annexing to it the same meaning which is given to it in Hindostan, where it is often applied to Mussoolmans in general

Editorial context

Correcting an earlier traveller who listed 'Moghuls' among the people of Herat, Fraser explains that Moghuls are properly one tribe of Tartars, and that in India the word was used loosely for Muslims in general because many descended from the Mughal conquerors.

James Baillie Fraser (footnote)

Strange talesNishapur1221 (legend); told 1822

The poet who carried his own head

he drew his sword, and smote off the sheikh’s head ; but no sooner had it rolled to the ground than the body taking it up, ran with it a full mile, to the spot where the tomb now is, and with its own finger for a pen, and its blood for ink, wrote upon a stone the epitaph which has since been engraved upon the tomb.

Editorial context

Local legend held that the Sufi poet Attar was captured when the Mongols sacked Nishapur and later beheaded by his owner. His headless body then picked up its head, ran a mile to the site of his tomb and wrote its own epitaph in blood. The tomb's guardians, Fraser adds, told of villagers who stole lead from it and died.

Local tradition, as retold by James Baillie Fraser

Strange talesGorgan plain1822

A wall against the Tartar tribes

It is said that this rich tract was once separated from the desert beyond it, and protected from the incursions of the Tartar tribes by a wall of great strength, which ran from the mountains to the sea. I believe, however, this great work is attributed to some of the fabulous kings, and is probably fabulous itself.

Editorial context

Riding along a long raised bank near Gorgan, Fraser heard that the rich plain had once been shielded from the Tartar tribes by a mighty wall from the mountains to the sea. He dismissed it as a legend about 'fabulous kings'.

James Baillie Fraser (footnote)
See the original page

About 'Tartar tribes' of the northern desert

Keep in mind

Fraser's doubt was misplaced: archaeologists have since traced a real brick barrier across this plain, the Great Wall of Gorgan, usually dated to the Sasanian period (5th to 6th century), long before any 'Tartars'.
