William Coxe on the Russian conquest of Siberia and the China trade

An English clergyman's 1780 compilation of Russian voyages to Alaska, the Cossack conquest of the Siberian Tatar khanate, and the Russia and China border trade at Kiakhta.

Full title Account of the Russian Discoveries between Asia and America

William Coxe

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

William Coxe (1747-1828), a Cambridge fellow and tutor who travelled in Russia, compiled this book in 1780 from Russian and German sources, above all G. F. Müller's Sammlung Russischer Geschichte, J. E. Fischer's Sibirische Geschichte and P. S. Pallas's travels, which he footnotes. It covers Russian fur-hunting voyages to the Aleutians, then 'The Conquest of Siberia': Yermak's Cossack campaign of 1579 to 1582 against Kuchum Khan of the Siberian Tatars and his capital Sibir. A final part describes Russia and China relations, the Treaty of Nerchinsk and the frontier towns of Kiakhta and Maimachin. Coxe witnessed none of the Siberian events himself.

How they knew
Compiled from others
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Siberian Tatars of Kuchum Khan's khanate on the Irtysh and Tobol rivers, and the other native 'Tartar tribes' of western Siberia; also the Kazan and Astrakhan Tatars conquered by Ivan IV, and, loosely, Mongols, Kalmyks and Buryats (the 'Tartar nations' described by Pallas).
Why read it
A clear eighteenth century English account of how a real Tatar state in Siberia, with a small fortress capital at Sibir, fell to a Cossack band, and how Russia then built Tyumen, Tobolsk and other towns on its lands.
Watch out
Secondhand throughout: Coxe condenses Müller and Fischer, who drew on seventeenth century Siberian chronicles. His language about 'barbarians' and 'idolatrous' subjects reflects his time and sources, and the Russian conquest is told from the conquerors' side.

CitiesTobolsk region, Siberiaabout 1578

Kuchum Khan's realm and his fortress of Sibir

Siberia was at that time partly divided among a number of separate princes; and partly inhabited by the various tribes of independent Tartars. Of the former Kutchum Chan was the most powerful Sovereign … His principal residence was at Sibir, a small fortress upon the river Irtish

Editorial context

On the eve of the Cossack invasion, Coxe says, Siberia was split between several princes and independent 'Tartar' tribes. The strongest ruler, Kuchum Khan, held the lands from the Irtysh and Ob to the Tobol and Tura, with his residence at Sibir, a small fortress on the Irtysh. The next page adds that it stood near today's Tobolsk and that some of its ruins could still be seen.

William Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller)

Yermak enters an empty capital

delay to Sibir, the residence of the Tartar princes … But the news of the late defeat had diffused universal consternation, and Sibir was entirely deserted … he himself soon after made his triumphant entry, and seated himself upon the throne without the least opposition.

Editorial context

After routing Kuchum's army on the Irtysh, Yermak marched on Sibir, 'the residence of the Tartar princes', expecting a hard fight. Instead the news of the defeat had emptied it; he rode in and sat on the Khan's throne unopposed, and neighbouring peoples came in to swear allegiance and pay tribute.

William Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller and J. E. Fischer)

CitiesSiberialate 16th to 17th centuries

New Russian towns on Tatar land

The Russians pushed their conquest far and wide: wherever they appeared, the Tartars were either reduced or exterminated. New towns were built and colonies were planted on all sides. Before a century had well elapsed, all that vast tract of country now called Siberia … was annexed to the Russian dominions.

Editorial context

After Yermak's death the Russians returned, built the fort of Tyumen and then the fortresses of Tobolsk, Tara and others (previous page). Coxe sums up the following century bluntly: the Tartars were subdued or wiped out, and new towns and colonies went up everywhere. In his account the Siberian towns were new Russian foundations, not inherited 'Tartar' cities.

William Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller)

CitiesOrkhon and Tuul rivers, Mongoliaabout 1690s to 1710s

A market at the Mongol camp

private merchants continued as before to carry on a separate trade with the Chinese, not only at Pekin, but also at the head quarters of the Mongols. The camp of these roving Tartars was generally to be found near the conflux of the Orchon and Tola, between the Southern frontiers of Siberia and the Mongol desert. A kind of annual fair

Editorial context

Besides the official caravans to Beijing, Russian merchants traded privately at the headquarters of the Mongols, a moving camp usually pitched where the Orkhon and Tuul rivers meet. An annual fair grew up there, which on the next page becomes a scene of drunken Russian 'riot and confusion' that angered the Kangxi emperor.

William Coxe (compiling Müller and Pallas)

CitiesKyakhta (Altanbulag, Mongolia)1770s

Maimachin: a wooden frontier town guarded by Mongols

Maimatschin has no other fortification than a wooden wall, and a small ditch of about three feet broad; the latter was dug in the year 1756, during the war between the Chinese and the Calmucs … over each of these gates there is a wooden guard-house for the Chinese garrison, which consists of Mongols in tattered clothes, and armed with clubs.

Editorial context

Following Pallas, Coxe describes the Chinese trading town of Maimachin facing Russian Kiakhta, with frontier posts inscribed in Russian and Manchu. Its only defence was a wooden wall and a small ditch dug in 1756 during the Qing wars with the Kalmyks (Dzungars), and its gates were watched by a garrison of Mongols in tattered clothes armed with clubs.

William Coxe (from P. S. Pallas's travels)

Daily lifeWestern Siberiaabout 1570s

A Khan who spread Islam by force

He had newly acquired a large part of his territories by conquest; and had, in a great measure, alienated the affections of his idolatrous subjects by the intolerant zeal, with which he introduced and disseminated the Mahometan religion.

Editorial context

Coxe gives one reason Kuchum's khanate was fragile: much of it was newly conquered, and he had alienated his 'idolatrous' (shamanist) subjects by pushing Islam on them with intolerant zeal. It is a useful reminder that 'Tartar' Siberia was religiously mixed, with Islam still spreading in the sixteenth century.

William Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller)

Daily lifeTransbaikalia, Siberia1770s

Raw rhubarb stalks that grip the throat

The stalks of the leaves are eaten raw by the Tartars: they produce upon most persons, who are unaccustomed to them, a kind of spasmodic contraction of the throat, which goes off in a few hours; it returns however at every meal, until they become habituated to this kind of diet.

Editorial context

In an appendix on rhubarb, Coxe (credited to Pallas) says the Tartars ate the leaf stalks of wild rhubarb raw, and that outsiders who tried them got a spasm of the throat at every meal until they grew used to it. A small, odd detail of steppe diet.

William Coxe (from information supplied by P. S. Pallas)

The nameWestern Siberiamid 16th century

A Khan descended from Genghis

Some Tartar tribes were indeed laid under contribution; and a chief, whose name was Yediger, consented to pay an annual tribute of a thousand sables. But this expedition was not productive of any lasting effects; for soon afterwards Yediger was defeated, and taken prisoner by Kutchum Chan; the latter was a lineal descendant of the celebrated Zinghis Chan

Editorial context

Coxe describes Muscovy's early raids into western Siberia, when 'Tartar tribes' paid tribute, and one chief, Yediger, promised a thousand sables a year. Yediger was then overthrown by Kuchum Khan, whom Coxe identifies as a direct descendant of Genghis Khan who had only recently founded his realm there.

William Coxe (compiling G. F. Müller)

The nameTobolsk region, Siberia16th century

The Tatars called it Isker, not Sibir

Several authors have supposed the name of Siberia to derive its origin from this fortress, soon after it was first taken by the Russians under Yermac. But this opinion is advanced without sufficient foundation; for the name of Sibir was unknown to the Tartars, that fort being by them called Isker.

Editorial context

In a footnote Coxe rejects the popular idea that Siberia was named after the captured fortress of Sibir, pointing out that the Tatars themselves called the fort Isker. He suggests the regional name came to the Russians earlier, through the Permian and Zyrian peoples.

William Coxe (footnote, following Müller)

The nameSiberia and Mongolia1776 to 1780

'Tartar nations': Kalmyks, Mongols and Buryats

No writer has placed the religion and history of the Tartar nations in a more explicit point of view than Mr. Pallas … concerning the Tartars, who inhabit parts of Siberia, and the territory which lies between that country and the Chinese wall … as they are divided into Calmucs, Mongols, and Burats.

Editorial context

In a note Coxe praises Pallas's new work on the 'Tartars' living between Siberia and the Great Wall, whom Pallas divides into Kalmyks, Mongols and Buryats, split in religion between shamanism and the Dalai Lama's Buddhism. It shows 'Tartar' used in 1780 as an umbrella term for Mongolic peoples.

William Coxe (note on P. S. Pallas)

Strange talesWestern Siberia (Irtysh)1584 onward

Miracles at the enemy's grave

Many miraculous stories were soon spread abroad, and met with implicit belief. The touch of his body was supposed to have been an instantaneous cure for all disorders; and even his clothes and arms were said to be endowed with the same efficacy. A flame of fire was represented as sometimes hovering about his tomb

Editorial context

After Yermak drowned in the Irtysh, his body was first abused on Kuchum's orders, but then, Coxe says, the Tatars came to revere him: they buried him with pagan rites, and stories spread that touching his body cured all illness and that a flame hovered over his tomb. A footnote adds that a Kalmyk prince, Allai, was said to have been cured by drinking earth from the grave.

William Coxe (compiling Müller's Siberian history)
