Johann Schiltberger's thirty years as a captive among Turks and Tartars

A Bavarian squire captured at Nicopolis in 1396 serves the Ottoman sultan, Timur and a prince of the Golden Horde, and comes home in 1427 to tell the tale.

Catalogue title The Bondage and Travels of Johann Schiltberger

Johann Schiltberger

Johann Schiltberger (born c. 1380) was taken prisoner as a teenager at the battle of Nicopolis in 1396. He served Sultan Bayezid, then Timur after 1402, then Timur's heirs, and around 1405 to 1410 rode with the Golden Horde prince Chekre and the warlord Edigü, even into 'Ibissibur' (Siberia). He escaped in 1427 and dictated his book from memory. It mixes what he saw in the Horde with lists of places and hearsay marvels. This corpus copy is J. Buchan Telfer's English translation (Hakluyt Society, 1879) of the Heidelberg manuscript, with long notes by Philipp Bruun and Karl Neumann.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Golden Horde ('Great Tartary') on the Volga and steppe, and the Turkmen and Mongol nomads of Anatolia ('White Tartary'). He also treats Timur's realm in Central Asia as part of the same world.
Why read it
One of very few medieval Europeans who actually lived and campaigned inside the Golden Horde: he describes khans raised on white felt, mare's milk, meat cured under the saddle, a woman warlord and the Horde's cities.
Watch out
Written from memory decades later by a man with little education; names are garbled and dates slip. His Siberian wild men and his notion that some Tartars were Christians (probably Buddhist Uighurs) are hearsay or misunderstanding. Many notes are by the 19th-century editors.

Sarai, Astrakhan, Bolgar and Kaffa: the cities of Great Tartary

Schiltberger lists the towns he visited 'that belong to Great Tartary': Urgench, Haitzicherchen (Hajji Tarkhan, old Astrakhan), Sarai 'the residence of the kings of the Tartars', Bolgar, Ibissibur, Tana on the Don, Solkhat in Crimea, and Kaffa with its double walls and its Italian, Greek, Armenian and Jewish quarters. So the Horde of his day had real cities as well as camps.

Item, a city called Haitzicherchen, which is a large city, and in a good country. Another city called Sarei ; there, is the residence of the kings of the Tartars. There is also a city called Bolar, in which are different kinds of beasts.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

CitiesKaffa (Feodosia), Crimeac. 1405-1410

Kaffa: a walled port of many nations

The fullest city description in his Tartary chapter is of Kaffa, the Genoese port in Crimea. He counts six thousand houses inside the inner wall and eleven thousand within the outer walls, three bishops (Roman, Greek and Armenian), Muslim and Jewish places of worship, and four thousand houses in the suburbs.

A city called Kaffa, which lies by the Black Sea, and is surrounded by two walls. Within one wall are six thousand houses, in which are Italians, Greeks, and Armenians; it is a chief city on the Black Sea

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

About Genoese colony within Golden Horde territory

Keep in mind

Kaffa was built and run by the Genoese, not by the Tartars, though it lay in lands they claimed.

A hundred thousand huts, and no wood or stone

Describing the Horde under the kingmaker Edigü, Schiltberger says the people roam winter and summer with families and herds, and a hundred thousand huts go up whenever the khan camps. Elsewhere (p.50) he says Tartary stretches three months' journey with no wood or stones, only grass and shrubs.

The vassals in Tartary wander about in winter and summer, with their wives and children, and their cattle, and when the king encamps, there must be erected one hundred thousand huts.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

Daily lifeGolden Horde steppe (lower Volga)c. 1405-1410

Raising a khan on white felt

Schiltberger says the Great Tartars sow only millet, eat no bread and drink no wine, but drink mare's and camel's milk and eat horse and camel meat. The khan and his people live in the open fields winter and summer. A new khan is set on a white felt and lifted up three times, carried round the tent, and given a golden sword.

It is also to be noted, that when they choose a king, they take him and seat him on white felt, and raise him in it three times. Then they lift him up and carry him round the tent, and seat him on a throne, and put a golden sword in his hand.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

Daily lifeGolden Horde steppec. 1405-1410

Meat cured under the saddle

Here Schiltberger insists he saw it himself: on long journeys Tartars bleed their horses and cook and drink the blood, and they slice meat, salt it and ride on it under the saddle until it dries and softens. Every morning the khan drinks mare's milk from a golden cup on an empty stomach.

I myself have seen them bleed [their horses] and drink the blood after they have cooked it. … I have also seen when they are long on a journey, that they take a piece of flesh, cut it into slices, place it under the saddle, and ride on it, and eat it when they were hungry ; but they salt it first

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879; bracketed words are the translator's)

Daily lifeIbissibur (western Siberia)c. 1405

A funeral like a wedding

He describes the funeral of a young unmarried man: the body in his best clothes on a bier under a canopy, musicians and young people in finery, feasting and merrymaking at the grave while the parents weep, so that it becomes the wedding he never had. He adds firmly: 'All this I have seen.'

It is also the custom in the country, that when a young man, who has not had a wife, dies, he is dressed in his best clothes, and players carry him, and he is laid on a bier, and a canopy is placed over him.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

The Lord's Prayer in Tartar

Schiltberger ends his book with the Lord's Prayer written phonetically in Armenian and in 'Tartar', a rare early European sample of a Turkic language as a captive heard it. It begins 'Atha wysum' ('our father').

Roughly: 'Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name, thy kingdom come...' in a Turkic language.

The nameGolden Hordec. 1405-1410

Three tribes of the Great Tartars

Schiltberger names three tribes among the 'Great Tartars': Kayat, Inbu and Mugal. The editors identify these as the Kerait, the Uighurs and the Mongols, so he correctly grasped that the Horde was a mix of peoples with the Mongols as one part. He also notes that Russia paid tribute to the Tartar king.

Item, the kingdom of Rewschen, which is tributary to the Tartar king. It is to be noted, that there are three tribes amongst the Great Tartars. One is called Kayat, the other Inbu, the third Mugal.

Heard itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

The name1879 (editor's note)

Why 'Tartar' and not 'Tatar'?

In a long note, the editor explains why he writes 'Tatar' rather than the manuscript's 'Tartar': Russians, Armenians and the peoples themselves say Tatar, and the extra 'r' came in Western Europe by association with Tartarus (he cites the story about St Louis). He also records the theory that the name goes back to a Chinese name for the Mongols.

The Russian word Tatarui, or Tatars, says Ralston … modified in Western Europe by a reference to Tartarus into Tartars, is now generally applied by Russian writers to what used to be the Turkish subjects of the Mongol Empire.

Read itEditor's note (J. Buchan Telfer, 1879), citing W. R. S. Ralston

Strange talesIbissibur (western Siberia)c. 1405

Hairy wild people in Siberia

Riding with Edigü and Prince Chekre into 'Ibissibur' (this is one of the earliest uses of the name Siberia), Schiltberger heard of a mountain range that ends in a desert at the end of the earth, and of hairy wild people who eat grass. A captured wild man and woman were sent to Edigü. He also describes sled dogs as big as donkeys.

On the same mountain there are savages, who are not like other people, and they live there. They are covered all over the body with hair, except the hands and face, and run about like other wild beasts in the mountain, and also eat leaves and grass, and any thing they can find.

Heard itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)

Strange talesGolden Horde steppec. 1405-1410

A widow warlord with four thousand women archers

A powerful Tartar widow named Sadur Melik came to Edigü with four thousand women and girls to avenge her husband. Her women rode to war with sword and bow like men. When the kinsman who had killed her husband was captured, she beheaded him herself. Schiltberger says he was there.

He was brought before the woman ; she ordered him to kneel, and drew her sword, and cut off his head at one blow, and said : " Now am I revenged." I was present there, and I also saw this.

Saw itJohann Schiltberger (translated by J. Buchan Telfer, 1879)
