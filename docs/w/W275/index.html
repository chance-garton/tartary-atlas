Written source · German · German

Weber's 'Russia Transformed', with reports on Siberia, the Kalmyks and the Tatars

A Hanoverian diplomat's account of Peter the Great's Russia, 1714 to 1720, stuffed with other people's reports from Siberia, Astrakhan, China and Bukhara.

Catalogue title Russia Transformed (Das veränderte Russland): the present constitution of church and state, the army, with hitherto unknown reports in a journal running to 1720, with an accurate map and copperplates; 3 parts

Friedrich Christian Weber

Friedrich Christian Weber was the Hanoverian resident at St Petersburg from 1714 to 1719. His 'Das veränderte Russland' (part 1, Frankfurt 1721; parts 2 and 3, 1739 and 1740) describes Peter the Great's court, army and reforms from his own observation, and prints many documents and reports he collected: Lorenz Lange's 1715 journey to Peking with a description of China, informants' news from Astrakhan about Kalmyks and Tatars, anonymous descriptions of the Kalmyks and Mongols, the journal of a painter-traveller who went down the Volga in 1703 (evidently Cornelis de Bruyn), and in part 2 an account of Bukhara. This corpus has the 1721 first edition, a 1744 reissue and parts 2 and 3.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
'Tartar' covers the Muslim Tatars of Kazan and Astrakhan, the Siberian and Baraba Tatars, the Kuban Nogais, the Crimean Tatars, the Mongols (and the medieval Mongol empire), the Manchu rulers of China, and in 'Tartarian Bukhara' the oasis peoples of Central Asia.
Why read it
A cabinet of first-hand snapshots from the edges of Peter's empire: pit houses on the Baraba steppe, Kalmyk tents like hooped petticoats, a Tatar camp outside Astrakhan, a burial mound said to be piled up with helmets, and frozen dumplings in Little Bukhara.
Watch out
Much of the material is second-hand, often from unnamed authors, and Weber rarely says where he got it; the historical notes on the Mongols repeat medieval books. Quotes are from the 1721 edition or its 1744 reissue (same text), and the OCR is often damaged.

CitiesBeijing1716

Beijing's 'Tartar cities' and a wall built by earlier Tartars

The description of China printed with Lange's journal explains that Beijing is three cities in one: an outer suburb now called the Chinese city, and two 'Tartar cities', inside which lies the emperor's city with its yellow or red wall. The outer earthen rampart, it says, was built about 500 years earlier by the western Tartars when they ruled China for some thirty years, and is now almost fallen.

The city of Peking is threefold: first the suburb, which is nowadays called the Chinese city; the other two are the cities of the Tartars, and within these the emperor's city, or the city with the yellow or red wall, in which the emperor's palace stands. The suburb has a rampart, which the Chinese call Tuchin or the earthen wall, made about 500 years ago by the Tartars living to the west

BuildingsBaraba steppe1716

Pit houses of the Baraba Tatars

Crossing the Baraba steppe between Tara and Tomsk, Lorenz Lange's party met the Baraba Tatars, whom he calls heathens living wretchedly. Their winter dwellings were dug into the ground, raised about an ell above it with stakes and roofed with straw; inside each stood a small wooden idol in human shape, dressed in rags, called Shaitan, to whom they promised a cap or collar in return for good hunting.

In winter this wilderness is inhabited by a horde of Tartars whom the Russians call Baraba Tartars … In their dwellings, which are dug into the earth and raised about an ell above it with stakes and covered with straw, they keep a small idol carved from wood in the shape of a man.

BuildingsAstrakhan1716

Kalmyk leather huts shaped like a hooped petticoat

Weber's informant Poussett described the Kalmyk caravan that came to trade at Astrakhan each year, grazing its way along the Volga. They carried leather huts called 'Kubiten' (kibitkas), which looked like a whalebone hooped petticoat; they raised them on poles, cooked inside, and closed the top hole when the fire was out to keep warm at night. Each morning the houses were loaded back onto the pack animals.

They had a kind of leather huts, which looked like a whalebone petticoat and were called Kubiten; these they set up with poles, made fire and food inside, and when the fire had burned out stopped up the hole at the top, and so kept warm at night. In the morning they loaded their houses onto the pack animals and moved on.

Original wording

Des Morgens lüden sie ihre Häuser auf die Last-Thiere und jagten weiter fort.

BuildingsNear Samara on the Volga1703

A khan's burial mound heaped up with helmets and shields

Sailing down the Volga below Simbirsk in May 1703, the traveller saw a high, almost treeless round hill called Sariolkiergan. Russians told him it was the tomb of a Tatar king named Mamon (Mamai), who had come up the Volga with seventy Tatar princes to conquer Russia, and that his soldiers had carried the earth for the mound in their helmets and shields. It is a local legend about a kurgan, reported as hearsay.

The Russians told us that there lay the tomb of a Tartar king or emperor named Mamon, who had come up the Volga with seventy other Tartar princes, intending to make himself master of all Russia. But because this prince died here, the soldiers he had brought in great numbers raised this tomb in his honour, and carried the earth to this hill in their helmets and shields.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Russen berichteten uns, daß daselbst das Begräbniß eines Tartarischen Königs oder Kaysers, Namens Mamon, wäre, welcher mit siebenzig andern Tartarischen Prinzen die Wolga herauf gekommen, in der Absicht, sich von ganz Rußland zu bemeistern. Weil aber dieser Prinz hier gestorben, hätten seine in grosser Menge mitgebrachte Soldaten ihm zu Ehren dieses Grabmahl errichtet, und in ihren Sturm-Hauben und Schildern die Erde zu diesem Berge angetragen.

BuildingsBaraba steppelate 1600s

'They have no town': low log houses and summer tents

An anonymous description of the Kalmyks printed by Weber says the Baraba people, whom it calls a kind of Kalmyk paying tribute both to the tsar and to the Zunghar khan, live like the Siberian Tatars in low wooden houses built close to the ground, with a smoke hole closed once the fire burns down. They have no town or fixed dwelling: in summer light huts and tents, in winter their warm wooden houses. It adds that they grow oats, barley and buckwheat but spit out rye bread.

Otherwise this nation is a wicked and warlike people, and lives like the Siberian Tartars in wooden houses built quite low on the ground … They have no town or other fixed dwelling, but live in summer in lightly pitched huts and tents, which they know how to strike and set up very quickly. In winter, however, they move into their warm wooden dwellings.

Working translation

Original wording

Sonsten ist diese Nation ein böses und streitbar Volk, wohnet wie die Siberischen Tartarn in hölzernen Häusern, welche ganz niedrig auf die Erde gezimmert … Sie haben keine Stadt noch andere beständige Wohnung, sondern wohnen des Sommers in leicht aufgeschlagenen Hütten und Gezelten, die sie gar hurtig wissen abzubrechen und aufzuschlagen. Des Winters aber ziehen sie in ihre warme hölzerne Wohnungen.

Daily lifeAstrakhan1703

A Tatar camp near Astrakhan, 'as Abraham must have lived'

Visiting the Tatar camp a short mile from Astrakhan, the traveller found families camped apart in tents shaped like parrot cages, very clean and well furnished. They lived from their herds, burned dried cow dung stacked around the tents like peat, and, he thought, lived as contentedly in their tents as Europeans in palaces. He sketched a finely dressed young woman and compared their life to the Arabs and the biblical patriarchs.

Their tents are like parrot cages, only not so high in proportion, very clean and provided with good furniture… They get their warmth from cow dung, which they dry and prepare as we do our peat, and lay in heaps around their tents. Their way of life agrees fairly well with the Arabs'

Ihre Lebens-Art kommt mit der Arabischen ziemlich überein

Daily lifeAstrakhan1716

'You are both right': a Sunni and a Shia before the judge

Poussett told Weber that a Persian and a Muslim Tatar at Astrakhan had quarrelled over religion and taken their complaint to the Russian commandant. When he learned that the Tatar had called the prophet Ali, and the Persian the prophet Muhammad, a cheat and a vagabond, the commandant shrugged and ruled that he could punish neither, since both were right. Poussett also praised the Astrakhan Tatars' orderly life and hospitality.

that the Tartar had abused the prophet Ali, and the Persian in turn the prophet Muhammad, as a cheat and vagabond, the commandant shrugs his shoulders and pronounces judgement: Gentlemen, I can punish neither the one nor the other of you, for you are both right.

Working translation

Original wording

daß der Tartar den Prophet Aly, und hinwiederum der Persianer den Propheten Mahomet, vor einen Betrüger und Landstreicher gescholten, so ziehet der Commendant die Schulter, und spricht das Urthel: Ihr Herren, ich kan weder einen noch den andern von euch strafen, denn ihr habt alle beyde recht.

Daily lifeLittle Bukhara (Yarkand and Kashgar)1730s

Frozen half-moon dumplings for winter journeys

Part 2 of Weber's work prints an account 'Of Tartarian Bukhara' by an author who says he had been in Little Bukhara himself. He notes that the men's dress differs little from the Tartar, that they eat with their fingers and wooden spoons, drink tea with milk, salt and butter, and fill half-moon-shaped pastries with chopped meat, which they freeze and carry in a sack on long winter journeys to boil into soup.

They fill a kind of pastry, shaped like a half moon, with it. On long journeys, especially in winter, they take a good supply of these pastries with them and carry them in a sack. When this food has first frozen thoroughly in the air, it is cooked in boiling water, and a fairly good soup is made of it.

Working translation

Original wording

Sie füllen mit denselben eine Art Pasteten, die wie ein halber Mond gestaltet sind. Auf weiten Reisen, insonderheit in Winter, nehmen sie einen guten Vorraht von diesen Pasteten mit, und tragen sie bey sich in einem Sakke. Wenn nun diese Speise vorher an der Luft rechtschaffen durchgefroren ist, so wird sie in einem siedenden Wasser gekocht, und eine ziemlich gute Suppe davon gemacht.

The nameMongolia1721

Four Mongol peoples, one of them called Tartars after a river

After the Astrakhan Tatars, the text turns to the history of the Mongols, retelling medieval reports: the khan's seal read 'One God in heaven and one Khan on earth', and Batu's army counted 160,000 Tartars and 440,000 Christians and others. It then lists four Mongol peoples, including the 'Saniongals' or water Mongols (the Su-Mongals of Carpini), who were also called Tartars after the river Tartar.

This Mongol land lies to the east and is inhabited by four different peoples, namely the … the Saniongals or water Mongols, who are also called Tartars after the [river] Tartar; the Merkits … These four peoples were fairly similar in language and way of life, but each had its own chief.

Working translation

Original wording

Dieses Mongalen Land lieget nach Osten und ist … vier unterschiedene Völcker bewohnet; nemlich die … die Saniongals oder See-Mongalen, welche auch nach den … Tartar, Tartern genennet werden; die Merkates … Diese vier Völcker waren in der Sprache und Lebens-Art … ziemlich ähnlich, hatten aber jede ihr eigen Ober-Haupt.

Strange talesKazan1703

Fireproof cloth from a Tatar prince

The traveller reports a quarrel between two Tatar princes of the Kazan region, father and son, which almost came to battle over a woman until Prince Golitsyn, governor of Kazan and Astrakhan, made peace. In thanks the Tatar prince gave Golitsyn a large piece of cloth that would not burn, said to be still made in Cathay between China and Bukhara. The traveller, who had himself once brought asbestos stone from Cyprus, received a piece.

whereupon the Tartar prince gave this arbitrator, among other things, a large piece of incombustible linen, of which Mr Poppe and I in turn received some from him. He told me that it was still actually made in Cathay, between China and Bukhara.

Strange talesOnon River (Mongolia and Transbaikalia)late 1600s

Gog and Magog on the Onon River

An anonymous description of the Mongols printed next in Weber sets out their lands, from Lake Khövsgöl east to the sand desert, to the Argun, and north-west to the rivers Onon and Sikoi, a country which, it says, has been said since ancient times to be inhabited by Gog and Magog. The same description goes on to the lamas, the Dalai Lama and whether he could be Prester John.

further to the land of Argun, and from there again north-west to the rivers Onon and Sikoi, which, as has been said from ancient times, is inhabited by Gog and Magog.

Card wording

mixed: firsthand observation in St Petersburg 1714-1719 combined with documents and reports obtained from others, including material on Siberia and the Kalmyks and an appended account of Peter's Persian and Caspian projects

Cautions
  • Published anonymously; Weber's authorship is standard attribution. Parts 2-3 appeared after his death and contain compiled documents. Multiple BSB copies and a 1744 reissue exist on IA.
  • English translation The Present State of Russia (1722-1723) exists but is not in the English pass.
  • Further copies: archive.org/details/10691836bsb

Why it matters

Standard German contemporary source on Petrine Russia; part 1 appends reports on Siberia and the Swedish captives, and Weber transmits early news of the Kalmyks, Bukhara expeditions (Bekovich-Cherkassky) and the Caspian; used by Strahlenberg's readers as context.
