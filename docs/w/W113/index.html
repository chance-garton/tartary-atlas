The Daoist master Changchun's journey to Chinggis Khan

A disciple's diary of the Daoist sage Qiu Chuji (Changchun) crossing Mongolia and Central Asia to Chinggis Khan's camp in Afghanistan and back, 1221 to 1224.

Full title Travels of Changchun to the West

Li Zhichang (Li Chi-chang); Qiu Chuji / Changchun

Read the 18 passagesOpen the original bookRide the route

In 1219 Chinggis Khan summoned the aged Daoist master Qiu Chuji, called Changchun, from Shandong. Changchun left Beijing in 1220, wintered north-west of it, and in 1221 crossed eastern and central Mongolia, the Altai, Dzungaria and the Tian Shan, wintered in Samarkand, met the Khan in the Hindu Kush in 1222 and was back in Beijing early in 1224. His disciple Li Zhichang kept the diary, published as the Si yu ki (Xiyouji) with a preface dated 1228. Emil Bretschneider translated it with long notes, leaving out most poems and Daoist talk, in Mediaeval Researches from Eastern Asiatic Sources, vol. 1 (London, 1888); the corpus copy is a later Kegan Paul reissue, catalogued as 1910. He also prints Chinggis's letter of invitation and Changchun's reply.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The word Tartar never appears in the translated diary. The diarist calls Chinggis's people Mongols, uses Hui-ho for Muslims, and names the Naiman, Khitan and Uighurs separately; only Bretschneider's notes, quoting Carpini and Rubruck, speak of 'Tartari'.
Why read it
The fullest thirteenth-century eyewitness picture of Mongol camps, dress and manners from inside the new empire, and a clear view of who actually built and lived in the towns along the Mongol roads: Chinese artisans, Muslim townspeople, Khitan ruins and Mongol princes' road and bridge crews.
Watch out
The narrator is the disciple Li Zhichang, not Changchun, and he writes as an admiring Chinese Daoist. Bretschneider abridged the text and notes that parts of the itinerary are out of order; the invitation letter is a separate document, not part of the diary.

CitiesEastern Mongolia (Khalkha river or Kerulun area)April 1221

A prince's camp of thousands of carts

The party reached the camp of the prince Otchigin, Chinggis's youngest brother, who governed Mongolia while the Khan was at war in the west. A wedding was under way and Mongol chiefs had come bringing mare's milk, and the diarist counted the prince's town of carts and felt tents in the thousands, set out in long rows. The prince's first question to the sage, like his brother's a year later, was how to prolong life.

At that time the ice was only beginning to melt, and the first green was seen on the ground. There was a wedding being celebrated, and many Mongol chiefs had arrived with mare's milk. We saw several thousands of black carts and felt tents standing in long rows. On the 7th (April 29) the master was presented to the prince, who asked him about the means of prolonging life.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

CitiesUpper Selenga basin, MongoliaJuly 1221

The Empress's moving palace

Beyond the Khangai forests the party reached the ordo of one of Chinggis Khan's principal wives, where more than a thousand carts and tents stood along a river and the captive Jin and Tangut princesses sent gifts of millet and silver. Flour was dear because 'Western barbarians' brought it by camel from beyond the Tian Shan, and the diarist notes that the Mongol word (wu-li-do, ordo) is rendered in Chinese as hing kung, which Bretschneider translates 'movable palace'. He found the carts and tents grander than anything the ancient Xiongnu rulers (shan yu) had possessed.

On the southern bank of the river there were more than a thousand carts and tents. … At this place eighty kin of flour cost fifty liang; for the flour is brought from beyond the Yin shan, a distance of more than 2000 li, on camels, by the Western barbarians. … Carts and tents had all a magnificent appearance, such as was unknown to the ancient Shan yü.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Mongol court of one of Chinggis Khan's chief wives, with captive Jin and Tangut princesses

Keep in mind

Bretschneider (after Potanin) places this ordo on the Eter, a headwater of the Selenga; the site is a guess. He reckons a liang of silver at about six shillings.

CitiesWestern Mongolia (between the Khangai and the Altai)August 1221

Chen-hai's town of Chinese craftsmen

North of the snowy mountains lay T'ien Chen-hai ba-la-ho-sun (balgasun, the diarist explains, means city), a granary town nicknamed 'the head of the magazines', where Chinese artisans and two concubines of the Jin emperor Zhangzong turned out to greet the sage, and Changchun was surprised to see farming in the desert. Bretschneider's note, from the Yuan shi, says Chinggis had founded a military colony and built a city here, settling over 300 families from Western Asia to weave gold brocade and 300 from Kaifeng to make woollen cloth, under Chen-hai, the chancellor Chinqai whom Carpini later met. Before moving on, the sage's followers built a monastery there in under a month, 'the rich with money and the workmen with their labour' (p.61).

a number of Chinamen, artisans and workmen, who lived there … came in procession to see the master. They were all ravished; met him with exclamations of joy, bowed before him, and accompanied him with variegated umbrellas and fragrant flowers. There were also two concubines of the Kin emperor, Chang tsung, and the mother of a Chinese princess, who met the master with exclamations and tears.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Chinese artisans and captives under Chinqai, Chinggis's official

Keep in mind

The numbers of brocade weavers and cloth makers come from Bretschneider's note citing the Yuan shi, not from the diary. The town's site is uncertain.

CitiesSamarkandWinter 1221-1222

Samarkand after the conquest: a quarter of its people left

Samarkand, taken by the Mongols in 1220, was laid out along canals that carried river water down every street to every house. The diarist says it had held over 100,000 families before the Sultan of Khwarazm fell but only a quarter remained, that Muslim landowners could not dispose of their fields and had to manage them jointly with Khitan, Chinese and Tangut (Ho si) men, and that Chinese workmen lived everywhere. Changchun lodged in the Sultan's new palace on a mound about a hundred feet high, which the governor (probably Yelu Chucai, says Bretschneider) had left because robbers made that quarter unsafe.

the city of Samarkand had a population of more than a hundred thousand families, but after the occupation only the fourth part remained behind. Most of the fields and gardens belong to the Mohammedans, but they are not allowed to dispose of them. They are obliged to manage their properties in conjunction with K'i-tan (i.e., Karakhitai), Chinese, and men from Ho si. Chinese workmen are living everywhere.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Muslim townspeople under Mongol rule, with Khitan, Chinese and Tangut overseers

Keep in mind

The population figures are what the diarist was told. Bretschneider notes it cannot be proved that the city Chinggis took in 1220 stood on the site of modern Samarkand rather than the Afrasiab ruins to the north.

CitiesBalkh, AfghanistanSeptember 1222

Balkh, empty but for barking dogs

Travelling by night after crossing the Amu Darya on the way to Chinggis's camp, the party passed Balkh, a very large city whose people had recently revolted and fled, and all they heard inside was the barking of dogs. Bretschneider's note adds, from Rashid al-Din, that Balkh fell to Chinggis in 1221 and most of its people were massacred.

travelling during the night, we passed Ban-li a very large city, the inhabitants of which had revolted not long ago, and fled. We heard the barking of the dogs in the city.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Inhabitants of Balkh under Mongol rule

CitiesAlmaliq (near Yining/Kuldja), XinjiangOctober 1221

Almaliq: a Muslim ruler beside a Mongol governor

At Almaliq in the Ili valley the local Muslim ruler and the Mongol governor (darughachi, Bretschneider explains) rode out together to greet the sage, a glimpse of how the Mongols ruled a town through its old prince. The city was named for its apples (alma in Turkic). The travellers bought cloth woven from 'vegetable wool', cotton, then little known in China, saw people carrying water in jars on their heads, and heard the locals praise the Chinese as 'very able men' when they saw a Chinese well-bucket (pp.70-71).

The ruler of the realm of P'u-su-man came out of the city, together with the Mongol ta-lu-hua-chi, to meet the master. We stopped at a fruit orchard west (of the city). The people here call a fruit a-li-ma; and as the place is famed for its fruits, the city received the above name.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Muslim ruler of the P'u-su-man; Mongol darughachi

Keep in mind

P'u-su-man (Muslims) and darughachi are Bretschneider's readings of the Chinese transcriptions; he places Almaliq near modern Kuldja.

BuildingsEastern Mongolia (south of the Kerulun)March to April 1221

Black carts and white tents

A few days beyond the fishing lake of Yu-rh-li, the travellers entered true nomad country, where every dwelling was a black cart or a white tent and the people moved with the water and grass. Bretschneider's note takes the carts to be the felt-covered 'tents on wheels' described by Marco Polo and Rubruck, a practice he says had died out in Mongolia by his own day. Farther on, beside the Kerulun, the diarist adds that the Mongols used the tall riverside willows 'for making their tents' (p.51).

All around we saw habitations, consisting of black carts and white tents. The people here are nomades, and change their abode according to the prevalence of water and pasture. No tree could be seen, and we met only yellow clouds (of dust) and decayed grass.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

BuildingsCentral Mongolia (Tuul or Orkhon valley)June 1221

A ruined Khitan city with lettered tiles

After crossing a river into a grassy plain ringed by mountains, the travellers found the ruins of an old city on both sides, where the line of the streets could still be traced, and were told it had been built (the sentence runs over from p.53) by the Khitan. They picked up a tile inscribed in Khitan script, and the diarist guessed the town was founded by Khitan warriors who had moved north rather than submit to the Jin. In the Mongol heartland, then, the ruin was credited to an earlier, known people, not to the Mongols. Bretschneider quotes Palladius that Khitan forts and towns lay in ruins on the Tuul, the Kerulun and in Manchuria.

by the K'i-tan. We found, indeed, on the soil a tile with letters of the K'i-tan. This was probably a city founded by those K'i-tan warriors who emigrated, unwilling to submit to the new dynasty.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Khitan (Liao dynasty) builders, in Mongol-ruled land

Keep in mind

The exact site is disputed (Tuul, Orkhon or Kharukha river). The 'new dynasty' is the Jin, who overthrew the Liao in 1125.

BuildingsTalki pass (Sayram Lake to the Ili valley), XinjiangBuilt 1219; crossed October 1221

Forty-eight bridges built by Chagatai

Below the mountain lake that Changchun named the Lake of Heaven (Sayram Lake), the road ran down a gorge of birch and pine, and the diarist credits Chinggis's second son Chagatai with cutting it through the rocks and building forty-eight timber bridges wide enough for two carts abreast. Earlier the cart road over the Altai had likewise been 'planned and constructed' by the third son, Ogedei, where the escort hauled the carts up with ropes and dragged the wheels going down (p.62). Here the Mongols appear as builders of roads and wooden bridges for their armies.

The second prince, who was with the emperor at the time he went to the west (in 1219), first made a way through these mountains, cut through the rocks, and built forty-eight bridges with the wood cut on the mountains. The bridges are so wide that two carts can pass side by side.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Mongol army under Chagatai, Chinggis Khan's second son

Keep in mind

'(in 1219)' is Bretschneider's insertion. The diarist saw the bridges but was told who built them, and he does not say whose labour the prince used.

BuildingsTalas valley (Kazakhstan-Kyrgyzstan border)November 1221

A city of red stone and mounds like the Big Dipper

Riding west along the mountains beyond the old Karakhitai lands, the travellers saw a city built of red stone and the traces of an ancient military camp. To the west stood great grave mounds arranged like the stars of the Big Dipper, and they went on over a stone bridge. The diarist names no builders and does not say whether the city was lived in.

After seven or eight days' journey, the mountains suddenly turned to the south. We saw a city built of red stones, and there were the traces of an ancient military encampment. To the west we saw great grave-mounds, which resembled the tou sing (the constellation of Ursa major).

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Unknown builders, on the Mongol road west

The site is uncertain: Schuyler took the red city for Talas (Taraz), but Bretschneider rejects his evidence. The diarist does not say who built the city or raised the mounds.

Daily lifeCentral Mongolia (upper Kerulun to the Tuul)June 1221

Furs, milk and the tall bark headdress

West of the Kerulun bend the country was crowded with carts and tents, and the people raised cattle, hunted, wore furs and skins and lived on milk and meat. Married women wore a two-foot headdress of tree bark covered in wool or, among the rich, red silk, with a long 'tail' called yu-yu that looked like a goose or duck, and they entered tents backwards with heads bent so as not to knock it (next page). Bretschneider matches it with the tall headdress described by Carpini and by Rubruck, who calls it bocca. On the same page, Mongols who had waited a year for the sage brought him millet and were amazed by the jujubes he gave them, a fruit they had never seen.

They dress in furs and skins, and live upon milk and flesh-meat. The men and unmarried young women plait their hair so that it hangs down over their ears. The married women put on their heads a thing made of the bark of trees, two feet high, which they sometimes cover with woollen cloth, or, as the rich used to do, with red silk stuff.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Daily lifeCentral MongoliaJune 1221

No writing, notched sticks and a kept word

The diarist was struck that the Mongols he met used no writing: they settled business by word of mouth and recorded contracts by cutting marks on wood. He praises them as never disobeying orders and never breaking their word, people who had 'preserved the customs of the early ages'. The first sentence finishes his description of the married women's tall headdress.

Therefore, when entering a tent, they are accustomed to go backward, inclining their heads. These people (the Mongols) have no writing. They settle their matters by verbal convention, and when they enter into contracts they cut certain marks on wood. They are never disobedient to orders, and never break their word. They have preserved the customs of the early ages.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Mongols

Keep in mind

'(the Mongols)' is Bretschneider's insertion. His note (after Palladius) says the Uighur script reached the Mongols a few years later, but most modern historians date its adoption at Chinggis's court to about 1204, so the diarist is describing ordinary herders rather than the Khan's chancery.

Daily lifeHindu Kush (near Kabul), AfghanistanMay 1222

'Have you a medicine of immortality?'

At his first audience, in the Khan's camp south of the Hindu Kush, Chinggis welcomed the sage, fed him and asked outright for an elixir of immortality; Changchun answered that there were ways to preserve life but no medicine for immortality, and the Khan praised his honesty. At a later audience the sage firmly refused to drink the tung lo set before him, which Bretschneider explains as kumiss or other fermented milk (p.94). His teaching was translated into Mongol by the councillor A-hai and written down in both Chinese and Mongol (p.95).

After this he asked: "Sainted man, you have come from a great distance. Have you a medicine of immortality?" The master replied: "There are means for preserving life, but no medicines for immortality." Chinghiz lauded him for his sincerity and candour. By imperial order two tents were pitched for the master east of the emperor's tents.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

Daily lifeWestern Mongolia (the Naiman ordo)May 1219

Chinggis Khan's self-portrait: one coat, one food

Bretschneider prints the Khan's letter inviting Changchun, dated 15 May 1219 and preserved in the fourteenth-century Chinese miscellany Cho keng lu. In it Chinggis sets himself against luxurious China: he lives in the 'northern wilderness', eats what his herdsmen eat and wears the same rags. Bretschneider stresses that the Khan could not write, so the polished classical Chinese was put down by a secretary, very likely Yelu Chucai.

Heaven has abandoned China owing to its haughtiness and extravagant luxury. But I, living in the northern wilderness, have not inordinate passions. I hate luxury and exercise moderation. I have only one coat and one food. I eat the same food and am dressed in the same tatters as my humble herdsmen.

Letter issued in Chinggis Khan's name (1219), written in classical Chinese by a secretary and preserved in the Cho keng lu (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Chinggis Khan

Keep in mind

A self-portrait in a formal letter meant to persuade; the frugal ruler is also a Chinese ideal, and the wording is a secretary's. The letter is not part of Li Zhichang's diary.

The nameWestern Mongolia (the Naiman ordo)May 1219

'Since our shan yu': the Mongols and the Xiongnu past

In the same letter Chinggis lists the peoples who have acknowledged him (the Song, the Hui-ho, the Tangut Xia and 'the barbarians in the west') and says no empire so vast has been seen 'since the remote time of our shan yu'. Bretschneider explains that shan yu was the title of the khans of the ancient Xiongnu of Mongolia from the second century BC, so the letter sets the Mongols in a line of steppe rulers reaching back to Han times. The diarist makes the same comparison at the Empress's ordo, whose carts and tents outshone anything the ancient shan yu had (p.58).

The Sung to the south, the Hui-ho to the north, the Hia … to the east, and the barbarians in the west, all together have acknowledged my supremacy. It seems to me that since the remote time of our shan yü such a vast empire has not been seen.

Letter issued in Chinggis Khan's name (1219), written in classical Chinese by a secretary and preserved in the Cho keng lu (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Mongols (Chinggis Khan's empire)

Keep in mind

A boast: the Song never submitted to Chinggis, and Bretschneider notes the directions are muddled. 'Our shan yu' may be the Chinese secretary's classical vocabulary rather than Mongol tradition.

Strange talesDzungarian desert, XinjiangSeptember 1221

The Field of White Bones

South of the Altai, Chen-hai warned that the road crossed the 'field of white bones', a plain strewn with black stones where a whole army had once died of exhaustion and where the Naiman army had lately been destroyed (by Chinggis, Bretschneider notes). Anyone who crossed by day in the sun would die with his horse, so the party marched all night, past more than a hundred great sand hills that 'seemed to swim like big ships in the midst of the waves', and reached a small Hui-ho (Muslim) town the next morning.

Chen-hai replied: "That is an old battlefield … a field of death. At one time a whole army perished there by exhaustion; no one escaped. A short time ago, at the same place, the army of the Nai-man was destroyed. Whoever crosses the desert in the daytime and in clear weather (i.e., exposed to the sun) will die from fatigue, and his horse also."

Chen-hai (Chinqai), as reported by Li Zhichang (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Naiman army; the travellers' Mongol escort

Keep in mind

The story of the older lost army is Chen-hai's hearsay; the diarist saw only the desert.

Strange talesAltai foothills, western MongoliaAugust 1221

Goblins of the Altai

As the party headed for the Altai, one of Chen-hai's servants said these mountains were notorious for goblins and that one had pulled him by the hair, and Chen-hai added that a Naiman khan passing through had been bewitched and forced to offer the goblin a sacrifice. Changchun said nothing. Crossing the desert beyond, the escort rubbed blood on their horses' heads against goblins, and the sage smiled that goblins flee from a good man (p.64); earlier the diarist had seen traces of sacrifices to mountain spirits on a peak, and many grave mounds below (p.58).

One of the servants of Chen-hai reported that these mountains had a bad fame for their goblins, and that once a goblin pulled him by the hair. Chen-hai narrated further, that once it occurred also to the Khan of the Naiman … to be charmed by a goblin, and that he was obliged to offer a sacrifice to him. The master did not make any remark on these tales.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)

About Chen-hai's men; the Naiman khan

Strange talesKerulun river, Mongolia23 May 1221

A total eclipse on the Kerulun

At noon on 23 May 1221, on the south bank of the Kerulun, the sun went dark enough for the stars to show. Bretschneider notes that the astronomer Alexander Wylie calculated a solar eclipse on exactly that day and that Chinese astronomical annals record it on the same date, 'a stamp of authenticity to the whole narrative'. In Samarkand that winter a local astronomer told Changchun it had reached six-tenths there, and the sage concluded that the same eclipse looks different from place to place (p.79).

On the 1st of the fifth month (May 23), at noon, an eclipse of the sun happened, while we were on the southern bank of the river. It was so dark that the stars could be seen, but soon it brightened up again.

Li Zhichang, disciple of Changchun, in the Si yu ki (translated by E. Bretschneider, 1888)
