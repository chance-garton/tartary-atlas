Captain Jesse's Crimea: Tatar villages and Bakhchysarai in 1839 to 1840

A British half-pay officer's travel notes on Russia, Circassia and the Crimea, 1839 to 1840, with nights in Tatar houses and a visit to the Khans' old capital.

Catalogue title Notes of a Half-Pay in Search of Health

William Jesse

Captain William Jesse, a British army officer on half pay, travelled in southern Russia, the Caucasus coast and the Crimea in 1839 and 1840, officially for his health, and published these notes in 1841 (this is Volume I). He is openly critical of Russia and says in his preface that most of his facts came 'under my own observation'. His Crimean chapters cover the south coast estates of the Russian nobility, Tatar villages on the mountainsides, the cave fortress of Mangup, Bakhchysarai with its palace, fountain and a Tatar wedding, the Karaite town of Chufut-Kale, and nights as a guest in Tatar homes, with Colonel Upton's son as interpreter.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Crimean Tatars: Muslim villagers and townspeople of the peninsula, conquered by Russia in 1783, and their former Khans; also Tatar pilgrims returning from Mecca on a Black Sea steamer.
Why read it
Short, concrete snapshots of Tatar building and village life around 1840: clay-roofed houses set into hillsides, two-storey tiled houses in the valleys, a wooden minaret used to call up post horses, the Khans' fountain inscription, and a host's bitter remark about the conquerors.
Watch out
Jesse is sharply anti-Russian and at times crudely disdainful of 'dirty' peoples, including some Tatars; he relied on an interpreter. Only Volume I is in the corpus, and the OCR of this Indian scan is uneven.

The Tatars' own old capital

Jesse calls Bakhchysarai one of the few Crimean towns inhabited only by Tatars, who still loved 'the ancient capital of their race'. He lists its trades: leather cushions, slippers, whips and saddles, black lambskin caps, and the shaggy woven cloaks called bourkas.

This is one of the few towns in the Crimea inhabited solely by Tartars, who still cling with affection and reverence to the ancient capital of their race … a few employ themselves in the manufacture of leather cushions, slippers, whips, saddles, &c., fur caps of the black lamb-skin, and cloaks called bourkas.

BuildingsBakhchysaraiinscription 18th century; copied 1839 to 1840

The Fountain of Tears inscription: 'never such a fountain'

Jesse copies the translated inscription on the Selsabil fountain in the palace vestibule (the famous 'Fountain of Tears'). It credits Khan Krym Giray with finding the spring and giving water to his people, and boasts that Damascus and Baghdad never saw its equal. He notes that the palace's rooms were small and the furniture mean and modern.

The town of Bagtché-Serai rejoices in the beneficent solicitude of the luminous Crim-Gheri-Khan: it was he, who with generous hand quenched the thirst of his countrymen … If there exists such another fountain, let it present itself. The towns of Scham and Bagdad have seen many things, but never such a fountain.

BuildingsVillage near Chufut-Kale, Crimea1839 to 1840

A night in a Tatar house

Lodged by a village head man, Jesse describes a room with a divan running all round it, no tables or chairs, a three-legged low table for meals, and a fireplace so big that six or eight people could stand inside the chimney. Each house had high wattle fences and at least half a dozen dogs; supper was kaimak (thick cream), milk and eggs.

A divan, covered with coarse cotton, ran round the apartment, and the fire-place, in the centre of the side near the door, was large enough to admit six or eight persons within the chimney. Of course, there were no tables or chairs; a low wooden stool or plateau on three legs, as in Turkey, being used for meals

BuildingsUzenbash, Crimea1839 to 1840

Two-storey tiled houses in the valleys

Jesse notices that Tatar houses in the inland valleys were better built than the flat-roofed coastal ones: still low, but often two storeys with an outside gallery, raised tiled roofs and carved wooden ceilings. At the village inn of Uzenbash a Tatar barber gave him an expert shave.

The houses in these valleys are much better built than those on the coast, and though very low, have frequently two stories, with a gallery running round them on the outside; the roofs also are raised and tiled, and the ceilings sometimes ornamented with carving in wood.

BuildingsMangup-Kale, Crimea1839 to 1840

Mangup: a Greek, Genoese and Karaite ruin, with a Tatar fountain

Jesse credits the cliff-top ruined town of Mangup to others: first Greek, then a Genoese fortress, then home to Karaite Jews. The Tatars appear in the village at the foot, where women hurried to veil themselves, and in a roadside fountain whose inscription said a 'benevolent Tartar' had built it as an act of charity.

The town was once in the hands of the Greeks, it became afterwards a Genoese fortress, and was subsequently the residence of the Karaite Jews … The inscription informed us that it was erected as an act of charity by some benevolent Tartar.

Daily lifeBaidar, Crimea1839 to 1840

The head man calls the horses from the minaret

Tatar villages had to supply post horses to travellers in turn. Jesse describes the village head man (bashi) climbing the wooden minaret in the middle of the village to shout out the names of those whose horses were due, with replies coming back from around the village.

On arriving at a station, the Bashi, or head man, was not always in the way; when found, he immediately ascended the wooden minaret in the centre of the village, and commenced calling a roster of those whose horses were next for duty, a reply was soon elicited from one corner or another

Daily lifeVillage near Chufut-Kale, Crimea1839 to 1840

'Our conquerors, Sir, have left us very poor'

In the morning Jesse's Tatar host was surprised to be paid, explaining that most of his guests were Russians travelling on duty, who did not pay. His short reply about the conquerors is a rare recorded Tatar voice on life under Russian rule, a generation after annexation.

Our host appeared rather astonished at being remunerated, in the morning, and said that the generality of his visitors were always on duty; adding, “Our conquerors, Sir, have left us very poor.”

Daily lifeBakhchysarai1839 to 1840

Woken by a Tatar bride's procession

At dawn in Bakhchysarai Jesse was woken by drums, gongs and bagpipes accompanying a Tatar bride in a curtained carriage drawn by two ponies, lit by torch-bearers. The same page gives his unimpressed verdict on the Khans' mausoleum and the palace's 'diminutive apartments'.

the concert was in honour of a Tartar bride, whose equipage, drawn by two ponies, was surrounded by curtains and torch-bearers, lighting her to the hymeneal couch, in a manner truly classical … The mausoleum of the Khans is a wretched edifice after that of the Sultans at Constantinople

Daily lifeBlack Sea (voyage to Odessa)1839

Hajj pilgrims on a Black Sea steamer

On the steamer to Odessa's quarantine, Jesse shared the deck with forty passengers including Tatars returning from the pilgrimage to Mecca. He sneers at their lack of washing but notes that they prostrated themselves constantly towards the Kaaba.

The Tartars were returning from a pilgrimage which they had made to Mecca; and though Mohammedans, did any thing but observe the ablutions recommended by their religion; but they unceasingly prostrated themselves in the direction of the Caaba.

Strange talesKerch1839 to 1840

The 'Golden Hill' of Kerch

In the Kerch museum of ancient Greek finds, Jesse reports a Tatar tradition that centuries earlier about forty poods (some 1,440 pounds) of gold had been taken from one of the nearby burial mounds, which they therefore called the Golden Hill. He calls the story 'no doubt fabulous'.

The Tartars have a tradition, that upwards of forty pouds, nearly one thousand four hundred and forty pounds English, of this metal, in ornaments and coin, were, several hundred years ago, taken from one of the tumuli in the neighbourhood; and they call it in consequence the Golden Hill. This is, no doubt, fabulous

Keep in mind

The mounds were ancient Greek and Bosporan burials, not Tatar; the tradition is about finding treasure, not building.
