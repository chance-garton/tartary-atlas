Julius Klaproth's travels in the Caucasus and Georgia

A German orientalist's scientific expedition through the North Caucasus and Georgia in 1807 to 1808, with a close study of Golden Horde ruins.

Full title Travels in the Caucasus and Georgia

Julius von Klaproth

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

Julius Klaproth (1783-1835), a linguist in Russian academic service, was sent by the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences to study the peoples, languages and history of the Caucasus in 1807 to 1808. His book (German 1812 to 1814) appeared in English in 1814, the edition in this corpus. Besides Circassians, Ossetes and Georgians, it covers the Nogai, Kumyk, Karachay and Balkar 'Tartars', and gives the fullest early account of the ruined town of Madzhar on the Kuma, combining Gmelin's 1772 description, Klaproth's own excavation of a vault, dated gravestones, coins of the Golden Horde khans and a Nogai-language history.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Turkic-speaking peoples of the North Caucasus and steppe (Nogais, Kumyks, and the Karachay and Balkar 'mountain Tartars'), and the Kipchak Tatars of the Golden Horde and their Genghisid khans; he notes that Batu's army had few actual Mongols.
Why read it
Shows how an early 19th-century scholar used bricks, inscriptions and coins to prove that a ruined stone town on the steppe was a 14th-century Golden Horde city, and how settlers and officials then quarried it away.
Watch out
Long passages quote earlier travellers (Gmelin, Güldenstädt, Gerber) or informants rather than Klaproth's own eyes, and he is combative toward rivals like Reineggs. The OCR mangles the Arabic-script inscriptions and some names.

CitiesMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1780s to 1807

Who pulled down Madzhar

Editorial contextWhen Klaproth arrived only two burial chapels were left. He explains that nearby colonists tore the ruins down for bricks, and that Count Paul Potemkin had most of what remained demolished to build the fortress-town of Yekaterinograd; peasants carried off the rest, and inscribed stones ended up in their yards.

The destruction of these remains of antiquity has been occasioned chiefly by the settlement of several colonies, which have established themselves in this neighbourhood, and have pulled them down for the sake of the serviceable bricks. Their total demolition, however, is to be ascribed more particularly to Count Paul Ssergeitsch Potemkin, who ordered the greatest part of the buildings remaining in his time to be taken down

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

CitiesGolden Horde lands (Sarai, Astrakhan, Kazan, Madzhar)text acquired 1807; describing the 14th to 15th centuries

A Tatar history: 'The habitations of the Chans were stone buildings'

Editorial contextAt Mozdok Klaproth bought a history of the Tartars in the Nogai dialect and translates its list of where the khans lived: Sarai, the Akhtuba, Madzhar, and vice-khans at Astrakhan and Kazan. He notes that the word he renders 'stone buildings' is 'Madshar' itself. The Nogais near the ruins also told him it had been the residence of Khan Mamai.

The habitations of the Chans were stone buildings. The residence of one Chan was Urisch, and the residence of another Chan was Churssan… Dshan Beg Chan's residence was at Ack Adil, with the name of Ssaray Aldy… Jaick Chan's was Madshar. There were three vice-chans; at Adshdruchan was the place of abode of Timur Ckutlu-Chan, Borki Chan's habitation was at Ckathatdr, and Sheich Aly Chan's residence at Ckasan.

Anonymous history of the Tartars in the Nogai dialect, as translated by Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

CitiesMadzhary; Sarai (lower Volga)1274 to 1408 (coins)

Coins struck at Sarai, capital of the Golden Horde

Editorial contextKlaproth reads the coins found at Madzhar: nearly all were minted at Sarai or New Sarai, the Golden Horde capitals, under khans from Mengu-Timur to Tokhtamysh and Pulad (1270s to 1408), some with names in Mongol script. One is struck at Astrakhan. The latest coin helps date the town's end to about 1400.

The great Ssulthan Ghajats Eddunia (the prop of the world) Toktégu-chan, the Just… Coined at Ssarai, the capital, in the year 715; that is, A.D. 1315.

Julius Klaproth, transcribing coin legends (English translation, 1814)

BuildingsMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1772

Madzhar's brick buildings, described in 1772

Editorial contextKlaproth quotes the naturalist S. G. Gmelin, who saw the ruins in 1772 when they were best preserved: a great city on a plateau, with fortified mansions at the edge. The bricks were like those Astrakhan Tatars still made; walls were bonded mostly with clay and plastered and whitewashed inside; buildings were square, octagonal or round, up to nine fathoms high and topped by pyramids or domes.

The bricks resemble those still made by the Tartars of Astrachan, that is to say, they are broader and thicker than ours. In the external walls, a mortar composed of lime and sand is used only here and there, the cement generally employed being clay alone… The figure of the buildings yet preserved is square, octagonal, and circular.

Samuel Gottlieb Gmelin (visit of 1772), in Klaproth's extract (English translation, 1814)

BuildingsMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1807 (arguing about the 14th century)

Proof that Madzhar was built by Kipchak Tatars

Editorial contextKlaproth sets out his evidence that Madzhar was a Tatar town: the shape of its buildings and burial chapels, which match a Tatar cemetery near Tbilisi; walls decorated with coloured glazed tiles, a Tatar and Mongol fashion; Arabic gravestones; coins of the Golden Horde; and mentions in Asian histories. He also opened a brick vault himself and found a wooden coffin.

The following facts, however, afford incontestable proofs that Madshar was a town built and inhabited by Ckipdshack Tartars. I. The form of the buildings and sepulchral chapels is characteristic of Southern Asia… The fashion of adorning the walls with tiles, which are glazed on one side with different colours, is also Tartar and Mongol.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

BuildingsLittle Kabarda (North Caucasus)1455 (inscription)

An octagonal stone tomb with a Tatar inscription

Editorial contextNear the rivulet Jaman-Kul, Klaproth describes the oldest monument of Little Kabarda: an octagon of hewn stone about twelve feet high with an arched door, windows and a collapsed vault, ringed by about a hundred earth mounds. Its Tatar inscription names 'Ckoban-Chan son of Berdi-beg' and the Islamic year 860 (1455).

It is an edifice of hewn stone, and round it are about a hundred hillocks of earth called Bugri… The building is an octagon, each of its sides measuring six feet… On the stone inserted over the door is engraved a Tartar inscription in three lines, of which only these words ‘Ckoban-Chan son of Berdi-beg, in the year 860’ (A. D. 1455) are legible.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

Daily lifeBalkaria (North Caucasus)1807

God called Tagri and sacrifices to the prophet Elijah

Editorial contextKlaproth says the ordinary Bassian Tartars had no fixed religion: they called God 'Tagri' rather than Allah, offered lambs, dairy and beer to the prophet Elijah on mountain tops with song and dance, ate pork and kept sacred springs. Like other Tartars they foretold the future from sheep's shoulder-blades cracking in the fire. Only their chiefs had been made Muslim by the Circassians.

The common people have, strictly speaking, no established religion, but worship God, whom they call Tagri and not Allah, as the giver of all good, and the prophet Elijah… who, according to them, frequently appears on the summits of the highest mountains, and to whom they offer sacrifices of lambs, milk, butter, cheese, and beer (ssra), accompanied with singing and dancing.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

Daily lifeMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1346 to 1376

A Muslim gravestone dated 1346

Editorial contextKlaproth read Arabic gravestones at Madzhar: one for 'Sina, son of Mohammed, son of Chalil' of AH 747 (1346-1347), another for a judge of AH 777 (1375-1376), which he carried away. All dated stones he saw belonged to the 8th Islamic century. He notes Pallas missed them because peasants had built them into their yards.

Here is buried the deceased, who needs the mercy of God in eternity, Sina, son of Mohhammed, the son of Chalil..... in the year of the era seven-and-forty and seven hundred.

Julius Klaproth, translating an Arabic gravestone (English translation, 1814)

Daily lifeKarachay (upper Kuban, North Caucasus)1807

Karachay marriage and the 'price of blood'

Editorial contextKlaproth describes the Karachay as usually monogamous and unusually kind to their wives. As among other Tartars, the bride's parents receive a kalym (bride price), here called 'the price of blood'; a rich groom sends the bride a full outfit and she is brought to him at night, followed by three days of feasting and dancing.

In general they take only one wife… so that here, as among the Europeans, the wife is the companion, and not the menial servant, of the husband… Among them, as among the other Tartars, the parents, on the marriage of a daughter, receive a kalim, which is here termed the price of blood.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814)

The nameBalkaria and upper Kuban (North Caucasus)1807 (tradition)

Mountain Tartars who remember a lost capital

Editorial contextKlaproth reports the origin tradition of the Turkic mountain peoples the Georgians called Bassiani: their elders say they once lived on the steppe of the Kuma up to the Don, with a magnificent capital, Ckirck Madshar ('forty stone buildings'), which they identify with the ruins at Madzhar, before being driven into the high mountains.

Their elders report that they were long ago settled in the steppe of the Kuma as far as the Don… Their capital, which is said to have been very magnificent, was named Ckirck Madshar, which in their language signifies the forty stone buildings, or the forty four-wheeled waggons… They assert that the ruins of Madshar, which yet subsist, are the remains of this city.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814), reporting the elders' tradition

The nameRussian Empire1807

A name Tatars called dishonourable

Editorial contextIn the written instructions the Academy gave Klaproth, a scholar remarks that peoples sometimes adopt names their neighbours use: just as the Tartar nations in Russia call themselves 'Tartars' for outsiders, though at home they consider the name dishonourable.

They perhaps assume it for the purpose of accommodating their neighbours, as the Tartar nations in Russia give themselves the name of Tartars, which in their own countries they consider as dishonourable.

Instructions for Klaproth from the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences (section II, probably by A. C. Lehrberg or Ph. Krug), printed in Klaproth's Travels

The nameSarai (lower Volga)1237 to 1240s

Batu's 'Mongol' army had very few Mongols

Editorial contextIn footnotes to his account of Batu's conquest of Russia and sack of Kiev in 1240, Klaproth points out that Batu's army contained very few Mongols and that he ruled as prince of the Kipchak Tartars. The khans of Kipchak called themselves Tartar sultans and lived at Sarai on the Akhtuba; after their empire split, the khans of Kazan, Astrakhan and Crimea kept the title.

Batu-chan had very few Mongols in his army, and had become prince of the Kipdschak Tartars… The chans of Kipdschak styled themselves Tartar sultans, and resided in the city of Sarai or Saratschik on the river Achtuba; an arm of the Wolga, till the division of their extensive dominions.

Julius Klaproth (his own footnotes, English translation, 1814)

Strange talesMadzhary (near Budyonnovsk, Stavropol Krai)1728 to 1807

The myth that Hungarians came from Madzhar

Editorial contextKlaproth traces a legend: in 1728 Gerber saw 'handsome stone houses' at Madzhar and guessed from the name that the Hungarians (Magyars) came from there, a notion copied onto maps. Klaproth calls it absurd: the name is Tatar for stone buildings, and the ruins are Golden Horde.

It is yet called Madshar, an appellation which is given by the Poles and Turks to the Hungarians, and by which the latter commonly distinguish themselves. It is probable that the founders of the kingdom of Hungary were originally from this country. Thus we see that Gerber was the first who broached the absurd opinion that the Hungarians derived their origin from Madshar

Johann Gustav Gerber (1728), quoted by Klaproth, followed by Klaproth's comment (English translation, 1814)

Strange talesKarachay (upper Kuban, North Caucasus)about 1780 (tale); recorded 1807

The long-haired forest goblin who boiled two children

Editorial contextAmong the Karachay's many tales of mountain demons, Klaproth records the Ssalmasti, a long-haired female spirit of the woods. A villager caught one and enslaved it by hiding its cut hair, until his children revealed the hiding place; the spirit threw them into the boiling pot and fled back to the forest.

A malignant spirit in female shape, and having very long hair, which they call in their language Ssalmasti, is reported to reside in a certain wood. About twenty-five years ago one of the inhabitants of the village caught this goblin, carried it home with him, and cut off its hair, which he carefully hid, and by which means he rendered the spirit subservient to him.

Julius Klaproth (English translation, 1814), from an Armenian informant who lived among the Karachay
