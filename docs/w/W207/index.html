Ibn Arabshah's hostile life of Timur, with the Golden Horde from inside

An Arab captive's rhymed-prose biography of Timur (1435 to 1437), with memories of Sarai and the Qipchaq steppe.

Full title Wonders of Destiny in the Vicissitudes of Timur: Arabic editio princeps by Golius (Leiden 1636) and Arabic-Latin edition by Manger (Leeuwarden 1767-1772)

عجائب المقدور في نوائب تيمور

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (1389-1450)

Read the 12 passagesOpen the original book

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (1389 to 1450) was carried off from Damascus to Samarkand as a boy in 1401. He later lived in Transoxiana, Khwarazm, the Golden Horde lands (Sarai, Astrakhan, Crimea) and Edirne before settling in Cairo. His Wonders of Destiny (1435 to 1437) is a savage, witty life of Timur in rhymed Arabic prose, drawing on his own memories and on informants. It includes a memorable description of the Dasht-i Qipchaq and Sarai. The corpus copy is an Arabic print catalogued as 1842, probably the Calcutta edition of 1818 or a reprint.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Tatar (al-Tatar, al-Tatar al-Tagham, 'the rabble Tatars') means the Turkic and Mongol peoples of the Golden Horde steppe, the Dasht of Berke. Timur's own people are the Chaghatay, and Ibn Arabshah also lumps them with the Tatars of the Dasht as followers of Chinggis Khan's law.
Why read it
A rare view from inside the steppe: Sarai as a great Islamic city founded by a Muslim khan, caravans crossing the Dasht by cart, and plain statements that Timur destroyed the Horde's cities and carried Damascus craftsmen off to build Samarkand.
Watch out
Ibn Arabshah hated Timur and wrote in ornate rhymed prose, so his anecdotes are often exaggerated. The corpus OCR is unreadable; every quote was transcribed from the page images.

CitiesPontic-Caspian steppe (Dasht-i Qipchaq)before 1395

The Dasht of Berke: 'a land of the Tatars alone'

the Dasht of Berke. It was a country belonging to the Tatars alone, and teeming with every kind of livestock and with the tribes of the Turks; its borders guarded, its sides well peopled, its reaches wide, its water and air wholesome.

Working translation

Original wording

دشت بركه وكانت بلادا بالتتار خاصه وبانواع المواشي وقبائل الترك غاصه محفوظة الاطراف معمورة الاكناف فسيحة الارجاء صحيحة الماء والهواء

Editorial context

Describing the steppe that Timur coveted, Ibn Arabshah calls it the Dasht of Berke (after the Golden Horde khan) and says it was a country belonging to the Tatars alone, teeming with herds and Turkish tribes, well guarded, well peopled and healthy in water and air. He had lived there himself in the 1410s and 1420s.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

CitiesSarai (Selitrennoye, Astrakhan oblast)founded c.1260s, described c.1430s

Sarai, an Islamic city built by Khan Berke

The throne of the Dasht is Sarai, a city of Islamic building and marvellous structure … Sultan Berke, may God have mercy on him, built it when he became a Muslim, and took it as the seat of kingship and chose it.

Working translation

Original wording

وتحت الدشت سراي وهي مدينة اسلامية البنيان بديعة الاركان … وكان السلطان بركه رحمه الله لما اسلم بناها واتخذها دار الملك واصطفاها

Editorial context

Ibn Arabshah names Sarai as the throne city of the Dasht and calls it 'a city of Islamic building, of marvellous structure'. Khan Berke, he says, built it when he became a Muslim and made it his royal seat. Just before this he laments that today, from Khwarazm to Crimea, nothing stirs of those peoples, and only gazelles and camels are left.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

CitiesSarai (Selitrennoye, Astrakhan oblast)14th century to 1395

Sarai: sixty-three years from building to ruin

So Sarai, through these masters, became a gathering place of learning and a mine of good fortune … Between the building of Sarai and its ruin there were sixty-three years; and it was one of the greatest of cities in layout and the most crowded with people.

Working translation

Original wording

فصارت سراي بواسطة هؤلاء السادات مجمع العلم ومعدن السعادات … وبين بنيان سراي وخرابها من الامكنه ثلاث وستون سنه وكانت من اعظم المدن وضعا واكثرها للخلق جمعا

Editorial context

Ibn Arabshah says Sarai drew so many scholars, men of letters and wits in a short time that no other city could match it, not even the great mosque of Cairo and its villages. He puts the whole life of the city, from its building to its ruin, at sixty-three years, and calls it one of the greatest and most populous of cities.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

CitiesSarai, Saraichuk and Astrakhan (lower Volga and Ural)1395

Timur tears down Sarai, Saraichuk and Astrakhan

… of wealth, captives and goods; his raiding parties reached Azaq, and he demolished Sarai, Saraichuk and Hajji Tarkhan and those regions.

Working translation

Original wording

من الاموال والاسرى والمتاع ووصلت طراشته الى ازاق وهدم سراي وسرايجوق وحاجي ترخان وتلك الآفاق

Editorial context

After defeating Toqtamish, Timur's raiders reached Azaq (Azov), and he destroyed Sarai, Saraichuk and Hajji Tarkhan (Astrakhan) and the surrounding lands. The line is short, but it records in plain words that the Golden Horde's cities were destroyed by Timur, not lost to any natural catastrophe.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

BuildingsDamascus to Samarkand1401

The craftsmen of Damascus marched off to Samarkand

He took from Damascus the men of learning and the craftsmen, everyone skilled and outstanding in any art: weavers and tailors, stone-cutters and carpenters, cap-makers and farriers, tent-makers, painters and bow-makers, and falconers; in short, the people of every craft whatever … He divided these groups among the heads of the army and ordered them to deliver them to Samarkand.

Working translation

Original wording

واخذ من دمشق ارباب الفضل واهل الصنائع وكل ماهر في فن من الفنون بارع من النساجين والخياطين والحجارين والنجارين والاقباعية والبياطرة والخيميه والنقاشين والقواسين والبازداريه وفي الجملة اهل اي فن كان … وفرق هؤلاء الطوائف على رؤوس الجند وامرهم ان يوصلوهم الى سمرقند

Editorial context

When Timur took Damascus in 1401, Ibn Arabshah says he carried off its learned men and craftsmen, 'everyone skilled in any art': weavers, tailors, stone-cutters, carpenters, cap-makers, farriers, tent-makers, painters and decorators, bow-makers and falconers. They were shared out among the army commanders, who were ordered to deliver them to Samarkand. Ibn Arabshah, then a boy, was himself taken from Damascus to Samarkand.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

BuildingsSamarkandc.1399 to 1404

A marble mosque for Samarkand, and a builder dragged to death

… and that blocks of solid marble be cut for it; and he entrusted the work to a man called Muhammad Jald … When he returned from his journey and inspected what had been done in his absence, he went to the mosque to look at it; the moment his eye fell on it, he gave orders about Muhammad Jald, and they threw him on his face, tied his feet, and kept dragging him on his face until they had cut him to pieces.

Working translation

Original wording

وان يقطع له احجار من المرمر الصلد وفوض امره الى رجل يقال له محمد جلد … فلما آب من سفرته وتفقد ما حدث في غيبته توجه الى الجامع لينظر اليه فبمجرد ما وقع نظره عليه امر بمحمد جلد فالقوه على وجهه وربطوا رجليه ولا زالوا يجرونه وعلى وجهه يسحبونه حتى بضعوه

Editorial context

Timur had seen a mosque of white marble in India and wanted one like it in Samarkand. Ibn Arabshah says he ordered solid marble to be quarried and put a man named Muhammad Jald in charge, who raised four minarets and outdid the master builders. When Timur came back from campaign and looked at the mosque, he had Muhammad Jald thrown on his face, his feet tied, and dragged until he was torn to pieces.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

Daily lifeKhwarazm to Crimeabefore 1395

Caravans on carts with no need for provisions

Caravans used to leave Khwarazm and travel by cart, safe, without doubt or fear … The caravan carried no provisions and no fodder, and took no companion along, because of the multitude of the peoples and the abundance of security, food and drink among the retinues; they set out only from a tribe and halted only with someone who honoured his guest.

Working translation

Original wording

وكانت القوافل تخرج من خوارزم وتسير بالعجل وهم آمنون من غير ريب ولا وجل … فكانت القافلة لا تحمل زادا ولا عليقا ولا يصحبون معهم رفيقا وذلك لكثرة الامم ووفور الامن والماكل والمشرب من الحشم فلا يصدرون الا عن قبيله ولا ينزلون الا عند من يكرم نزيله

Editorial context

Ibn Arabshah says caravans used to set out from Khwarazm and travel by cart (ajal) across the steppe to Crimea, a journey of about three months, without fear. They carried no food or fodder and took no guide, because the steppe was so thick with peoples and so full of security and food that they only ever stopped at some tribe, and only halted with people who honoured their guests.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

Daily lifeTransoxiana, the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestanc.1400

Chinggis Khan's law set above Islam

He held to the rules of Chinggis Khan, which are like the branches of jurisprudence in the Islamic faith … And so do all the Chaghatay and the people of the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestan, that rabble: all of them put the rules of the accursed Chinggis Khan ahead of the rules of Islam.

Working translation

Original wording

وكان معتقدا للقواعد الجنكيزخانيه وهي كفروع الفقه من الملة الاسلاميه … وكذلك كل الجغتاي واهل الدشت والخطا وتركستان اولئك الطغام كلهم يمشون قواعد الملعون جنكيزخان على قواعد الاسلام

Editorial context

Ibn Arabshah says Timur spoke Persian, Turkish and Mongol but no Arabic, and held to the 'Chinggis Khan rules' (the yasa), treating them like branches of Islamic law. So, he says, did all the Chaghatay, the people of the Dasht, Cathay and Turkestan, whom he calls rabble. For this reason, he adds, leading jurists declared Timur an unbeliever.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

Daily lifeAnatolia1400 to 1402

'Where would the rabble Tatars learn the sword?'

As for Toqtamish Khan, most of his army betrayed him; and where would the rabble Tatars get striking with the keen, cutting sword? They have nothing but the showering of arrows, unlike the lions of Rum.

Working translation

Original wording

واما توقتاميش خان فان غالب عسكره خان ومن اين للتتار الطغام الضرب بالبتار الحسام وما لهم سوى رشق السهام بخلاف ضراغم الاروام

Editorial context

Ibn Arabshah gives the Ottoman sultan Bayezid a furious reply to Timur's letter. Bayezid scorns the idea that he is like 'the Tatars of the Dasht' and says Toqtamish's army was mostly treacherous: the rabble Tatars, he says, have no skill with the sharp sword and nothing but volleys of arrows, unlike the lions of Rum.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah, putting words in the mouth of the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I

The nameTransoxiana (Uzbekistan)14th century

Timur's four tribal vizirs and the Barlas

The Turks have tribes and clans that almost match the tribes of the Arabs, and each of these vizirs was from a tribe … the first of them called Arlat, the tribe of the second called Jalabir [Jalayir], the tribe of the third called Qauchin, and the tribe of the fourth named Barlas; and Timur was the son of the fourth of them.

Working translation

Original wording

والترك لهم قبائل وشعب تكاد توازي قبائل العرب وكل واحد من هؤلاء الوزراء كان من قبيلة … احدهم تسمى ارلات وقبيلة الثاني تدعى جلابر وقبيلة الثالث يقال لها قاوجين وقبيلة الرابع اسمها برلاس وكان تيمور ابن رابعهم

Editorial context

Ibn Arabshah opens his life of Timur by explaining that the Turks, like the Arabs, are divided into tribes. The four chief officers of the Chaghatayid sultan came from four tribes, the Arlat, Jalayir, Qauchin and Barlas, and Timur was the son of the Barlas chief. On the same page he reports a Persian genealogy linking Timur to Chinggis Khan through the female line, and explains Timur's title Gurkan as Mongol for 'son-in-law', since he married royal daughters.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)
See the original page (p. 10)

Strange talesSarai (Selitrennoye, Astrakhan oblast)14th century

The runaway slave who hid in Sarai for ten years

It is told that a slave of one of its notables ran away to a spot off the main road, opened a shop there, traded in it and earned his living; this went on for about ten years, and his master never came upon him there, nor met him, nor saw him, because of the city's size and the multitude of its peoples.

Working translation

Original wording

حكي ان رجلا من اعيانها هرب له رقيق في مكان منحي عن الطريق وفتح له حانوتا يتسبب فيه ويحصل له قوتا واستمر ذلك الحين نحوا من عشر سنين لم يصادفه فيه مولاه ولا اجتمع به ولا رآه وذلك لعظمها وكثرة اممها

Editorial context

To show how vast Sarai was, Ibn Arabshah tells a story: a slave ran away from one of the city's notables, opened a shop in an out-of-the-way street and made his living there for about ten years, and in all that time his master never came across him or saw him.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)

Strange talesOtrar, Kazakhstan (Syr Darya)winter 1404 to 1405

Breath that froze on the beard

When a man breathed out, his breath froze on his moustache and beard … and if he spat phlegm from his mouth, for all its warmth it did not reach the ground except as a frozen pellet.

Working translation

Original wording

وكان الرجل اذا تنفس جمدت انفاسه على سباله ولحيته … وان لفظ من فيه نخامة لا تصل الى الارض مع ما فيها من الحرارة الا وهي بندقة جامدة

Editorial context

On Timur's last march toward China, Ibn Arabshah describes a winter so cold that a man's breath froze on his moustache and beard, and spittle turned to a frozen pellet before it hit the ground. It is a set piece of rhymed prose about the winter in which Timur died at Otrar.

Ahmad ibn Arabshah (Arabic text, print catalogued 1842)
