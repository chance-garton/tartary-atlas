Olearius's embassy down the Volga to Persia

A German scholar-diplomat's eyewitness account of the Tatars of Kazan, Astrakhan, Dagestan and the Terek, 1633 to 1639, with Mandelslo's travels appended.

Catalogue title The Voyages and Travels of the Ambassadors … to Muscovy and Persia

Adam Olearius; appended Johann Albrecht von Mandelslo

Adam Olearius (1599-1671) was secretary to the Duke of Holstein's embassy to Moscow and Persia. In 1636 the embassy sailed down the Volga from Nizhny Novgorod past Kazan to Astrakhan and across the Caspian; in 1638 it returned overland through Dagestan and Terki. Olearius published his account in German in 1647 and an enlarged edition in 1656, mixing his own observations with history from older books. This corpus copy is John Davies's English translation, second edition 1669, which also contains J. A. von Mandelslo's travels to the East Indies, edited and enlarged by others.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Tatars of Kazan, Astrakhan (Nogais) and Crimea. Olearius also calls the Mari (Cheremis), the Kumyks of Dagestan, the Kabardian 'Circassians' of the Terek, the Kalmyks and the Uzbeks 'Tartars'; the appended Mandelslo volume uses it for the Mongols and Manchus who conquered China.
Why read it
A careful observer on the spot: what Tatar towns, huts, food and princes looked like just after the Russian conquest of the Volga, and how Russians policed where Tatars could live and build.
Watch out
Eyewitness scenes sit beside history copied from Herberstein, Miechow and others, so check whether he says 'we saw' or 'they say'. The Mandelslo part is not Olearius and its China history is compiled. OCR of the 1669 type is rough, with long s read as f.

CitiesKazan1636

Kazan: a wooden town with a stone castle Tatars may not enter

Olearius stopped at Kazan on the way down the Volga. The town was large but built of wood, houses, towers and ramparts alike; only the castle was stone. Russians and Tatars lived in the town, but the castle was all Russian and Tatars were forbidden to enter it on pain of death. He goes on to tell how Ivan IV took the city by mining its walls in 1552.

It is of a considerable bigness, but all its Houses, as also the Towers and Rampiers, are of wood. Only the Castle and its Fortifications are of stone … the Inhabitants, who are Muscovites and Tartars: But in the Castle they are all Muscovites, and the Tartars are prohibited entring into it upon pain of death.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

CitiesTarki (Makhachkala), Dagestan1638

Tarku, an unwalled capital of a thousand houses

On the way home the embassy spent five weeks at Tarku (Tarki), capital of Dagestan, whose people Olearius calls Tartars. The town had no walls and about a thousand houses built in the Persian manner, set among rocks full of fossil shells. He found the local lords frightening: over dinner, he says, they talked only of robbing and selling people.

The City is not wall'd. It may contain a thousand houses, built according to the Persian way, but not so well. These Tartars, as also those of … and the others who live more Northerly, are called Kaitacks

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

CitiesKasimov1636

A Tatar boy prince in an old stone castle at Kasimov

At Kasimov on the Oka the embassy met its first Muslims. A twelve-year-old Tatar prince lived with his mother and grandfather in an old stone castle that had once been a fort, under the Tsar's protection. He politely refused baptism until he was old enough to choose, and sent the travellers sheep, mead, beer and butter his mother had churned herself.

This City lies on the right side of the River Occa, in the Principality of Cassinon in Tartary, and there it was we first met with any Mahometans. Not far from the City, in an old stone Castle, which had sometime been a Fort, lived a young Prince of that County

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

BuildingsAstrakhan1636

Reed huts shaped like chicken coops, outside Astrakhan's walls

At Astrakhan the Tatars were not allowed to live inside the Russian-held city or to build walled towns. They lived in round huts of reeds or canes about twelve feet across, with a smoke hole and a cloth flap turned to the wind. In summer they loaded the huts onto carts and followed the pasture, which is why, Olearius says, the Russians called them wanderers.

The Inhabitants of the Country, who are the Tartars of Crim and Nagaia, are not permitted to live within the City … because they are not suffer'd to build Cities, nor to fortify their Towns nor Villages with walls. Their Huts are made either with Bull-rushes or Canes, being about twelve foot diameter, round, and towards the top, like the Coops wherein Chickens are kept in Europe.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

BuildingsMiddle Volga below the Kama mouth, near Tetyushi1636

Ruined cities on the Volga, blamed on Tamerlane

Sailing below the Kama, the embassy passed rich, empty grassland where only the ruins of cities and villages could be seen. Olearius was told they had been destroyed by Tamerlane; nearby hills were named after vanished cities, and on the previous page he notes the site of a former 'City of Tartary' with a saint's tomb still visited by locals. A treasure stone with a joke inscription on its underside is told on the same page.

On the right hand, we had the view of a spacious and very delightful Champain Country, the ground very rich, with a high grass on it; but it was not inhabited, and there could be seen only the ruins of Cities and Villages, which had been heretofore destroy'd by Tamberlane.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

BuildingsTerki, on the Terek river, North Caucasus1638

Circassian houses of earth and boughs, and no walled towns

At Terki the Tsar kept garrisons in all the towns, leaving the Circassians only open towns and villages under their own lords. Their houses were poor ones of earth and branches, plastered inside with clay. Olearius adds that they spoke the common Tartar language and were Muslims in name, though without Koran or priests.

he hath put Garrisons into all the Cities, leaving the Circassian Tartars, for their habitations, but only open Towns and Villages … Their houses are but poor ones, built, for the most part, of earth and boughs of trees, and within, plaister'd over with Clay.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

Daily lifeKazan1636

'The dead are really dead': a Volga 'Tartar' on the afterlife

Olearius lodged at Kazan in the same house as a Cheremis man of about 45, and when talk turned to the resurrection the man laughed. The Cheremis, he writes, lived in poor huts, kept no churches, priests or books, sacrificed horses and hung the skins on poles, and feared spirits rather than a hell. They were Mari, a Finnic-speaking people, but to Olearius they were one more kind of Tartar.

and said to me, Those who are dead are really dead, and will never return again, no more than my Horses and Cows, which are long since rotten. I asked him whether he knew who Created this World, and made Heaven and Earth? his answer was, … The Devil may haply know.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

Daily lifeAstrakhan1636

Dried fish for bread, and mare's milk from a leather bag

The Nogais near Astrakhan lived on their herds, hunting and fishing. Sun-dried fish served them as bread, they ate camel and horse meat, and they prized mare's milk, which they offered the ambassadors from a leather bag. On the same page he describes their fat-tailed sheep, two-humped camels and small tough horses, and the white linen coifs hung with Russian coins worn by the women.

Their ordinary food is fish dry'd in the Sun, which serves them instead of bread. … They eat Camels and Horse-flesh, and drink water and milk. Mares milk they much esteem; with this they treated our Ambassadors, when we gave them a Visit, taking it out of a … Leather-bag, to present it to us.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

Daily lifeTerki, on the Terek river, North Caucasus1636

A princess with an ox-bladder at her neck

The ambassadors visited Princess Bike, mother of the Tartar prince Mussal, in a hall with earthen walls lined with vaulted niches full of silk bedding and Persian carpets, weapons hung on the pillars. She and another lady wore a blown-up ox-bladder wrapped in an embroidered scarf behind the neck: the badge of widowhood. Her servants still bore the scratches of mourning on their faces.

She wore, at her neck behind, an Ox-bladder, blown to the utmost, which was wound about towards her hair, with a rich Scarf embroider'd with Gold and Silk … Behind the Chair stood a Lady, who had also a Bladder at her neck, and we were told afterwards that it was the Badge of Widdowhood.

Saw itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

The nameAstrakhanwritten 1647 (citing Miechow, 1517)

Tartars as runaway 'Indian lords' who came in 1212

Before describing Astrakhan, Olearius turns to the Polish scholar Matthias of Miechow. Miechow said the Tartars must not be confused with the ancient Scythians and Sarmatians, since they were only known from the thirteenth century; a comet of 1211 foretold their coming, and they were 'Indian lords' who had killed their king David and fled west. Olearius then says he will stick to what he saw himself.

a Physician, and Canon of Cracow, who liv'd at the beginning of the last age, says that those, who confound the Tartars with those Nations, which the Antients called Getae, Scythae and Sarmatae, are much mistaken, in as much as the Tartars have not been known, but since the thirteenth Age. … They were certain Indian Lords, who having kill'd their King, David, got away

Read itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

About Tartars in general (Crimean, Kazan, Nogai)

Keep in mind

Book learning passed on second-hand; the 'King David' story echoes medieval Prester John legends.

The nameChinacompiled 1650s to 1660s, about events of 1206 to 1640s

'Tata', because the Chinese cannot say R

The appended East Indies volume includes a history of the Tartar conquests of China. It explains that the Chinese called the Tartars 'Tata' because they could not pronounce R, and then tells how the Mongols ruled China until 1368 and how a new Tartar kingdom, the Manchus of 'Niuche', overran it in the 1640s. The same word covers both conquests.

For in the year 1206, the Tartars, whom the Chineses call'd Tata, because they do not pronounce the Letter R, entred China with a powerful Army, and after a War of seventy two years became Masters of it

About Mongols (1206) and Manchus (1600s), both called Tartars

Strange talesVolga above Samara1636

A Tartar emperor buried under his soldiers' helmets of sand

Near Samara the Russians showed Olearius a sand hill called Sariol Kurgan. They said a Tartar emperor named Momaon, who meant to invade Muscovy with seven kings, died there, and his soldiers heaped sand over him with their helmets and shields until the grave became a hill. A tale about a burial mound, told to him by Russians.

The Muscovites call it Sariol Kurgan, and affirm, that a certain Tartarian Emperour, named Momaon, who had a design to enter Muscovy, together with seven Kings of the same Nation, dy'd in that place, and that his Soldiers, instead of burying him, fill'd their Head-pieces and Bucklers with Sand, and so cover'd the body that it became a Mountain.

Legend or rumourAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

About Tartars (a Golden Horde ruler)

Keep in mind

Russian folk explanation of a natural or man-made hill; 'Momaon' may be a memory of Mamai, the Horde commander of the 1370s, but the source does not say so.

Strange talesMoscow1521 (retold 1647)

The khan's statue in Moscow

Olearius retells the great Crimean raid of 1521: the khan besieged Moscow and forced the Grand Duke to sign himself a tributary. To prove it, the story goes, the khan set up a statue of himself in Moscow before which the Grand Duke had to bow to the ground each time he paid tribute; when the charter was later recovered, the Muscovites smashed the statue.

Mendligeri, to make it appear he was Sovereign Lord of Moscou, caused a Statue of his to be erected in the heart of the City, and would needs oblige the Great Duke, to express his subjection, to smite the ground with his head, before that Statue, as often as he paid Tribute

Read itAdam Olearius (English translation by John Davies, 1669)

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

History retold from older books a century after the event; the khan's name is garbled and the statue detail is not confirmed.
