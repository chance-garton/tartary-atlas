Master Roger's lament for Hungary, destroyed by the Tartars (1241 to 1242)

An Italian cleric's eyewitness account of the Mongol invasion of Hungary, written after a year as their captive.

Catalogue title Sorrowful Lament on the Destruction of the Kingdom of Hungary by the Tartars (Carmen miserabile), in Bongars’s Hungarian historians collection

Master Roger of Torre Maggiore, archdeacon of Várad (Oradea); Jacques Bongars, editor

Master Roger (Rogerius) of Torre Maggiore, an Italian churchman serving as archdeacon of Várad (Oradea) in Hungary, lived through the Mongol invasion of 1241 to 1242. He fled Várad, hid in woods and a fortified island, was taken by the Mongols, worked for a Hungarian who had gone over to them, and escaped as they left Hungary. About 1243 to 1244 he wrote this 'Sorrowful Song' as a letter to a bishop. No manuscript survives; the text comes from the 1488 printings. This corpus has it in the 1600 Bongars collection of Hungarian historians and, used here for clearer print, in the 1885 Budapest edition in Chronica minora.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongol armies of Batu, Kadan and their fellow princes who invaded Hungary in 1241, together with Cumans, Russians and captives serving in their host.
Why read it
It is the closest look we have at Mongol war and Mongol occupation inside a European kingdom: sieges, tricks, the treatment of towns, and the officials they set over captured villages.
Watch out
Roger writes a lament and dwells on atrocities; numbers such as 500,000 invaders are guesses or rumours. He saw some events but reports others from survivors, and the text survives only in late printed editions.

CitiesOradea (Várad)1241

The fall of Várad: stone-throwers, a new wall, a burning cathedral

Roger was a canon at Várad and had just had the damaged castle repaired with a broad new wall; he himself fled to the woods. He describes the castle's ditches and wooden towers on the walls. The Tartars set up seven stone-throwing machines against the new wall and battered it day and night until it fell. The noblewomen who had fled into the cathedral were burned inside it.

For it was fortified with great ditches and wooden towers on the walls, and many knights in mail were there … And at once surrounding the castle, they set up seven machines facing the new wall, and did not stop hurling stones with them day and night until the new wall was completely thrown down. … And because they could not quickly get into the cathedral church, they set fire to it and burned the church, the ladies and whatever was in the church.

CitiesEsztergom1242

Wooden houses burned, stone palaces stormed

Seeing they could not hold the town, the Hungarian, French and Lombard citizens who ran Esztergom burned the suburbs and the many wooden houses right up to the stone palaces, destroyed their cloth and goods, buried their gold and retreated into the palaces. Enraged at losing the plunder, the Tartars stormed the palaces; Roger doubts fifteen people survived. The castle itself, held by a Spanish count with crossbowmen, was not taken.

But the Hungarians, the French and the Lombards, who were as it were the masters of the city, seeing that they could not defend themselves, burned the suburbs and the wooden houses, of which there were many, right up to the palaces of the city. … Afterwards they began to storm the palaces. These were quickly taken, and I do not believe, to tell the truth, that fifteen people of the whole city survived

BuildingsEsztergom1242

A wall of brushwood built by captives at Esztergom

Esztergom, the richest city in Hungary, had fortified itself with ditches, walls and wooden towers. Roger says the Tartars had their captives carry bundles of branches and pile them into a high wall along the edge of the ditch in a single effort, then set thirty stone-throwing machines behind it and pounded the city and its wooden towers day and night. The builders of this siege work, he notes, were the prisoners.

Meanwhile the people of Esztergom had fortified themselves very strongly with ditches, walls and wooden towers. … Then one day the Tartars surrounded the city, and the captives who were with them carried so many bundles of branches that on one side of the city, on the brink of the ditch, they built a high wall of the bundles all at once, and immediately behind that wall they set the thirty machines already mentioned

BuildingsAlba Iulia (Gyulafehérvár)1242

Nothing left at Alba but bones and broken walls

After escaping as the Tartars withdrew, Roger and a companion walked for eight days through empty country, guided by the bell towers of churches, and reached the cathedral town of Alba (Alba Iulia in Transylvania). There they found only the bones and heads of the dead and the broken, undermined walls of churches and palaces, the stones still stained red. It is a rare eyewitness description of a town just ruined by the Mongols.

At last, hardly on the eighth day after leaving the forest, we came to the city of Alba, in which nothing could be found but the bones and heads of the slain, and the walls of churches and palaces broken and undermined, which the great outpouring of Christian blood had stained. And although the earth, drunk with the innocent blood it had soaked up, did not show it, the stones still showed themselves dyed rose red with gore

BuildingsHungary (Great Hungarian Plain)1241

They spared the houses to winter in them

Roger watched Tartar and Cuman raiders return from a night of slaughter in the villages, loaded with spoil. Yet they burned neither grain, straw nor houses. He guessed, and later confirmed, that they meant to winter there and needed shelter and food for their horses. It is a telling detail: these herders used the settled country's buildings rather than destroying them.

Yet they burned neither the crops, nor the straw, nor any houses, but brought all these in to keep themselves alive. From this I firmly concluded that they meant to winter in those parts, or leave their families there, so that in winter they would find shelter and provisions for their horses. All of which I afterwards learned to be true.

Verumtamen nec fruges nec stramina nec domos aliquas combusserunt, sed in praeseruationem vitae omnia haec induxerunt. Daily lifeHungary (near Eger)1241

Dummies on spare horses to fake an army

Bishop Benedict of Várad chased a small Tartar band that had sacked Eger. Knowing they were few but had many horses, the Tartars made dummies and figures, set them on the empty horses like riders and hid them behind a low hill. When they pretended to retreat and the 'army' of dummies came out in battle order, the Hungarians thought they had fallen into an ambush and fled, and were cut down.

They made a great many masks and figures, setting them on the empty horses as if they were soldiers. And they left those horses below a little hill, with a few servants left with them … And when these came out from under the hill with the masks, in battle order as had been agreed, the Hungarians, seeing this and thinking an ambush had been laid for them, turned their backs and quickly took to flight.

Daily lifeHungary (east of the Danube)1241

Tartar bailiffs, markets and justice under occupation

After the massacres the Tartars lured people back from the woods and set up a working occupation. Each village chose a Tartar 'king'; the invaders appointed about a hundred 'canesii' (Roger glosses them as bailiffs) who kept justice and collected horses, animals, arms and gifts. Roger's own master was one of them and ruled almost a thousand villages. 'We had peace and markets,' he writes. A few lines later the same officials summon villagers with gifts and massacre them.

and each village chose for itself a king from among the Tartars, whomever it wished. … They appointed Canesii, that is bailiffs, to do justice and to procure for them horses, animals, weapons, gifts and useful clothing. My master was one of these lords, and ruled almost a thousand villages. And there were about a hundred Canesii. We had peace and markets, and justice was kept for each person.

Working translation

Original wording

et quaelibet villa elegit sibi regem de Tartaris quem optauit. … Constituerunt Canesios id est Baliuos, qui iustitiam facerent, et eis equos animalia arma exenia et vestimenta utilia procurarent. Et sic procurator meus de istis dominis erat unus, et pene mille villas regebat. Et erant Canesii fere centum. Pacem habebamus et fora, iustaque unicuique iusticia seruabatur.

Daily lifeDanube, HungaryWinter 1241 to 1242

Bait on the ice: how they crossed the frozen Danube

In a hard winter the Danube froze over, which had not happened for many years, but the Tartars did not dare ride onto the ice. They drove horses and cattle onto the bank and left them unguarded for three days. The Hungarians, thinking the enemy gone, crossed and led the animals over the ice, which showed the Tartars that it would bear horses; they then crossed in one rush (as the next page tells).

And then in winter a great deal of snow and ice came, so that the Danube froze, which had not happened for many years past. … They brought many horses and animals onto the bank of the Danube and left no one to guard them for three days, so that the beasts seemed to be wandering without keepers, and none of the Tartars appeared in those parts.

Original wording

Daily lifeMohi (Sajó river), HungaryApril 1241

Arrows like locusts at the river Sajó

At the battle by the river Sajó (Mohi), the Tartars crossed at night by a ford, surrounded the royal camp at dawn and shot into it. Roger says the arrows were so thick they almost shaded the fighters and flew through the air like swarms of locusts, so that the king could not even draw up his lines. He was not in the battle himself and writes from survivors' reports.

For so dense was the shooting of arrows that it almost made shade for the fighters; and the arrows flew through the air as locusts and grasshoppers go in swarms. And so, unable to bear the strikes of the arrows, they fell back inside the ring of the army. The king could not draw up his battle lines

The nameHungary1241 to 1242

"Held not by Tartars but in Tartarus"

In his opening letter Roger plays on the old pun that made 'Tartars' out of 'Tatars': whoever falls into their hands will feel he is held not by Tartars but in Tartarus, the pit of the underworld. He stresses that he writes from experience, having lived among them 'for a time and half a time', a biblical phrase for a long ordeal. The pun shows how the name itself carried a hellish ring for Latin Christians.

And let everyone know that I do not report these things rashly: whoever comes into the hands of these Tartars, it would have been better for him not to have been born, and he will feel himself held not by Tartars but in Tartarus. I report this as one who went through it. For I was among them for a time and half a time, in which death would have been a comfort, just as life was a punishment.

Working translation

Original wording

Et sciant cuncti haec me temere non referre; quod quisquis ad manus ipsorum deuenerit Tartarorum, si natus non fuisset melius esset ei; et sentiet se non a Tartaris sed in tartaro detineri. Hoc refero ut expertus. Fui enim per tempus et dimidium temporis inter eos; in quo mori solatium exstitisset, sicut supplicium fuit vita.

The nameHungary1241

Batu, 'king of kings', and the princes who invaded

Roger lists the Tartar leaders who entered Hungary. Batu is the 'king of kings'; under him a commander Roger calls Bochetor held most power, and Kadan was the bravest. Several more names follow in garbled Latin forms, and Roger gives the army as 500,000 men. It shows how a Latin writer who had met the invaders understood their chain of command.

The king of kings and lord of the Tartars who entered Hungary was called by name Bathus [Batu]. Under him the commander more powerful in the army was called Bochetor. Cadan [Kadan] was said to be the most valiant. Coacton, Feycan, Peta, Hermeus, Cheb and Ocadar were counted as the greater kings among the Tartars; though there were among them very many other kings, princes and mighty men, who invaded the kingdom of Hungary with five hundred thousand armed men.

Working translation

Original wording

Rex regum et dominus Tartarorum qui Hungariam intrauerunt Bathus suo nomine vocabatur. Rector erat sub ipso in militia potentior Bochetor appellatus. Cadan in probitate melior dicebatur. Coacton Feycan Peta Hermeus Cheb Ocadar maiores reges inter Tartaros censebantur; quamquam essent inter eos alii reges quamplurimi, principes et potentes, qui cum quinquies centenis millibus armatorum regnum Hungariae inuaserunt.

Strange talesHungary (a fortified island in the Körös region)1241

Turning over corpses for buried flour

After the Tartars stormed the island refuge where he had sheltered, Roger lived as a fugitive in the woods. Hunger drove him to creep back onto the island at night and turn over the bodies of the dead in search of buried flour and meat. He hid in caves, dug holes or crept into hollow trees, while (he says on the next page) the Tartars combed the thickets like dogs hunting hares and boars.

So, with hunger and thirst pressing me hard, I was forced at night to go onto the island and turn over the bodies of the dead, to find buried flour and meat to eat, or anything else edible … I had to find caves, or dig pits, or look for hollow trees in which I could hide

Cautions
  • Textual transmission entirely via the 1488 Thuroczy prints (no MS); modern critical edition Juhász in Scriptores rerum Hungaricarum II (1938) and Bak/Rady CEMT 5 (2010) not open access. The Bongars 1600 volume also contains Thuroczy and other Hungarian chronicles with Tartar notices.

Why it matters

The most detailed contemporary eyewitness account of Mongol administration in a conquered European kingdom (census, appointed officials, harvest under occupation), and of the withdrawal of 1242; central for what 'Tartar' rule looked like on the ground.
