Deguignes' general history of the Huns, Turks, Mongols and western Tartars

A Paris orientalist's four-volume history of the steppe peoples from the Xiongnu to the Ottomans, built from Chinese, Arabic and Persian books, 1756 to 1758.

Full title General history of the Huns, Turks, Mongols and other western Tartars before and after Christ down to the present, preceded by an introduction with chronological tables of the princes who reigned in Asia (4 vols in 5, 1756-1758)

Joseph de Guignes

Read the 4 passagesOpen the original book

Joseph de Guignes (Deguignes), a Paris scholar of Chinese and Arabic, set out to write one continuous history of the 'western Tartars': the Huns, Turks, Mongols and their successor states from antiquity to his own century. He argued that the Huns who reached Europe were the Xiongnu of the Chinese annals. He travelled nowhere; he worked from Chinese histories (partly via Gaubil and Visdelou), Arabic and Persian manuscripts and European travel books. This corpus has the 1756 to 1758 Paris edition (Desaint and Saillant) in several Getty scans (T0628 to T0632) and the 1751 prospectus (T0627).

How they knew
Compiled from others
Who “Tartar” means here
For Deguignes 'Tartares' is an umbrella for all the nomadic peoples of inner Asia and their descendants, from the Xiongnu and Uighurs to the Mongols, the Uzbeks, the Nogais and Timur's armies, divided into 'Eastern' and 'Western' Tartars.
Why read it
The great eighteenth-century synthesis that fixed the idea of 'Tartary' as a single history; it shows how much (and how little) a learned European of the 1750s thought he knew.
Watch out
Derivative by design, and argued: the Xiongnu-Hun identification and his tidy genealogy of peoples were his theories, not settled facts. Passages on later peoples (Uzbeks, Nogais) are borrowed from travel books and older compilations.

CitiesDelhi1398

Timur's 'Tartars' slip into Delhi and sack it

the Sultanas, Tamerlane's wives, wishing to see this fine city and above all a superb palace adorned with a thousand columns, went in with their whole court; the city gate was opened, and fifteen thousand Tartars got in without anyone noticing. … Delhi was given over entirely to pillage.

Working translation

Original wording

les Sulthanes, femmes de Tamerlan, ayant envie de voir cette belle ville & principalement un superbe palais qui étoit orné de mille colonnes, elles entrèrent avec toute leur cour, la porte de la ville fut ouverte, & quinze mille Tartares y pénétrèrent sans qu'on s'en apperçût. … Dehli fut entièrement livrée au pillage.

Editorial context

After taking Delhi, Timur's wives wanted to see the city and its palace of a thousand columns; when a gate was opened for them, fifteen thousand 'Tartars' slipped in unnoticed. Fighting with the inhabitants, fires and looting followed, and Delhi was given over to plunder; Deguignes says some soldiers ended up with a hundred and fifty slaves each.

Joseph de Guignes

BuildingsBalkhearly 18th century

Balkh: stone and brick houses and a marble castle of the Uzbek khan

The town of Balkh, at present the most considerable of all the towns the Mahometan Tartars possess, is large and well peopled; most of its buildings are built of stone or brick … The Khan's castle is a large building in the oriental style, almost entirely built of marble, of which there are very fine quarries in the neighbouring mountains.

Working translation

Original wording

La ville de Balkh, à présent la plus considérable de toutes les villes que les Tartares Mahométans possedent, est grande & bien peuplée, la plupart de ses bâtimens sont bâtis de pierre ou de brique … Le château du Khan est un grand bâtiment à l'orientale, presque tout bâti de marbre dont on trouve des carrières fort belles dans les montagnes voisines.

Editorial context

Describing the Uzbek khanate of Balkh, Deguignes calls Balkh the most important town held by the 'Mahometan Tartars': large and populous, mostly built of stone or brick, with earth ramparts faced by a wall, a trading hub between Bukhara and India, and a khan's castle 'in the oriental style' built almost entirely of marble from nearby quarries. He also calls Balkh's Uzbeks the most civilised of the Muslim Tartars.

Joseph de Guignes

The nameMongolia (north of Shaanxi, Shanxi and Beijing)antiquity (written 1756)

One northern nation, parent of Huns, Turks, Mongols and Tartars

North of the northern frontiers of the provinces of Shaanxi, Shanxi and Beizhili there once lived a famous nation, which gave birth to those we have since known under the names of Huns, Turks, Mongols, Hungarians and Tartars

Working translation

Original wording

Au Nord des frontières Septentrionales des Provinces de Chensi, de Chansi & de Petcheli, habitoit autrefois une Nation célèbre, qui a donné naissance à celles que nous avons connues depuis sous le nom de Huns, de Turcs, de Mogols, de Hongrois & de Tartares

Editorial context

Deguignes opens his history of the ancient Huns with his big claim: a famous nation north of China's frontier provinces gave rise to the peoples later known as Huns, Turks, Mongols, Hungarians and Tartars. He lists the names the Chinese gave it over the dynasties, ending with Hiong-nou, which he thinks produced the name Hun.

Joseph de Guignes

About Xiongnu (Hiong-nou), in Deguignes' view the ancestors of the Huns and later Tartars

Keep in mind

A scholarly theory, not a report; the Xiongnu-Hun link is still debated and 'Hungarians' in this list is Deguignes' own speculation.

Strange talesMongolia and southern Siberialegendary

A nation born of a princess and a wolf

An old wolf prowled night and day around the prison of the Tartar princesses; he watched the tower, howling frightfully, and finally made his den at its foot. The younger one, persuaded that this wolf, which had been watching the tower for so long, could only be the god to whom her father had dedicated her, urged her sister to go down with her.

Working translation

Original wording

Un vieux loup rodoit nuit & jour autour de la prison des Princesses Tartares, il observoit cette tour en faisant des heurlemens épouvantables; enfin il établit sa tanière au pied. La plus jeune persuadée que ce loup, qui examinoit depuis si long-tems la tour, ne pouvoit être que la divinité à laquelle son père l'avoit consacrée, encouragea sa sœur à descendre avec elle.

Editorial context

Retelling a Chinese legend of the Uighurs, Deguignes says a Hun ruler shut his two beautiful daughters in a tower in the north so that heaven might marry them. An old wolf howled round the tower and made its den at the foot, and the younger sister, taking it for the god, went down to it. He adds that the Uighurs were said to imitate wolf howls in their songs, and that their tents stood on unusually high wheels, so the Chinese called them Kao-tche, 'high carts'.

Joseph de Guignes, retelling a Chinese legend

Research notes
  • Derivative by design; Deguignes' Xiongnu-Hun identification and his use of Chinese chronology are the disputed points. Multiple IA sets exist with inconsistent volume labelling (Getty vs Google); cite by IA identifier.
  • Further copies: archive.org/details/histoiregnralede04guig; archive.org/details/histoiredes00guig (1751 Histoire des Huns)

Why it matters

The work that gave "Tartares occidentaux" a systematic history from the Xiongnu to the Ottomans and first argued the Huns were the Xiongnu; the eighteenth-century synthesis behind most later French and English statements about what Tartary and the Tartars were.
