A British officer's ride through 'Crim Tartary' in 1829

A Scottish officer's travels through Russia and the Crimea during the Russo-Turkish war of 1828 to 1829, with close views of Crimean Tatar towns, villages, palace and people.

Full title Travels to the Seat of War in the East, through Russia and the Crimea, in 1829 (2 vols, London 1830)

James Edward Alexander

Read the 15 passagesOpen the original book

James Edward Alexander (1803 to 1885), a Scottish officer of the East India Company's army, travelled in 1829 from St Petersburg and Moscow through Ukraine to the Crimea, hoping to join the Russian forces in the war with Turkey. Held under suspicion for a time at Sevastopol, he eventually reached the Balkan front. Volume 1 carries his tour of the Crimea: the Nogay steppe, Simferopol (Akmetshet), the khans' palace at Bakhchysarai, the mountain ruins of Mangup and the Tatar villages of the south coast, where he dressed in Eastern clothes and used his Turkish. Volume 2 covers the war and his return by Kiev. The corpus holds the 1830 London first edition: one scan of volume 1 and four copies of volume 2.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Crimean Tatars, Muslim townspeople and mountain villagers of the peninsula he still calls 'Crim Tartary', and the nomadic Nogays of the steppe north of Perekop. In Russia 'Tartar' means the medieval Mongol-Tatar invaders, and 'Tartaric' is his label for a style of battlement.
Why read it
A late, vivid eyewitness picture of Crimean Tatar life half a century after Russian annexation: stone houses, mosques, bazaars, the khans' palace and tombs, food, dress and music, with his notes on who built the ruins he passed.
Watch out
A romantic traveller writing for British readers, fond of classical parallels and 'Oriental' colour. The revolt story is hearsay, and his architectural labels (Tartaric, Genoese) are impressions, not research. The Google OCR of volume 1 is patchy.

CitiesBakhchysarai1829

Bakhchysarai, the Tartar capital in its valley

Riding south past Tatar cottages with minaret-shaped chimneys and the domed tombs of the khans, Alexander looked down on Bakhchysarai, the old capital of the Crimean khans. He describes flat-roofed, tiled houses filling a narrow valley and climbing its sides, with the palace in the middle. On the next page he walks streets lined with workshops where Tatar tailors, smiths, shoemakers and saddlers sat cross-legged, smoking as they worked.

we saw beneath us the singular and romantic Tartar capital of the Crimea. Buckteserai, the Palatium of Strabo, is seated in a narrow valley, through which runs the stream of the Tchuruksu. The flat-roofed and tile-covered houses are built in the bottom of the valley, and up the sides of the hills : in the midst of them is a most interesting object, viz. the Tartar Palace.

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

Identifying the town with Strabo's 'Palatium' is a scholarly guess of his time.

CitiesSimferopol1829

Akmetshet: stone houses and three mosques

Simferopol, the Russian capital of the Crimea, still went by its Tatar name Akmetshet ('white mosque'). Alexander lists three Tatar mosques alongside the new cathedral and government buildings, and notes that the houses were of stone with tiled roofs, while in the Tatar quarter houses stood behind courtyards and high walls. His guide was the governor's Tatar interpreter.

The public buildings of Akmetshet, besides three Tartar mosques, are a very handsome cathedral, the halls of justice, residences of the civil officers, &c. The houses are built of stone, and roofed in the ancient Greek style with tiles: in the Tartar quarter courts surround the houses and high walls

James Edward Alexander (1830)

CitiesFeodosia1829

Caffa: Tartar songs among the ruins

Arriving at night at Theodosia, the old Caffa, Alexander found ruin on every side. He heard Tatars singing under an arch in a melancholy strain, as if mourning the decay of the once-flourishing city, and noted the crumbling citadel walls by the shore. The town's houses, he says, were built partly in the Tatar and partly in the Genoese style. From there he crossed the steppe by 'the commercial Tartar city of Karasubazar'.

we heard some Tartars under an arch singing in a most melancholy strain, and seemingly mourning over the decay of this once flourishing city: the dilapidated walls of a citadel were on the sea-shore, and near them was the stranded wreck of a Greek mystico. The houses of Caffa were built partly in the Tartar and partly in the Genoese style of architecture.

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Crimean Tatars; the former Genoese colony

Keep in mind

'Seemingly mourning' is his romantic reading of a song he could not follow.

BuildingsBakhchysarai1829

The khans' palace: painted walls and a fountain

Alexander found the palace of the former khans in good repair, though he had been told it was a ruin. He describes gaudily painted walls, minarets and tall pointed chimneys, an oblong courtyard with a marble fountain said to honour a khan's Christian wife, and a mosque by the gate. Inside were painted ceilings, stained-glass windows and brocade divans; he calls it 'a most interesting specimen of Tartaric magnificence' and notes that no Russians were allowed to live in the town.

It is a most grotesque pile : the walls are gaudily painted, and above the roofs rise minarets and numerous tall sharp-pointed chimnies. The buildings of the palace inclose an oblong court, in which is a marble fountain, erected to the memory of the Christian wife of a khan ; on the left of the gateway is a mosque

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About The Crimean Tatar khans (Giray dynasty)

Keep in mind

He does not say who built it. The Christian-wife story is the local legend behind the 'Fountain of Tears'.

BuildingsMangup1829

Mangup: a ruined mountain city of many peoples

With a Tatar guide Alexander climbed to Mangup Kale, a deserted fortress-city on a table mountain. He credits its battlemented walls to the Genoese and reads the ruins as layers of occupation: a Tatar mosque, a synagogue, a small church, and Muslim, Jewish and Christian graves. The only living things were a few grazing Tatar horses.

passed through some noble remains of battlemented walls of Genoese architecture, flanked with square and round towers. Before us were the remains of a Tartar mosque, a Jewish synagogue, a small church, and the tombs of Moslems, Israelites, and Christians, pointing out the successive occupiers of this strange city, the ruins of whose habitations were very numerous

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Crimean Tatars and earlier occupants of the site

Keep in mind

'Genoese' is his guess: Mangup was the capital of the Orthodox principality of Theodoro until the Ottomans took it in 1475.

BuildingsVeliky Novgorod1829

'Tartaric battlements' where no Tartars came

At Novgorod, Alexander saw citadel walls topped with what he calls 'Tartaric battlements', copied from the Kremlin's, and notes that the Tartars never reached Novgorod, so its church crosses have no crescent beneath them. He repeats the popular idea that a crescent under a cross marks the Tatar period, and calls Moscow's Kremlin battlements 'Tartaric' as well. He also uses the label for a tower in the Genoese fortress at Balaklava.

The walls of the citadel were surmounted by Tartaric battlements, in imitation of those of the Kremlin; yet the Tartars in their invasions never penetrated to Novogorod, and the crosses on the churches do not, therefore, surmount a crescent

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Medieval Tartars (Mongol-Tatar invaders of Rus)

Keep in mind

'Tartaric' is his style label: the Moscow Kremlin's brick walls and swallow-tail merlons were built by Italian masters in the 1480s and 1490s. The crescent-under-the-cross explanation is a popular belief that most historians reject.

BuildingsBakhchysarai1829

Black sarcophagi with white turbans

Two domed mausoleums beside the palace held the remains of a long line of khans. Inside, Alexander was startled to find black sarcophagi on the floor with white turbans at their heads; outside, the graves had carved marble headstones topped with turbans, shaded by cypresses.

a burial ground attached, contained the remains of a long line of khans : on entering the former it was rather startling to see black sarcophagi on the floor, with white turbans at the head. In the open air the tombs were adorned with carved and turbaned headstones of marble, which, with the accompanying cypress, give such effect to Moslem cemeteries.

James Edward Alexander (1830)

BuildingsCrimea1829

Village houses built into the hillside

On the south coast, below the Baidar pass, Alexander found houses unlike any he had seen: roofs carried on low stone walls on three sides, with the fourth side set against the slope like Swiss chalets, among fruit trees and poplars. The villagers wore the same Tatar dress as before, but he thought their features Grecian and both men and women very handsome.

The houses, as well as their inhabitants, were now totally different from what we had hitherto seen : the roofs were supported on three sides by low stone walls, and the fourth rested against the side of the declivity (after the manner of Swiss chalets) … but their features were Grecian, and both men and women were extremely handsome.

James Edward Alexander (1830)

Daily lifePerekop1829

Nogay herdsmen, 'inhabitants of waggons'

Crossing the sandy steppe on the way to the Crimea, Alexander passed herds of horses guarded by Nogay Tartars and their dogs. He likens them to the wagon-dwellers Herodotus described among the Scythians, notes their two- and four-wheeled carts drawn by horses, oxen or camels, and describes their sheepskin caps, grey coats and sandals of leather or linden bark.

passed several taboons of horses guarded by wild-looking Nogay Tartars and their dogs: these are the Hamaxobitii, or inhabitants of waggons, of Herodotus : their arbas are two or four-wheeled, and drawn by horses, oxen, or camels. The dress of these nomade tribes harmonises well with the Scythian deserts which they frequent ; a rough sheep-skin cap, a grayish surcoat reaching below the knees

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Nogay Tartars (Nogais)

Keep in mind

The Herodotus comparison is Alexander's classical flourish, not evidence that the Nogays descend from Scythians.

Daily lifeSimferopol1829

Koumis, chess and music in a Tartar coffee-house

Drawn in by music, Alexander entered a Tatar coffee-house in Akmetshet. Greeks and Tatars sat cross-legged on carpets, smoking long pipes and drinking coffee; an old man urged a youth to drink koumis, which Alexander calls a spirit distilled from mare's milk; two men played chess; and three musicians played a stringed instrument and a drum and sang.

on entering it, I found it divided into compartments by railings, and Greeks and Tartars were sitting cross-legged on carpets, and smoking cherry-stick chebouks and drinking coffee. A gray-beard, in a corner, was encouraging a youth to drink koumis or spirits, distilled from mare's milk. A grave-looking pair were engaged at chess ; and in the window-seat were three musicians

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Crimean Tatars and Greeks

Keep in mind

Koumis is fermented mare's milk; the distilled spirit made from it is a different drink, and Alexander may be running the two together.

Daily lifeCrimea1829

An old Tartar woman's welcome

At the village of Koochook Koi (Kuchuk-Koy) an old Tatar woman invited Alexander in for bread and bekmes, a thick pear syrup. He records her dress closely: a white headcloth over hair in six long plaits, a coloured petticoat under a blue knee-length coat held by a patterned belt, and silk trousers gathered at the ankle. A little girl nearby wore a red fez with silver coins sewn on the front.

a clean old Tartar woman invited me to eat some bread and bekmis with her : her head was enveloped in a white handkerchief … her hair, in six long plaits ; her coloured petticoat was covered with a blue surcoat reaching below the knees, and confined round her waist with a figured belt ; her trousers were of silk, drawn in at the ancles

James Edward Alexander (1830)

The namePerekop1829

The Nogays: a fragment of the Golden Horde

Alexander describes the Nogays' faces and then explains who they are: a part of the Golden Horde scattered by Timur (Tamerlane), whose wars shattered the Horde in the 1390s. It is a short, fairly accurate piece of steppe history attached to a first-hand description.

Their noses are short, eyes elongated, and beards scanty : such are the Nogays, a party of the Golden Horde dispersed by Timoorlung (Tamarlane).

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Nogay Tartars (Nogais)

Keep in mind

The physical description reflects the racial typing common in travel books of his day.

Strange talesSimferopol1829

A khan's descendant from Edinburgh University

In Simferopol an English gig drove into a courtyard and out stepped an old fellow-student of Alexander's from Edinburgh University: Sultan Katty Gherry Crim Gherry, of a branch of the Tatar khans of the Crimea, born in the Caucasus. On the next page Alexander adds that a missionary brought him to Britain, that he married a Miss Nelson of Edinburgh, and that he now had an imperial pension and estates on the south coast.

One day I was surprised to see an English gig drive into their court, with a mounted servant behind. A dark good-looking gentleman then alighted, and I recognised an old fellow-student at the University of Edinburgh, the Sultan Katty Gherry Crim Gherry. He was born in the Caucasus, of a branch of the Tartar Khans of the Crimea, was brought to Britain

James Edward Alexander (1830)

Strange talesBakhchysarai1828

A carter's boast exposes a Tartar plot

Alexander heard from a civilian that in 1828 the Crimean Tatars were ready to revolt, stirred up, he guessed, by the Turks. A Karaite Jewish merchant of Bakhchysarai grew suspicious when his Tatar carter refused well-paid work, followed him home and overheard him telling his mother to wait until the Russians lost at Anapa. The man was arrested and confessed to a plot, spies were set to work and the leaders punished.

Russians are defeated before Anapa, and you'll see that we will be masters of the Crimea again. I have cast an eye at the Karaémee's house and garden; his tribe, as well as the Muscovites, will ere long not possess a foot of the country." On this the Tartar was taken up, and he confessed that there was a plot on foot to rise against the Russians

Words attributed to a Crimean Tatar carter, in a story Alexander heard from a civilian (1830)

About Crimean Tatars under Russian rule

Keep in mind

Secondhand anecdote from the Russian side; the 'confession' may have been forced. On the previous page Alexander says he himself saw no cause for Tatar complaint.

Strange talesKyiv1829

Bones of those who hid from a Tartar invader

In the cave monastery at Kiev, after oil-weeping skulls and a supposed relic of the Holy Innocents, the monks showed Alexander walled-up cells, fitted with glass panes, holding the bones of a thousand priests and townspeople. They were said to have hidden from a Tartar invader and been suffocated in their refuge.

In cells which were built up, with a thick piece of glass in the wall, were the bones of one thousand of the unfortunate priests and people of Kiev, who thought to conceal themselves from a Tartar invader, but who were inhumanly suffocated in their sanctuary.

James Edward Alexander (1830)

About Medieval Tartars (Mongol-Tatar invaders of Rus)

Keep in mind

He saw the bones; the story of how they died is the monks' tradition, told alongside other relics he plainly doubted.
