Nieuhof's Dutch embassy to the 'Great Tartar Khan' in Beijing

A Dutch steward's illustrated eyewitness account of travelling from Canton to Beijing in 1655 to 1657, just after the Manchu ('Tartar') conquest of China.

Catalogue title The embassy of the Dutch East India Company to the Great Tartar Khan, the present emperor of China (1655-1657 embassy of De Goyer and De Keyser)

Johan Nieuhof

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

Johan Nieuhof (1618-1672) was steward of the Dutch East India Company embassy of Pieter de Goyer and Jacob de Keyzer, which travelled by river and canal from Canton to Beijing in 1655-1656 to seek trade with the new Qing emperor. His journal, reworked by his brother Hendrik and the publisher Jacob van Meurs, appeared in 1665 with about 150 engravings and became a best-seller. Part one is the eyewitness journey; part two is a general description of China and of the Manchu conquest, compiled mostly from the Jesuit Martino Martini. The corpus holds the 1665 Dutch first edition and John Ogilby's 1669 English translation, which also carries an appendix taken from Athanasius Kircher.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Manchus who had just conquered China (the Qing), their emperor being the 'Great Tartar Cham'; in the historical chapters also the earlier Mongol conquerors (the Yuan) and the people of Niuche or East Tartary (Manchuria).
Why read it
The fullest early eyewitness picture of 'Tartars' as the new masters of China: their soldiers, lords, women and court, and the ruined cities they left along the road.
Watch out
Keep the eyewitness journey apart from the compiled general description and from Kircher's appendix. The printed text was edited and embellished by others, and the English OCR is poor in places, so quotes here are normalized from the scans.

CitiesGuangzhou (Canton)24 November 1650

The fall and sack of Canton, 1650

Nieuhof tells how the Tartar army took Canton after a siege that, he says, lasted a whole year, helped by a bribed Chinese governor who opened a gate. Two Dutch gunners married to Chinese women had helped the defenders. The sack that followed lasted weeks. He arrived five years later, so this is what the townspeople told him.

It was upon the 24. of November, in the year 1650. when the Tartars upon this advantage rushed with their whole Army into the City … The whole Tartar Army being got into the City, the place was soon turned to a Map of misery … there was heard no other cry in the Streets, but Strike, Kill, and Destroy the Rebellious Barbarians

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

CitiesManchuria (Niuche)1665 (claim about the Yuan period)

124 cities once, now 'movable cities'

The Tartars say there were 124 cities in East Tartary under the Yuan (Mongol) dynasty. The author will not vouch for it, since the Tartars in China themselves could not tell him whether they still stand, only that there were many low huts and earthen walls. Their real towns, 'so to speak', move with them and their herds.

The Tartars relate that under the rule of the Yuan dynasty there lay in this East Tartary one hundred and twenty-four cities; but whether they are still to be found today I dare not affirm as true … but [they said] indeed that there were many low huts and earthen walls. They have, so to speak, movable and portable cities

Zy hebben, zoo te zeggen, beweeghlijke en verplaatzelijke steden

BuildingsNanjing1656

At Nanjing the conquerors live in huts; the Chinese keep the stone city

Describing the ruined Ming imperial palace at Nanjing, Nieuhof notes that the Tartar garrison settled near a temple and built themselves huts, leaving the Chinese to live and trade in the city. The palace buildings, of hard stone painted yellow, were Chinese work. On the same page he says the Tartars destroyed the palace out of hatred for the Ming family.

The Tartars seated themselves near to an Idol Temple called Paolinxi, where they built themselves several Hutts, leaving the Chineses to dwell in the City, and there to drive their Trade. The buildings are all of a hard sort of Stone, which the Natives have most curiously Painted with a Yellow Colour, that when the Sun reflects on them, they shine like Gold.

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

BuildingsNanjing1656

The Porcelain Tower: said to be built by Chinese on a Tartar's orders

Nieuhof admired the nine-storey glazed Porcelain Tower of Nanjing and repeats what he was told: the Chinese built it at their own cost on the orders of a Tartar conqueror seven hundred years before, and the new Tartar conquerors chose to spare it. In fact the tower was built by the Ming Yongle emperor in the early 1400s, so the story he was told is wrong, but it shows buildings being linked to Tartar conquerors in popular memory.

This wonderful Pile (as they inform us) the Chineses built at their own charges, commanded by the insulting Conquerour the Tartar, to them a Pillar of Honour, to the Subdued of Obloquie, seven hundred years since, when in like manner, as lately, they over-ran all China

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

About Tartars (an earlier conquest, and the Manchus)

BuildingsBeijing (imperial palace)1656

Golden roofs? No, glazed clay tiles

Describing the palace where the 'Great Cham' now lived, Nieuhof explains that the roofs are covered with yellow glazed tiles that shine brighter than gold in the sun. This, he says, is why some people reported that the palace was roofed with pure gold, when in fact the tiles are made of clay.

All the Roofs of the Buildings are covered with Yellow glazed Pantiles, which shine, when the Sun reflects, brighter then Gold; which has made some believe and report, that the Roof of this Royal Palace was covered with pure Gold; whereas in truth the Tiles are only made of Clay, and Glaz'd and Neil'd over with Artificial Yellow

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

BuildingsManchuria (Niuche)1665

Not caves but shining tents of waxed silk

The Chinese, bitter enemies of the Niuche Tartars, mock them as cave-dwellers, but the text calls this false: they live in fine tents used instead of houses, made of silk or cloth coated with clear wax so they shine like mirrors and shed the rain. Great men have several tents joined so they look like one, for wives, servants and kitchen.

The Chinese, who with good reason are very bitter against the Tartars of Niuche, invent and mock them by saying that they live in caves and underground pits; which is false: for they keep themselves in very fine and handsome tents … of silk stuffs or cloths, so neatly and artfully coated with bright, translucent wax that they glitter and shine in the sun like a mirror

Daily lifeNanjing1656

A Tartar lady tries on the ambassador's hat

Riding past the old imperial court at Nanjing, the Dutch were invited in by a Tartar lady of about forty, 'very debonair and free'. She came out to meet the ambassador, admired how their swords bent without breaking, put his hat on her own head and unbuttoned his doublet. She then served them a strong drink made of beans. A lively picture of a Manchu noblewoman's easy manners with strangers.

we past by the Gate of the old Imperial Court, where sat a great Tartar Lady, with her servants waiting upon her, about forty years of Age: She very civilly sent to our Interpreter to invite the Embassadours into her House … She was very debonair and free, looked upon our Swords, and much admired their bending without breaking: She took the Embassadours Hat, and put it on her own Head

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

Daily lifeBeijing1656

A Tartar grandee eats half-raw pork at the council table

At their first reception in Beijing the envoys sat with the council, whose Tartar members wore plain clothes and sat on a bench covered with old linen. The presiding lord grew hungry and ate half-raw pork 'more like a Butcher, then a Prince'. The Jesuit Adam Schall, dressed in Tartar fashion, interpreted. The Dutch were shocked by the lack of ceremony.

his Highness found himself hungry, and sent for a piece of Pork to satisfie his Appetite, which was half-raw, whereof he did eat most heartily, in a slovenly manner, that he looked more like a Butcher, then a Prince … When Dinner was brought up, his Highness and the rest of the Tartar Lords fell on again as greedily, as if they had eat nothing all that day

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

Daily lifeChina1640s to 1650s

Forced to wear the Tartar queue

The general description explains that since the conquest the Chinese must shave their heads in the Tartar manner and keep only one lock hanging behind. Thousands, it says, chose death rather than lose their long hair. This is the Qing head-shaving order, told from the Chinese side.

since this Famous Empire fell under the Tyrannous yoke of the Tartars, and became subject to them, they have also submitted to the Tartarian mode of cutting and wearing the Hair: for now the Chineses do cut off all their Hair after the manner of their Conquerour, and reserve only one Lock, which hangs down behind

Johan Nieuhof, general description of China (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669); this part is compiled, largely from Martino Martini

Daily lifeGuangdong, on the river north of CantonMarch 1656

A Tartar archer hits a palm-sized mark three times

While the embassy camped ashore, the Tartar escort put on a display of arms. One bowman shot three arrows in a row through a target no bigger than a hand at thirty-five paces and was paid a small coin. A small eyewitness scene that matches the Manchus' fame as archers.

The Tartars in the mean time, to show some pastime, exercised their Arms before the Tent of the Embassadours, which was performed by them with much dexterity. Amongst the rest, there was one with a Bow and Arrow, who was so rare a Marks-man, that he shot thrice together through the white, no broader then the Palm of a Hand, at the distance of thirty five Paces

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

Daily lifeManchuria and China1650s

Horsemen raised to arms from the cradle

A compiled portrait of the Tartar soldier: iron helmets with dyed horse-tail plumes, bows and sabres but no muskets, nearly all cavalry. They are pale, silent, trained to arms from infancy, unmatched archers, and eat any flesh half cooked. The same page calls them 'a Nation of Plunderers' and says few have any religion beyond some lamas.

They are whitish of Complexion, not talkative, but silent, and well composed, bred up to Arms from their very Cradles, which makes them such excellent Souldiers … They eat whatsoever they can get, but chiefly flesh, and that half roasted or boiled, being not curious of what sort it is, whether of Camels, Horses, or other Creatures.

Johan Nieuhof, general description of China (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669); this part is compiled, largely from Martino Martini

The nameManchuria (Niuche)1665

The Manchus called 'Kin', lords of the golden mountains

The Dutch general description says the people of Niuche, 'properly East Tartary', are known to the Chinese and their neighbours as Kin, meaning gold, and are called lords of the golden mountains because the Chinese believe their land is rich in gold mines. Kin is the Jin, the dynastic name used by the Jurchen and again by the Manchus before 1636.

The inhabitants are known among the Chinese and their neighbours by the name of Kin, which in Dutch means Gold. They are also commonly called the Lords and Masters of the golden Mountains, because it is a common belief among the Chinese that the land they possess holds very gold-rich mines

The nameBatavia (Jakarta)1655

The emperor of China is the 'Grand Cham of Tartary'

In his opening words Nieuhof explains that the Company sent its envoys to Peking 'to the Grand Cham of Tartary, the now Emperour of China'. The old European title for the Mongol Great Khan is simply transferred to the new Manchu ruler of China, the Shunzhi emperor. This is the vocabulary later writers like Witsen and Dapper inherited.

with Credentials, and a considerable train of Attendants, to Peking in China, to the Grand Cham of Tartary, the now Emperour of China, impowering to negotiate concerning a Free and Mutual Commerce with them in his Kingdoms and Territories.

Johan Nieuhof (English translation by John Ogilby, 1669)

Strange talesCentral Asia (placed by Kircher near Cathay and Tangut)1667 (Kircher), citing a medieval Arabic geographer

Gog and Magog beyond the mountains, fires in the night

This page is not by Nieuhof but from Athanasius Kircher's China Illustrata, bound into Ogilby's English volume. Kircher quotes an Arabic geographer on the cities of Gog and Magog beyond an unclimbable snowy mountain: people who climb it rarely return, and those who do report many fires at night. Kircher then fits this to the Great Cham's empire and Prester John.

On the other side of the Mountain are many Cities of Jagog and Magog. And it often happened, that the Inhabitants of this Region, out of a certain curiosity, ascend some part of it … but many never return … some that have return'd safe, relate, that in the Region on the other side of the Mountain in the night season, are many fires

an Arabic geographer as quoted by Athanasius Kircher (appendix in Ogilby's 1669 volume, not by Nieuhof)

Why it matters

The best-selling early European description of Qing China at the moment of Manchu ("Tartar") conquest; its title equates the Qing emperor with the Great Tartar Khan, fixing the vocabulary later writers (Witsen, Dapper) inherited. Dedicated to Cornelis Witsen, father of Nicolaas.
