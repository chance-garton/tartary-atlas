Edmund Spencer's 1836 voyage round the Black Sea, volume one

A British travel writer's letters from a Danube and Black Sea voyage in 1836, including the south coast of 'Krim Tartary' and the Russian forts of Circassia.

Catalogue title Travels in Circassia, Krim-Tartary, etc.

Edmund Spencer

Edmund Spencer, a British travel writer, went down the Danube to Constantinople in 1836, then sailed from Odessa with Count Mikhail Vorontsov, governor-general of New Russia, along the Crimean coast to Kerch and on to the Russian forts of the Circassian coast. His letters were published in London in 1837. This corpus copy is Volume I, which reaches Crimea and Circassia only in its last hundred pages; the fuller description of Crimean Tatar towns belongs to Volume II. What is here is brief: Tatar villages and fishermen glimpsed from the deck, Tatar names for kurgans, and a Tatar-English-Circassian vocabulary.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Crimean Tatars ('Krim Tartars') and the Nogai Tatars, including those who fled Russian rule into the Caucasus; he also calls the Natukhai of the Kuban 'originally Tartars'. 'Tartar' is also his name for the Turkic language spoken there.
Why read it
A snapshot of the Crimean Tatars fifty years after Russian annexation, seen from a Russian governor's steamer: small villages with mosques among the new villas of Russian nobles, and Tatar traditions about the steppe mounds.
Watch out
Spencer travelled as a guest of the Russian governor, wrote with strong pro-Circassian and anti-Muslim opinions, and saw the Tatars mostly from the ship. Volume II is not in this corpus.

CitiesAnapa1836

Anapa: mud cabins and a medley of peoples

Anapa, the first Russian fortress on the Circassian coast, is described from notes by the expedition's historiographer: most houses were cabins of wood and mud thatched with maize leaves or reeds, and its 2,000 people included Circassians, Nogai Tatars, Kalmyks, Kumyks, Kabardians, 'Kazannes', Dagestanis and Bukharans. Spencer admits he did not land there himself.

The greater number of the houses of the town are mere cabins, constructed of wood and mud, thatched with the leaves of Indian corn or reeds. The inhabitants, consisting of Circassians, Nogay Tartars, Kalmucks, Komouks, Kabartis, Kazannes, Demikarponis, Daghistanes, Boukhares, with a few Greeks, Armenians, and Russians, speaking a medley of tongues

BuildingsKerch1836

Kurgans raised by each mourner's handful of earth

After watching a kurgan being opened at Kerch, Spencer reflects on the countless mounds of the region. He reports the tradition of 'descendants of the aboriginal inhabitants' in the Crimean mountains: when a great warrior or king died, every admirer carried a portion of earth and threw it on his grave. On the next page he calls this 'the tradition of the Tartars' and compares it to Scottish cairns.

these tumuli were voluntarily erected by the people ; as when any of their great warriors or kings expired, his ashes were placed in the tomb, and every man who admired his virtues carried a portion of earth and threw it over his grave.

Who Crimean Tatars and older mountain peoples (as tradition-keepers)

Heard itEdmund Spencer

Keep in mind. A local tradition; the Kerch kurgans are mostly Greek and Scythian burials many centuries older than the Tatars. Spencer credits the builders to 'a great and powerful people' of antiquity, not to the Tatars.

BuildingsAlupka, Crimea1836

Tatar cottages and little mosques among the vineyards

Sailing along the south coast of Crimea past Alupka, where Vorontsov's palace stood, Spencer saw terraced vineyards on the slopes and, in the small valleys below, clustered Tatar cottages with their little mosques and slender minarets. It is a brief glimpse of Tatar villages surviving beside the new estates of Russian nobles.

the whole space between the towering cliffs and the sea, forming a splendid panorama, presented one mass of the richest foliage ; terraced vineyards adorned the hills, mingling their lighter green with the dark cold pine of the mountain; while the miniature valleys beneath glowed with the clustering cots of the Tartars, and their pretty little mosques and slender minarets.

The nameKerch1836

Altyn Obo, the Tatars' 'hill of gold'

At Kerch's museum Spencer saw finds from a mound opened in 1830 that the Tatars called Altyn Obo, 'hill of gold', and which was claimed to be the tomb of Mithridates. The gold vases and ornaments justified the name, he says, though the best had gone to St Petersburg. The Tatars named the mound; the treasures were Greek work.

we then extended our promenade to the Museum, the collection of which has been considerably augmented since the opening of a tumulus in 1830, called by the Tartars, Altyn Obo, or the hill of gold ; pretended to have been the tomb of Mithridates.

Who Crimean Tatars (as name-givers)

Saw itEdmund Spencer

Keep in mind. The 1830 excavation was of the Kul-Oba kurgan; Spencer's 'Altyn Obo' may conflate names. He himself says the finds were 'unquestionably of Grecian workmanship'.

The nameCircassia (northwest Caucasus)1836

Crimean and Nogai refugees in the Caucasus

Explaining why his book includes a Tatar vocabulary, Spencer says many Circassians knew the language and that Crimean and Nogai Tatars who had fled Russian rule were settled throughout the Circassian provinces. He adds that the word lists should disprove the common idea that Circassians and Tatars shared an origin.

many of the Circassians are not only acquainted with it, but we every where find, throughout the whole of these provinces, Krim and Nogay Tartars domiciliated, who, on the subjection of their country to the rule of Russia, fled into the Caucasus.

The nameKuban River1836

The Natukhai, 'originally Tartars'

Vorontsov told Spencer he had been visited at Anapa by a prince of the Natukhai, a tribe 'originally Tartars' who had fled into the Caucasus after Russia conquered the lands of the Kuban and Azov. Spencer calls them Russia's most uncompromising enemies there. The Natukhai are usually counted as a Circassian (Adyghe) tribe.

he had been honoured with a visit from a Circassian prince, chief of the Natouhay tribe, originally Tartars, who had fled into the mountains of the Caucasus upon the conquest of the provinces on the banks of the Kouban and the sea of Azov.
