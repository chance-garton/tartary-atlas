The Orkhon inscriptions: the Turks write their own history in stone

Royal memorial stones of the Turk khagans in Mongolia (c.720 to 735), deciphered in the 1890s, where the name 'Tatar' first appears in writing.

Full title The Old Turkic Orkhon inscriptions (732, 735, c.726) and Yenisei inscriptions: Radloff’s editions with transcription and German translation (St Petersburg 1894-1899) and Thomsen’s decipherment and French translation (Helsingfors 1896); Finnish 1892 atlas of squeezes

Orkhon inscriptions (Kul Tegin, Bilge Qaghan, Tonyukuk)

Second Turk qaghanate court (Yollugh Tegin; Tonyukuk)

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

The Orkhon inscriptions are carved stone memorials for the Turk prince Kul Tegin (732), his brother Bilge Qaghan (735) and the minister Tonyukuk (c.720 to 726), found in the Orkhon valley of Mongolia in 1889. They are written in Old Turkic runic script, with a Chinese text on the Kul Tegin stone. The corpus holds Radloff's German edition (1894 to 1899), Thomsen's decipherment and French translation (1896), Heikel's report of the 1890 Finnish expedition (1892), and a Yenisei glossary. The editors' introductions add Chinese annals and site descriptions.

How they knew
Official record
Who “Tartar” means here
In the inscriptions, the Otuz Tatar ('Thirty Tatars') and Toquz Tatar ('Nine Tatars') are a real people on the eastern edge of the Turk empire, which Thomsen identifies as Mongols. In the 1890s editions, 'Tatar' also means Siberian Turkic groups (Abakan Tatars) and the Tatars of the Volga.
Why read it
These are the oldest dated texts in a Turkic language and the first written appearance of the name Tatar, and they show nomad khans hiring Chinese builders and warning their people against settled life.
Watch out
The 1890s readings have been much improved since (Tekin 1968 and later), and Radloff and Thomsen often disagree. Many quotes here are from the editors' own notes or from Chinese annals they cite, which are marked.

What it says: 14 passages

CitiesOrkhon valley (Mongolia)c.716 to 720

'No fixed dwelling': why the Turks refused a walled capital

'The Turk people do not amount to a hundredth part of the population of China, and if, despite their small numbers, they have been able to resist that empire, it is because the Turks, seeking only grass and water, have no interest but hunting and have no fixed dwelling, but practise only the trade of war.'

Working translation

Original wording

»Le peuple doulgase (c. a. d. les Toukioux) n'atteint pas en nombre la centième partie de la population de la Chine, et si, malgré son infériorité numérique, il a pu résister à cet empire, la raison en est que les Doulgases, ne recherchant que de l'herbe et de l'eau, n'ont d'autre intérêt que la chasse, et n'ont pas de demeure fixe, mais ne s'exercent qu'au métier de la guerre.

Editorial context

Heikel retells the Chinese annals: Bilge Qaghan wanted to build a wall round his residence and temples to Buddha and Laozi, but his old adviser Tonyukuk warned him off. The Turks, he said, were not a hundredth of China's population and survived only because they follow grass and water, hunt, have no fixed homes and practise war; Chinese armies live in towns and are captured when beaten, and Buddhism makes men weak. Heikel concludes the khan's camp probably stayed unfortified.

Chinese annals (Tang history) in Axel Heikel's French retelling, Inscriptions de l'Orkhon (1892)

CitiesKarakorum and Khara Balgasun (Orkhon valley)8th century and 1230s

Karakorum and the older Uighur city: two sites on the Orkhon

'Before the entrance of this palace lie stones covered with engraved characters, which we have seen. In the reign of the Qaan (Khan) Ogedei these stones were lifted and a pit was found, in which there was a great stone slab bearing an inscription.'

Working translation

Original wording

»Devant l'entrée de ce palais gisent des pierres couvertes de caractères gravés que nous avons vus. Sous le règne du Caan (Khan) Ogotaï on leva ces pierres, et l'on découvrit une fosse, dans laquelle il y avait une grande table de pierre, chargée d'une inscription».

Editorial context

Heikel quotes the Persian historian Juvaini: Ogedei founded Karakorum, and on the Orkhon were found the ruins of a city and palace that had belonged to the Uighurs, with inscribed stones before the palace gate. Heikel argues that Karakorum stood where the Erdene Zuu monastery was built about 1590, and was not the same as the older Uighur ruins of Khara Balgasun. Real cities rose and fell here, and their builders are named.

Juvaini, History of the World Conqueror (13th century), quoted by Axel Heikel (1892) after d'Ohsson

BuildingsKhosho Tsaidam, Orkhon valley (Mongolia)732

The khan sent to China for sculptors

From the khagan of the Chinese I brought sculptors and had them carve. My invitation was not refused: they sent the khagan's inner (household) sculptors. I had them put up a separate building (the temple, the hall), carve sculptures inside and outside, and cut the stone.

Working translation

Original wording

De chez le kagan des Chinois j'ai fait venir des sculpteurs et je (leur) ai (fait) sculpter. On n'a pas rejeté mon invitation, mais on a envoyé les sculpteurs intérieurs (c.-à-d. attachés à la maison) du kagan. Je leur ai fait ériger à part l'édifice (le temple, la salle), et tailler à part les sculptures à l'intérieur et à l'extérieur, et je leur ai fait tailler la pierre.

Editorial context

Bilge Qaghan says he brought sculptors from the Chinese emperor, who sent his own palace craftsmen. Through them he had a separate building (a temple or hall) put up, had sculptures carved inside and outside, and had the 'eternal stone' cut and inscribed. The Turk ruler plainly credits Chinese craftsmen with the stonework and the temple beside his brother's grave; his cousin Yollugh Tegin wrote the text.

Bilge Qaghan (inscription composed by Yollugh Tegin), Kul Tegin inscription, 732, in Vilhelm Thomsen's French translation (1896)

BuildingsKhosho Tsaidam, Orkhon valley (Mongolia)732

Named Chinese builders of the memorial

To raise the buildings, to make the sculptures and the inscribed stone, Chankany and Chang-Sangun came.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Gebäude aufzuführen, die Skulpturarbeiten und den Schriftstein herzustellen, sind Tschänkany und Tschäng-Sängün gekommen.

Editorial context

Radloff's translation of the end of the Kul Tegin inscription lists the embassies at the funeral and then names the builders: 'to raise the buildings, make the sculptures and the inscribed stone came Tschankany and Tschang-Sangun', both from the Chinese emperor. Radloff's glossary adds that 'barq' here means the temple built by the grave, and that the old pair 'eb barq' meant 'houses (yurts) and other buildings'.

Kul Tegin inscription, 732, in Wilhelm Radloff's German translation (1895)

BuildingsKhosho Tsaidam, Orkhon valley (Mongolia)1890 (visit to a site of 732)

Chinese-style roof tiles in a marmot burrow

From one of the holes made by these animals I pulled out a quantity of half-round tiles, such as are still generally used in China to cover the roofs of houses. There were several others like them scattered over the hill and round about.

Working translation

Original wording

Je retirai d'un des trous faits par ces animaux une quantité de tuiles demi-rondes, telles qu'on les emploie encore généralement en Chine pour couvrir les toits des maisons. Il y en avait plusieurs autres semblables éparpillées sur la colline et aux alentours.

Editorial context

Heikel describes the mound beside the Kul Tegin monument. Marmots had dug into it, and from one of their holes he pulled a quantity of half-round roof tiles 'such as are still used in China to cover the roofs of houses', with more scattered on the hill. He concluded the mound was a fallen building, a temple put up when the monument was erected. It is a first-hand archaeological observation that fits the inscription's statement about Chinese builders.

Axel Heikel, Inscriptions de l'Orkhon (Helsingfors 1892), report of the Finnish expedition of 1890

BuildingsKhara Balgasun (Orkhon valley)745 to 841

From tents to palaces: the Uighur khans' capital

The founding of the Uighur khanate and the setting of its khagans' main seat on the banks of the Orkhon go back to 745, but only later did the khagans replace the tents of their royal camp with palaces. Indeed it is only in 780 that the Tang history speaks, as something new among the Uighurs, of the building of a palace.

Working translation

Original wording

La fondation du Khanat ouïgour et l'établissement du siège principal de ses Khakans sur les bords de l'Orkhoun remonte à l'année 745, mais ce ne serait que plus tard que les Khakans auraient substitué des palais aux tentes de leur camp royal. Ce n'est en effet qu'en 780 que l'histoire des T'ang parle, comme d'une chose nouvelle chez les Ouïgours, de la construction d'un palais

Editorial context

In notes to the same volume the French sinologist Deveria writes that the Uighur khanate set up its main seat on the Orkhon in 745, but that the khans only later replaced the tents of their royal camp with palaces: the Tang history mentions a palace built for the Uighur ruler in 780 as something new, and in 784 a khan had an inscription carved on a stone stele 'before the gate of his capital'. The capital was burned by the Kirghiz in 841. It is a clear dated sequence from tent camp to built city.

Gabriel Deveria, notes on the Chinese inscriptions, in Inscriptions de l'Orkhon (Helsingfors 1892)
See the original page (p. XXXIV)

Daily lifeOtuken (Khangai mountains, Mongolia)732

'Sweet words and soft silks': a khan's warning about China

Living in this land I entered into relations with the Chinese people. The call of the Chinese people, who give us so much gold, silver, isigti and silk(?) without trouble, was sweet, and their riches were soft. Worming their way in with their sweet call and soft riches, they (the Chinese) drew distant peoples close to them.

Working translation

Original wording

Habitant ce pays j'entrai en relations avec le peuple chinois. L'appel du peuple chinois, qui nous donne sans peine tant d'or, d'argent, d'isigti, de soie(?), était doux, ses richesses étaient molles. En s'insinuant par leur doux appel et leurs richesses molles, ils (les Chinois) firent approcher d'eux le peuple lointain.

Editorial context

Bilge Qaghan tells his people that the forest of Otuken is the true centre of their realm. The Chinese give gold, silver and silk without trouble, but their words are sweet and their riches soft; with them they lure distant peoples close and many Turks who settled near China died. If the Turks stay in Otuken and send caravans, they will keep an eternal empire. It is the steppe ruler's own argument for staying nomads rather than moving into Chinese-style towns.

Bilge Qaghan (inscription composed by Yollugh Tegin), Kul Tegin inscription, 732, in Vilhelm Thomsen's French translation (1896)
See the original page (p. 116)

About Turks (Kok Turk)

Keep in mind

Thomsen marks 'silk' and some verbs as uncertain; the moral is the khan's political advice, not a neutral report.

Daily lifeMongolia6th to 7th century (Chinese record quoted 1892)

The dead in the yurt, faces cut, stones for the slain

'The body of the dead man is placed in the yurt, in front of which his children and other relatives of both sexes lay out as offerings horses and sheep that have been killed. They ride six times round the tent on horseback, gashing their faces with a knife, so that their tears are dyed with blood.'

Working translation

Original wording

»Le corps du défunt est placé dans la yourte, devant laquelle les enfants et autres parents du défunt des deux sexes déposent en offrandes des chevaux et des moutons qu'on a tués. Ils font à cheval six fois le tour de la tente, en se tailladant le visage avec un couteau, de telle façon que leurs larmes se teignent de sang.

Editorial context

Heikel quotes the Chinese account of Turk funerals: the body is laid in the yurt, relatives sacrifice horses and sheep, ride six times round the tent and gash their faces so that their tears run with blood. The body is burned with the dead man's horse and belongings, and the ashes are buried in autumn or spring. A statue of the dead is placed in a small building by the grave, with a painted record of his battles, and stones are set up for every enemy he killed, 'sometimes up to 100 and even 1000'.

Chinese annals quoted by Axel Heikel in Inscriptions de l'Orkhon (Helsingfors 1892)
See the original page (p. XVIII)

Keep in mind

Heikel's version says six circuits; Thomsen's version of the same Chinese text (1896) says seven.

Daily lifeMongolia and the Altai6th century (Chinese records quoted 1896)

Chinese annals: felt tents, grazing, and stones for enemies killed

'The Tujue let their hair hang loose, fold the flap of their garment to the left, and live in felt tents. They move from one place to another according to where they find water and grass. Their main occupation is raising herds and hunting.'

Working translation

Original wording

«Les Tou-kioue laissent flotter leurs cheveux, jettent à gauche le pan de leur vêtement, et habitent sous des tentes de feutre. Ils se transportent d'un lieu à un autre, suivant qu'ils y trouvent de l'eau et des herbes. Leur principale occupation est l'élève des troupeaux et la chasse.

Editorial context

Thomsen quotes Chinese annals (through Stanislas Julien's translations) on the customs of the Turks, the 'Tou-kioue': they wear their hair loose, fold their robes to the left, and live in felt tents, moving wherever they find water and grass. At burials they set up stones beside the grave, one for each enemy the dead man had killed, sometimes a hundred or a thousand. Thomsen says these notices describe a time somewhat earlier than the inscriptions.

Chinese dynastic annals (6th to 7th century) in Stanislas Julien's French translation, quoted by Vilhelm Thomsen (1896)

The nameMongolia (Orkhon valley)inscription of 732, about 6th-century events

The 'Thirty Tatars' mourn the first Turk khans

Weeping and lamenting there came from the front, from the side of the rising sun, the mighty peoples of the desert (that is, foreign peoples?): the Chinese, the Tibetans, the Apar and Apurim, the Kirghiz, the Three Qurıqan, the Thirty Tatars, the Kitay, the Tatabi … all these peoples came to lament and weep, so valiant had these khagans been.

Working translation

Original wording

Pleurant et se lamentant arrivèrent de l'avant, du côté du soleil levant, les puissants peuples du désert (c'est-à-dire étrangers?), les Chinois, les Thibétains, les Apar et Apourim, les Kirghiz, les Trois-Kourikans, les Trente-Tatars, les Kitaï, les Tatabi, … tous ces peuples vinrent se lamenter et pleurer: si vaillants avaient été ces kagans.

Editorial context

The Kul Tegin stone opens with the Turks' own origin story and then says that when the first khans, Bumin and Istemi, died, peoples came 'weeping and lamenting' from the east: the Chinese, the Tibetans, the Kirghiz, the Three Qurıqan, the Thirty Tatars, the Kitay (Khitan) and the Tatabi. This is one of the oldest written uses of the name Tatar anywhere, carved in stone by the Turks themselves, and here the Tatars are one steppe people among many.

Bilge Qaghan (inscription composed by Yollugh Tegin), Kul Tegin inscription, 732, in Vilhelm Thomsen's French translation (1896)
See the original page (p. 98)

About Otuz Tatar (the Thirty Tatars), a Mongolic people east of the Turks

Keep in mind

Thomsen marks several words as uncertain; the list of mourners is a royal boast carved some 180 years after the funeral it describes.

The nameEastern Mongolia (east and southeast of Lake Baikal)1896 (about the 8th century)

Thomsen: the Tatars of the stones were Mongols, not Turks

a well-known name that we meet here for the first time in history. In Chinese literature, Ta-ta appears at the beginning of the ninth century. This name belongs to the Mongols, or rather to one of their tribes, and not to any people of Turkic race.

Working translation

Original wording

nom bien connu que nous rencontrons ici pour la première fois dans l'histoire Dans la littérature chinoise, Ta-ta se présente au commencement du IXe siècle. C'est aux Mongols ou plutôt à une de leurs tribus que revient cette dénomination, et non à aucun peuple de race turque

Editorial context

In his notes Thomsen comments on 'Otuz Tatar' (Thirty Tatars) and 'Toquz Tatar' (Nine Tatars): the name meets us here 'for the first time in history', appears in Chinese books only in the ninth century, and belongs to the Mongols or one of their tribes, not to a Turkic people. He places them between the Oghuz (Uighurs) to the west and the Khitan to the east, near the Kerulen river. So the original Tatars were a real Mongolic group on the edge of the Turk empire.

Vilhelm Thomsen, commentary in Inscriptions de l'Orkhon dechiffrees (1896)
See the original page (p. 140)

Keep in mind

Thomsen's 1896 identification; later scholars still debate how Mongolic the eighth-century Tatars were.

The nameMongoliac.723 (Bilge Qaghan's 33rd year)

'Nine Tatars' join the Oghuz against the khan

I went on campaign and devastated their houses and their buildings. The [Oghuz] people allied with the Nine Tatars, and they came. Near Agu I fought two great battles; I destroyed their army and there I enslaved their people.

Working translation

Original wording

j'entrai en campagne et dévastai leurs maisons et leurs bâtiments. Le peuple [ogouz] s'allia avec les Neuf-Tatars, et ils vinrent. Près d'Agou, je livrai deux grandes batailles; je dévastai leur armée et j'asservis là leur peuple.

Editorial context

In the Bilge Qaghan inscription the khan says he campaigned against the Oghuz and 'devastated their houses and their buildings', and that the Oghuz then allied with the Nine Tatars and came against him. He fought two great battles at Agu, destroyed their army and enslaved their people. The Tatars appear here as a real political ally of the Oghuz, and the Oghuz are said to have houses and buildings.

Bilge Qaghan inscription, 735, in Vilhelm Thomsen's French translation (1896)

The nameYenisei (Khakassia)1895 (about 6th to 16th century)

'Abakan Tatars': the Kirghiz' descendants in 1895

When the Russians came to know them in the sixteenth century in the southern part of the Yenisei province, the Kirghiz were complete Turks, and even now descendants of the Kirghiz form a large part of the Turkic population of that area, the Abakan Tatars and Soyots.

Working translation

Original wording

Als die Russen sie im XVI. Jahrhundert im südlichen Theile des Jenisseischen Gouvernements kennen lernten, waren die Kirgisen vollkommene Türken und noch jetzt bilden Nachkommen der Kirgisen einen grossen Theil der türkischen Bevölkerung desselben Gebietes, der Abakan-Tataren und Sojonen.

Editorial context

Radloff notes that the Yenisei inscriptions were left mostly by the Kirghiz, and that their descendants still form a large part of the Turkic population there, 'the Abakan Tatars and Soyots'. In the nineteenth century Russian usage called many Siberian Turkic groups 'Tatars'. The same people who carved runic stones in the early Middle Ages were called Tatars by the time Radloff wrote.

Wilhelm Radloff, Die alttuerkischen Inschriften der Mongolei (1895)
See the original page (p. 301)

About Kirghiz (Khakas), called Abakan Tatars in Radloff's time

Keep in mind

Radloff's own ethnographic judgement; his claim that the Kirghiz were first a non-Turkic people rests on a Chinese remark about red hair and blue eyes.

Strange talesKhosho Tsaidam, Orkhon valley (Mongolia)1890 (visit)

Headless statues and skull cups

These, like all the other statues found near the first and second monuments, have no heads, which is not surprising to those who know the ancient customs of the peoples of Mongolia, among whom it was the practice to carry enemies' heads long distances as trophies. The skulls of chiefs killed in war were even made into cups.

Working translation

Original wording

Celles-là comme toutes les autres statues trouvées auprès de ce 1er monument et du 2e, n'ont pas de tête, ce qui n'a rien d'étonnant pour ceux qui connaissent les anciennes moeurs des peuples de la Mongolie, chez lesquels on avait coutume d'emporter à de longues distances les têtes des ennemis en guise de trophées. On façonnait même en coupes les crânes des chefs tués à la guerre.

Editorial context

Heikel found that all the carved stone figures at the first two monuments had lost their heads. He explains this by old Mongolian customs: enemies' heads were carried off as trophies, chiefs' skulls were made into drinking cups (the Irkutsk museum held one), and stones set round a grave stood for enemies killed. So attackers would naturally knock the heads off figures that stood for Turks. His explanation is a guess drawn from old accounts, not something he saw happen.

Axel Heikel, Inscriptions de l'Orkhon (Helsingfors 1892)
See the original page (p. VIII)

The fine print

How we know

official or contemporary record An official document or a record made at the time.

official record: contemporary royal funerary inscriptions of the Turk qaghanate, in Old Turkic runiform script with a Chinese text on the Kul Tegin stele; the earliest self-designation 'Turk' and the earliest Turkic-language description of the steppe polities (Toquz Oghuz, Qirqiz, Qurıqan, Otuz Tatar, Qitany, Tatabi)

Research notes
  • Readings of 1894-1896 are superseded (Tekin 1968, Berta 2004, Aydin 2012) but the editions remain the base; Radloff and Thomsen disagreed on many readings. The Chinese face of the Kul Tegin stele (732) is a separate Tang text.
  • Uyghur (Turfan) documents: only F. W. K. Mueller's Uigurica (IA Muller_uigurica, 1908) was located; Radloff's Uigurische Sprachdenkmaeler (1928) not found open. Left as a gap.
  • Further copies: archive.org/details/worterverzeichnisienissei (Donner 1892 Yenisei glossary)

Why it matters

The one class of steppe source with fixed dates and provenance: dated eighth-century monuments in which the Turks name themselves, their neighbours (including the 'Thirty Tatars') and their empire's extent, independent of Chinese or Islamic mediation. They are the terminus a quo for the name 'Tatar' in a Turkic-language document.
