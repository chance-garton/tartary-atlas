The Siberian Chronicles: Yermak and the fall of Kuchum's khanate

Seventeenth-century Russian chronicles of the Cossack conquest of the Siberian Tatar khanate (1581 to 1585) and of the towns and rulers that came before it.

Full title Siberian Chronicles (Imperial Archaeographic Commission, 1907): Stroganov chronicle (Spassky, Tolstoy, Afanasiev copies), Esipov chronicle (several copies), Remezov chronicle and shorter Siberian annals

Сибирские летописи (Строгановская, Есиповская, Ремезовская и др.)

Savva Esipov (1636), anonymous Stroganov chronicler, S. U. Remezov and others; ed. L. N. Maikov and others for the Imperial Archaeographic Commission

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

This 1907 Archaeographic Commission volume gathers the main Russian accounts of Siberia's conquest: the Stroganov chronicle (1620s to 1630s), the chronicle of Savva Esipov, a Tobolsk church clerk (1636), and the chronicle associated with Semen Remezov (about 1700), with the 'Kungur' articles. None is by an eyewitness. Esipov drew on the Tobolsk archbishop's 1622 collection of Cossack memories and on local tradition about the Siberian khans; the Kungur-Remezov text adds folk stories heard from Tatars, Ostyaks and Cossacks. For the Tatars they give an origin legend of the Siberian rulers, the towns of Chingi-Tura and Sibir, Kuchum's khanate and its fall, and later Tatar memories of Yermak. The corpus holds the 1907 volume and an 1880 edition of the Kungur chronicle.

How they knew
Compiled from others
Who “Tartar” means here
The Siberian Tatars of the Tura, Tobol and Irtysh and their khans (Kuchum, his sons, the Taibugid princes), described as Muslims; the chronicles distinguish them from Ostyaks (Khanty), Voguls (Mansi), Samoyeds, Kalmyks and Mongols, and mention Bukharan merchants and a prince of the 'Kazakh horde'.
Why read it
They show what 17th-century Russians in Siberia believed about the land before them: a Muslim khanate with its own dynasties and towns (Chingiden where Tyumen now stands, Sibir on the Irtysh), a tribute system in furs, and Tatar songs, sacred stones and relics told to Russian writers.
Watch out
Written 40 to 120 years after the conquest by the victors' church and military circles; they glorify Yermak and mix Cossack memory, Tatar legend and miracle stories. Dates and names in the dynastic legend are muddled (Chinggis Khan appears as a local commoner). The OCR of both volumes is fair and has been normalized.

CitiesTyumenLegendary (Chingi-Tura existed by the 14th to 15th centuries)

Chingiden, the Tatar town where Tyumen stands

Editorial contextAfter conquering peoples along the Irtysh and Ob for Chingis, Taibuga asked leave to settle where he liked. He went with his household to the Tura river, 'built a town there and named it Chingiden; now on that place is the town of Tyumen'. Another page of the same volume says the Russian voevodas built Tyumen in 1586 'where before there was a Tatar town, Chingidi'. The Tatar builder and the later Russian town are named in sequence.

Prince Taibuga went off with all his household to the river Tura, and there built a town and named it Chingiden; and now on this place is the town of Tyumen.

CitiesIsker (Sibir), near TobolskAbout 1495 (legendary retrospect)

Sibir, 'the chief town', built on the Irtysh

Editorial contextEsipov continues: Taibuga's descendant Mamet killed Upak (Ibak, khan of Tyumen, whom Esipov calls 'the Kazan tsar'), destroyed his own town of Chingiden, went deeper into Siberia and built a town on the Irtysh, naming it Sibir, 'that is, the chief one'. This is the Tatar capital also called Isker or Kashlyk, which Yermak took in 1582. The Russian name for the whole region comes from this Tatar town.

Mamet killed Upak, the Kazan tsar, and destroyed his own town Chingiden and went away from there into the interior of the Siberian land and built himself a town on the river Irtysh and named it the town of Sibir, that is, the chief one; and the tsar lived in it many years

Original wording

Маметъ Казанского царя уби Упака и градъ свой Чингиденъ разруши и отиде оттуду внутрь Сибирския земли и постави себѣ градъ на рѣке Иртише и назва его градъ Сибирь, сиречь началный; и живяше в немъ царь лѣта многа

Savva Esipov, Siberian Chronicle (1636), in Sibirskie letopisi (1907)

About Mamet (Muhammad), Taibugid prince; Upak (Ibak), 'the Kazan tsar'

BuildingsVagai River (Irtysh)August 1585

Kuchum's hidden ford of stone and sand

Editorial contextBefore Yermak's last night, the chronicle explains the trap: across a swampy stream near the Vagai, Kuchum had built a broad ford, wide enough for three or four carts abreast, in one spot only, packing it firmly with stones and sand; anyone who missed the spot drowned. A Tatar condemned to death was sent across it to spy on the sleeping Cossacks. It is one of the few Tatar-built works the chronicles describe.

because Kuchum had made a broad ford, so that three or four carts could pass abreast, in one place, filled in firmly with stones and sand; and whoever did not hit upon it would drown.

Daily lifeTobolsk1588

Out hawking: Seydyak, a Kazakh tsarevich and Karacha

Editorial contextA year after the Russians founded Tobolsk on the hill opposite the Tobol mouth, the Tatar prince Seydyak, a Kazakh tsarevich named Saltan and Kuchum's counsellor Karacha rode out from the old Tatar capital Sibir 'for bird sport' (falconry) with five hundred Tatars. The voevoda invited them to a feast as if for peace talks; when the guests choked on the wine he had offered, he took it as a sign of treachery, had their men cut down and sent the princes to Moscow.

In the second year after the building of the town of Tobolsk, from the town of Sibir, from Chuvash, there came out for bird sport the Tatars Prince Sedyak and the tsarevich of the Kazakh horde Saltan and Tsar Kuchum's counsellor, the Tatar Karacha, and with them five hundred Tatars.

Daily lifeIrtysh River (Aremzian volost)1582 or 1583 (the chronicle's year 7090)

Tribute sworn on a bloody sabre

Editorial contextYermak sent the fifty-leader Bogdan Briazga down the Irtysh to bring the Tatar and Ostyak districts under the tsar and collect yasak (fur tribute). At the first district he stormed a strong little town, hanged the best 'mergens' (warriors) by the leg and shot them, collected tribute 'by the sabre', laid the bloody sabre on the table and made the people kiss it as an oath to pay every year. The terror, says the chronicle, silenced the whole district.

And coming to the first, the Aremzian district, he took the strong little town by battle, and hanged many of the best mergens by the leg and shot them, and collected the yasak by the sabre, and laid it, bloody, on the table, and ordered them to kiss it in faith to the sovereign tsar, that they would serve and pay yasak every year

Original wording

И пріѣхавъ въ первую Аремзянскую волость, и городокъ крѣпкій взялъ боемъ, и многихъ лутчихъ мергеней повѣсилъ за ногу и розстрѣлялъ, и ясакъ собралъ за саблею, и положилъ на столъ кровавую, и велѣлъ вѣрно цѣловати за государя царя, чтобъ имъ служить и ясакъ платить по вся годы

Daily lifeMouth of the Ishim RiverSummer 1585

A Tatar lament: 'five Cossacks'

Editorial contextAt the mouth of the Ishim there was a fierce hand-to-hand fight in which five of Yermak's Cossacks died. The chronicle says the Tatars still sang about it at their gatherings, weeping, with the refrain 'yanym, yanym, bish kazak', which it translates as 'warriors, warriors, five men defeated and ruined'; the song was called 'the tsaritsa's lament'. The Tatar words more likely mean 'my soul, my soul, five Cossacks'.

and about those five men the Tatars sing with weeping at their gatherings, in songs with the refrain: 'yanym, yanym, bish kazak, bish kazak', that is: 'warriors, warriors, five, five men defeated and destroyed'. And this song is called the tsaritsa's lament.

Working translation

Original wording

и о тѣхъ пяти человѣкахъ Татара поютъ съ плачемъ при бесѣдахъ въ пѣсняхъ припѣваючи: янымъ, янымъ, бишь казакъ, бишь казакъ, сирѣчь: воины, воины, пять пять человѣкъ побѣдиша и разориша. И ся пѣсня и словетъ царицынъ плачь.

The nameWestern Siberia (Tobol, Irtysh, Ob)1636 (Esipov's description)

Tatars keep the law of Muhammad; the Kalmyks, 'I do not know'

Editorial contextEsipov opens with the peoples living along the Tobol, Irtysh and Ob: Tatars, Kalmyks, Mongols, the 'Piebald Horde' (Selkups), Ostyaks and Samoyeds. The Tatars, he says, keep Muhammad's law; as for the Kalmyks, he honestly does not know their faith, since he found nothing written and could not find out. The Ostyaks and Samoyeds, he adds, have no law and worship idols.

Along these rivers live many peoples: Tatars, Kalmyks, Mongols, the Piebald Horde, Ostyaks, Samoyeds and other peoples. The Tatars keep the law of Muhammad; which law or tradition of their fathers the Kalmyks keep, I do not know, since I found nothing written about it and could not find out.

Original wording

По сихъ же рѣкахъ жителствуютъ мнози языцы: Тотаровя, Колмыки, Мугалы, Пѣгая орда, Остяки, Самоядь и прочая языцы. Тотаровя законъ Моаметовъ держатъ; Колмыки же который законъ или отецъ своихъ преданіе держатъ, невѣмъ, понеже писменно о семъ не обретохъ ни испытати возмогохъ.

The nameIshim RiverLegendary (before the 15th century)

Tsar On and a commoner called Chingis

Editorial contextEsipov's history of the Siberian rulers begins with a Muslim 'tsar named On' on the Ishim river, overthrown by a man 'from the simple people' named Chingis, who killed him 'like a robber' and took his realm. A servant saved On's son Taibuga, founder of the Taibugid princes. In this local legend the world conqueror Chinggis Khan has become a Siberian upstart, and the 'Ong Khan' of Mongol history has become a Muslim tsar.

on this river Ishim there was a tsar of Muhammad's law named On. And there rose against him, of his own realm, from the simple people, one named Chingis, and he went against him like a robber with others, and killed Tsar On, and Chingis himself took the realm.

Working translation

Original wording

на сей же рѣцѣ Ишимѣ бѣ царь Моаметова закону именемъ Онъ. И воста на него его же державы отъ простыхъ людей именемъ Чингисъ, и шедъ на него, яко разбойникъ, съ прочими, и уби царя Она, и царство самъ приемлетъ Чингисъ.

Savva Esipov, Siberian Chronicle (1636), in Sibirskie letopisi (1907)

Strange talesDemyanka River (Irtysh)1582 or 1583 (the chronicle's year 7090)

A golden 'Russian god' in a cup

Editorial contextYermak's men besieged Prince Demyan's big hill-town on the lower Irtysh, held by 2000 Tatars, Voguls and Ostyaks. A Chuvash who had been Kuchum's captive told them the defenders prayed to 'a Russian god, cast in gold, sitting in a cup'; they poured water in the cup and drank it, called it Christ, and said it had come from Vladimir's baptism of Rus. Sent to steal it, he reported that it stood on a table surrounded by burning fat and sulphur fumes. The Cossacks never found it.

One of them, a Chuvash who had been with Kuchum, a Russian captive, said: they pray, he said, to a Russian god, and that Russian god is cast of gold, sits in a cup, and they pour water into that cup and drink it, and call him Christ, and they say he was brought from Vladimir's baptism

Original wording

Одинъ же въ нихъ Чювашенинъ былъ у Кучюма, руского полону, сказалъ: молятся де они рускому богу и тотъ де руской богъ литой золотой, въ чашѣ сѣдитъ, и въ ту де чашу наливши воды пьютъ, и зовутъ его Христомъ, а сказываютъ де привезенъ отъ Владимерова крещенія

Strange talesTashatkan (upper Irtysh)Summer 1585

The sky-stone that makes cold, rain and snow

Editorial contextRowing up the Irtysh, Yermak's men came to the little town of Tashatkan, where, they were told, a stone had fallen from the sky, dark red in colour, and from it at times came cold, rain and snow. Yermak and his men marvelled at 'God's work'. Weather stones (the 'yada tash' of Turkic and Mongol lore) were believed to bring rain and snow.

And they rowed to the little town of Tashatkan; and in that town a stone fell from the sky … dark red in appearance, and from it, they say, at times there arises cold, rain and snow. And at this Yermak and his comrades marvelled at God's doing.

Working translation

Original wording

И погребоша къ Ташатканскому городку; и въ томъ городкѣ спалъ камень съ небеси … видомъ багровъ, и отъ него де временемъ восходитъ стужа, дождь и снѣгъ. И о семъ Ермакъ съ товарыщи дивился Божію дѣянію.

Kungur (Brief Siberian) Chronicle, Remezov tradition, 1880 edition

Strange talesCamp of the Kalmyk taisha Ablai1650 to 1651

Yermak's armour and the Tatars' vow of silence

Editorial contextIn 1651 the Tobolsk officer Ulian Remezov took Yermak's famous mail coat, reclaimed from Tatar princes, as a gift to the Kalmyk ruler Ablai, who kissed it and raised it to his head. Ablai then told his own version of Yermak's death: drowned at the ford, found and shot at, his blood flowing, his armour divided up, miracles from his armour and clothes, and a Tatar oath 'on pain of death' not to tell the Russians about him. Ablai said that as a sick child he was given earth from Yermak's grave to drink and was cured.

And Ablai began to tell stories about him, according to his own history: how he came to Siberia, and fled from Kuchum at the ford, and drowned, and was found, and was shot at, and blood flowed, and they divided up his armour and carried it off, and how there were miracles from the armour and the clothing, and how the Tatars made a vow on pain of death not to tell the Russians about him

Working translation

Original wording

И нача Аблай повѣсти дѣяти о немъ по своей исторіи, какъ пріѣхалъ въ Сибирь, и отъ Кучюма на перекопѣ побѣжа, и утопе, и обрѣтенъ, и стрѣлянъ, и кровь течаше, и пансыри раздѣлиша и развезоша, и какъ отъ пансырей и отъ платья чюдесъ было, и какъ Татара смертной завѣтъ положиша, что про него Русакамъ невѣщати

Kungur (Brief Siberian) Chronicle, Remezov tradition (reporting Ulian Remezov's embassy), 1880 edition

About Ablai, Kalmyk (Oirat) ruler; Ulian Remezov, Tobolsk envoy; Siberian Tatars

The fine print

How we know

compilation Assembled from earlier sources. The author did not observe it directly.

retrospective compilations (1620s-1700) of the Yermak conquest 1581-1585 from Cossack oral testimony (Kiprian synodik 1622), Stroganov family papers and Tobolsk records; not eyewitness

Research notes
  • Esipov and Stroganov chronicles share a lost common source (Miller vs Bakhrushin debate on priority); Remezov chronicle late and embellished; Kungur chronicle interpolated in Remezov.

Why it matters

The core Russian narrative sources for the fall of the Siberian khanate; they describe Kuchum, Isker and the Tatar princes of the Irtysh from the conqueror side, essential for testing claims about a 16th century Siberian state.
