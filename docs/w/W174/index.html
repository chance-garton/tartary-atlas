Captain Unkovsky's embassy to the Dzungar khan

A Russian artillery captain's day-by-day journal of ten months at the felt-tent court of the Dzungar ruler Tsewang Rabtan on the Ili river, 1722 to 1724.

Catalogue title Embassy to the Dzungar Khong Tayiji Tsewang Rabtan of artillery captain Ivan Unkovsky and his travel journal for 1722-1724 (Zapiski RGO po otd. etnografii X.2, 1887)

Посольство к зюнгарскому хун-тайчжи Цэван Рабтану капитана от артиллерии Ивана Унковского и путевой журнал его за 1722-1724 годы

Captain Ivan Unkovsky; ed. N. I. Veselovsky

Ivan Unkovsky, a captain of artillery, was sent by Peter the Great in 1722 to Tsewang Rabtan, ruler of the Dzungar (western Mongol, Oirat) state, whom Russians called the Kontaisha. His brief was to talk the ruler into Russian protection, gain leave to prospect for gold, and free Russian prisoners. He travelled from Moscow via Tobolsk up the Irtysh, lived beside the ruler's moving camp in the Ili valley from November 1722 to September 1723, and kept a plain, careful journal of audiences, festivals, trade, rumours and landscape. This corpus copy is N. I. Veselovsky's 1887 edition, with an introduction, archive documents and a letter by A. M. Pozdneev on Oirat history.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Unkovsky calls the Dzungars Kalmyks, never Tatars. His 'Tatars' are the Muslim service Tatars of Tobolsk and Tara who travelled in his suite, Siberian Tatar captives, and the Crimean and Kuban Tatars he describes to the Dzungar ruler.
Why read it
The best close-up of a great steppe state that Western maps folded into 'Tartary': a capital made of felt tents and reed fences, lama dances, wrestling, courts, new farms and workshops, and robbed burial mounds on the steppe.
Watch out
Unkovsky was a diplomat on a political errand and often repeats what interpreters, captives and Bukharan merchants told him; he calls the Kalmyks deceitful from childhood. Veselovsky prints variant readings from a Moscow copy in footnotes.

CitiesIli valley, Xinjiang (Dzungar headquarters)20 November 1722

A capital of tents laid out like streets

Riding to his first audience, Unkovsky entered the ruler's urga, the moving headquarters camp. He describes rings of Kalmyk and Bukharan tent-camps (kuri) set out 'like streets', which he was led around for about a verst, and a bazaar in front of the ruler's own tent. This is what the capital of a powerful steppe state looked like: a planned city of portable dwellings.

When we rode into the first kuri (tent-rings), which are set out like streets, Kalmyk and Bukharan, they led us among them for about a verst, not straight but around the Urga; and when we came to the front of the Kontaisha's Urga, where the bazaar is held, about a hundred sazhens short of it, Kashka said we must get off our horses here.

BuildingsIli valley, Xinjiang20 November 1722

The khan's felt house inside a fence of reeds and willow

The ruler of the Dzungars received the Russian envoy in a 'felt house' (a large yurt), set inside an enclosure woven from reeds and willow withies, entered through a door made of six planks. Unkovsky had to lift the felt door flap himself. The detail shows the materials of a steppe palace: felt, reed, willow and a little timber.

When we came within about 6 sazhens of the enclosure (kosha), which is woven of reed and willow around the Kontaisha's felt house, they opened the doors, which are joined from six planks.

BuildingsIli valley, XinjiangJanuary 1723

A New Year banquet in a felt tent 14 metres wide

At the Mongol New Year (Tsagan Sar, 'White Month') about 300 nobles feasted in one enormous round felt kibitka, some 20 arshins (about 14 metres) across. The lattice walls were hung with blue cloth and a fire burned on an iron hearth in the middle. Tea, meat and vodka were served and musicians played fiddles, long flutes and a three-stringed instrument.

The kibitka was of felt, very large and round, about 20 arshins across; inside, the lattice walls (on which the roof is fixed) were hung with blue cloth; in the middle was a hearth made of iron, on which wood was burning.

BuildingsKhorgos, Ili valley (Huocheng, Xinjiang)told on 9 November 1722; events c. 1388 by the Bukharans' reckoning

A Muslim saint-khan's tomb, and the wrestling match that converted his people

The Tobolsk Bukharans in Unkovsky's party told him of the tomb of 'Temer khan', a ruler of the Ili who, they said, had worshipped idols until a Muslim merchant threw his strongest wrestler three times. The khan then converted, his people were circumcised and threw the tassels from their caps into the Borotala river, damming it. Muslims built chambers over his grave and still brought offerings. Unkovsky wanted to see it but was not allowed. The tomb is generally identified with the mausoleum of the Chagatai khan Tughluq Timur near Khorgos.

For that reason Temer khan accepted the Muhammadan law, and all the people of his realm were circumcised and threw the tassels from their caps into the river Borotala, and supposedly dammed the river with them. And for this the Muhammadans revere Temer khan as a saint, and chambers have been built over his tomb, and to this day the Muhammadans bring offerings to his tomb, and there are guards at that tomb.

Working translation

Original wording

Того ради Темерханъ принялъ Махометской законъ, и всѣ его владѣнія люди обрѣзались и съ шапокъ залы (или кисти) въ рѣку Борталъ побросали, и будто оными рѣку запрудили. И для вышеозначенного махометане оного Темерхана, святымъ почитаютъ, а надъ гробомъ его полаты построены и приносятъ доднесь махометане приносы на гробъ его, и сторожи при томъ гробѣ имѣются.

BuildingsIrtysh steppe between Semey (Semipalatinsk) and YamyshevoNovember 1723

Opened burial mounds all over the steppe: whose were they?

On the way home Unkovsky saw many burial mounds dug open 'like cellars' across the steppe. The Dzungar envoy Darzha said the dead were of his own people. Unkovsky objected that by their religion Kalmyks burn their dead or give them to dogs and birds, and never bury them with gold, so the mound-builders must have been someone else. An early recorded argument about who built the kurgans.

Travelling across the steppe we saw a great many dug-open mounds … like opened cellars, visible all over the steppe. … He was asked: what people were buried here? He said: supposedly of their own people. To this he was told that by their faith they burn their dead and throw them in the water, give them to dogs and birds for food, and do not bury them with gold or with any wealth at all.

Working translation

Original wording

Ѣдучи въ пути степью, видѣли множество разрытыхъ бугровъ … яко погребы раскрытые, по всей степи видны. … Спрошено у него: Какіе люди тутъ погребены были? Оной сказалъ, будто ихъ народу. На сіе сказано ему, что по ихъ вѣрѣ умершихъ людей жгутъ и въ воду бросаютъ, собакамъ и птицамъ на кормъ отдаютъ, а съ золотомъ и ни съ какимъ богатствомъ не погребаютъ.

Daily lifeIli valley, XinjiangJanuary 1723

Horned masked dancers and stones thrown at China

Before the New Year the lamas performed a masked dance (the Buddhist tsam) before a great tent. Unkovsky describes masked dancers wearing caps with horns, one like a cow's, one like a wild goat's, cymbals, drums and long brass trumpets, and an offering blown up in a fire. At the end the whole crowd, the ruler among them, marched east and hurled stones, sticks and earth toward the east with a great shout, 'as if for victory over the Chinese', their enemy at the time.

Two men came out with covered faces, with two horns each on their caps; one had cow's horns, the other horns like a wild goat's … and all the people, seizing whatever came to hand (a stone or a stick or earth), threw it toward the east with a great shout, as if for victory over the Chinese; and the Kontaisha himself walked with them.

Working translation

Original wording

Вышли два человѣка, закрытыя лица, на шапкахъ по два рога было; у одного коровей, у другого яко у дикой козы … и весь народъ, ухватя что попало въ руки (камень или палку или земли), съ великимъ крикомъ бросали на востокъ, будто бы въ побѣду на Китайцевъ; притомъ и самъ Контайша шелъ.

Daily lifeIli valley and Yarkandreported September 1723

A Swedish prisoner casts cannon for the Dzungar ruler

A Russian soldier freed from Dzungar captivity told Unkovsky that a foreign sub-lieutenant, Renat, captured with the Russians in 1716, had cast seven bronze cannon and three mortars for the ruler, and that other captive officers had taught the Dzungars to make cloth and needles; one was said to be making velvet in Yarkand. It shows a steppe state importing skills through its prisoners.

he heard that the foreigner, sub-lieutenant of artillery Renat, who was taken prisoner with them at Koryakov, cast for the Kontaisha seven bronze cannon and three mortars. And at the same Kontaisha's Urga, Lieutenant Debesh and other officers taught them to make cloth and needles. And now it is said that this Debesh is making velvet in Erken (Yarkand).

Original wording

слышалъ, что иноземецъ штыкъ-юнкеръ Ренотъ, которой взятъ съ ними въ полонъ съ Корякова, вылилъ Контайшѣ семь пушекъ мѣдныхъ, три мортиры. Да у него жъ Контайши въ Ургѣ поручикъ Дебешъ и другіе офицеры научили дѣлать суконъ, иголъ. А нынѣ слышно, что оной Дебешъ дѣлаетъ въ Эркени бархатъ.

Daily lifeIli valley, Xinjiang1723

Wrestlers in rolled-up trousers

Invited to watch wrestling, Unkovsky sat near the ruler under awnings on tall poles. He describes bouts between the ruler's side and his son Galdan Tseren's side, with bets and judges who wrote down the results. The wrestlers came out bare but for trousers rolled above the knee, raised their arms, glared to look fearsome, and gripped each other by the trousers. A bout only counted when a man was thrown flat on both shoulders.

And so from both sides the wrestlers come out naked, only in trousers rolled up well above the knee. And when they are about four sazhens from each other they raise their arms, and, rolling their eyes (to make themselves look fearsome), slowly close in; and when they meet they bend forward and seize each other by the trousers

Daily lifeIli valley, Xinjiang1723

No bread, only meat and fermented milk; the Tatars will drink it

Instead of flour the Dzungars sent the embassy chigan, fermented milk, saying they had no grain and ate only meat and drank chigan. Unkovsky answered that some of his people would drink it and some would not, since a Christian fast was about to begin: during it nobody but the Tatars would drink chigan. The 'Tatars' here are the Muslim service Tatars of Tobolsk and Tara travelling with him.

and instead of flour and groats they brought chigan, declaring that they have no bread now, and their people are not eating now, only meat, and drink chigan. They were told that some of our people will drink chigan and some will not; and especially since from tomorrow our fast begins … and nobody except the Tatars will drink chigan

Daily lifeDzungar khanate (Ili valley)1722 to 1724

Verbal courts and savage punishments

In his closing description of the Dzungar state Unkovsky says the ruler took no great decision without his council of zaisangs, and that the court (zargo) of ten or more zaisangs judged cases orally, not in writing. Capital sentences were carried out by flogging to death or by tying limbs to horses' tails, though he adds that such executions were rare.

They decide cases not in writing but orally. If they condemn someone to death for serious matters, some they kneel down and flog to death with whips; others they tie by the hands and feet to horses' tails, and the horses, running, tear off those people's arms and legs. However, such executions happen rarely among them.

Working translation

Original wording

Суды рѣшатъ не письменно, но на словахъ. Ежели за важныя дѣла кого на смерть осудятъ: иныхъ, поставя на колѣнки, плетьми до смерти убиваютъ; иныхъ же, привязавъ къ лошадинымъ хвостамъ руки и ноги, и тако оныя лошади на бѣгу тѣмъ людямъ руки и ноги отрываютъ. Однакожъ, рѣдко у нихъ оныя казни случаются.

Daily lifeDzungar khanate (Ili valley and Yarkand)1720s

Nomads turning farmers on the khan's orders

Unkovsky notes that thirty years earlier the Kalmyks had little grain because they did not know how to plough, but now fields were multiplying: not only their subject Bukharans (settled Turkestani farmers) but many Kalmyks had taken up farming by the ruler's order. He lists wheat, millet, barley and rice, and melons, apricots, grapes and pomegranates from Yarkand and the other towns under Dzungar rule.

Some thirty years before this they had little grain, because they did not know how to plough. Now their fields multiply by the hour, and not only the subject Bukharans sow grain, but many Kalmyks are taking up ploughing, because there is an order from the Kontaisha about it.

Original wording

Предъ симъ временемъ лѣтъ за тридцать хлѣба мало имѣли, понеже пахать не умѣли. Нынѣ пашни у нихъ отъ часу умножаются, и не токмо подданные Бухарцы хлѣбъ сѣютъ, но и Калмыки многіе за пашню пріемлются, понеже о томъ Контайши приказъ есть.

The nameDzungaria and Eastern Turkestan1723

The peoples of the Dzungar realm, none of them called Tatars

Unkovsky lists the peoples under the Kontaisha: the Dzungars proper ('of the left hand'), Kyrgyz, Uriankhai, Telengits and others, the town-dwelling Bukharans of Yarkand, the Buruts (Kyrgyz) of lake Issyk-Kul and the Barabinians, who paid tribute to both Russia and the Dzungars. No one here is called a Tatar; the same land appears on Western maps of the time as part of 'Great Tartary'.

Under the Kontaisha's rule are various peoples, namely: his own people, called the Dzungars (or 'of the left hand'), the Kyrgyz, Uriankhai, Telengits, Mingats, Khoits, Khoshuts, the Bukharans of Erken (Yarkand), who live in towns, though the better sort roam with him, the Buruts, who roam around lake Tuskel (Issyk-Kul), and the Barabinians, who pay tribute to His Imperial Majesty and to the Kontaisha.

Working translation

Original wording

Подъ его контайшинымъ владѣніемъ обрѣтаются разные народы, а именно: его настоящей народъ, имянуемой Зюнгоры (или лѣвой руки), Киргизы, Урянхайцы, Теленгуты, Мингаты, Каюты, Кошеуты, Эркенскіе Бухарцы (въ городахъ живущіе, а лучше при немъ кочуютъ), Буруты, которые около озера Тускелъ кочуютъ, Барабинцы, которые ясакъ платятъ въ сторону Императорского Величества и Контайшѣ.

Strange talesIrtysh steppe between Semey (Semipalatinsk) and YamyshevoNovember 1723

A Russian envoy guesses that ancient Russians lay under the mounds

Continuing the argument about the robbed mounds, Unkovsky told Darzha that in pagan times, over seven hundred years earlier, Russians had buried their dead with war-gear under great earth mounds, so perhaps some of 'our peoples' had once lived here. He blamed the digging on Kalmyks, Ostyaks or Kazakhs, and said Russian law punished grave-robbers with death. Darzha, doubtful, replied that his people had only had writing for a hundred years and could not know. A striking early example of a claim about who once lived on the steppe, made on the spot and without evidence.

Among us people are buried with their wealth, and especially in ancient times, before the Christian law, more than seven hundred years ago, they were buried with all their war-gear and great mounds of earth were heaped over them; and it may be that in ancient times some of our peoples lived in these places … [Darzha] said that among them writing began only a hundred years ago.

Working translation

Original wording

У насъ погребаются люди съ богатствомъ, а паче что въ древнія времена еще до христіанского закона, лѣтъ слишкомъ за семь сотъ погребалися со всѣмъ военнымъ уборствомъ, и насыпали на нихъ великіе бугры земли, и можетъ быть, что въ древнія времена нѣкоторые изъ нашихъ народовъ въ сихъ мѣстахъ жили … сказалъ, что у насъ де только сто лѣтъ какъ грамота началась
