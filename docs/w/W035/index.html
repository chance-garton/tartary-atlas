Herberstein's Notes upon Russia (Muscovy and its Tartar neighbours)

A Habsburg ambassador's description of Muscovy in 1517 and 1526, with a long chapter on the Tartars of Kazan, the Nogai steppe, Astrakhan and the Crimea.

Full title Notes upon Russia (Rerum Moscoviticarum Commentarii)

Sigismund von Herberstein

Read the 15 passagesOpen the original book

Sigismund von Herberstein (1486-1566), a diplomat from Carniola, went to Moscow as envoy of the Holy Roman Emperor in 1517 and 1526. His Latin Rerum Moscoviticarum Commentarii (1549) became Western Europe's standard book on Russia. He combined what he saw at court with Russian chronicles, a Russian route book for Siberia, and interviews with soldiers, merchants and exiles. His Tartar chapter covers origins, the hordes, food, horses, warfare, law, Kazan's wars with Moscow, the 1521 Crimean raid on Moscow, the Nogais, Astrakhan and the Crimean khans. This corpus copy is R. H. Major's two-volume English translation for the Hakluyt Society (1851-52), with Major's notes.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Muslim Turkic-speaking peoples of the steppe and Volga: the Kazan Tatars, the Nogai, the Crimean (Taurida, Precop) khanate, Astrakhan, and the 'Savolha' (Great Horde) khans descended from the Golden Horde. He says outright that the name covers many different nations.
Why read it
The classic Renaissance portrait of the Tartars as Muscovy's neighbours and rivals, full of concrete detail on horses, food, tactics and politics, plus the famous 'vegetable lamb'.
Watch out
Much is hearsay from Russians who were at war with the Tartars, so hostile stereotypes (no justice, gluttony, huge captive numbers) are common. The Siberian marvels come from a Russian itinerary he translated and did not believe.

What it says: 15 passages

CitiesKazan1517-1526

Kazan: Tartars who farm, trade and live in houses

The kingdom of Kazan, with the city and fortress of the same name, is situated on the further bank of the river Volga, nearly seventy miles below Lower Novogorod. … These Tartars are more civilized than the rest, in as much as they cultivate their lands, live in houses, and carry on various branches of merchandize.

Editorial context

Herberstein sets the Kazan Tartars apart from the steppe herders: their kingdom has a city and fortress on the Volga, an army of 30,000 largely made up of Cheremis and Chuvash archers, and settled farming and trade. He says Moscow controlled their kings largely through the river routes and trade they depended on.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

CitiesMoscow (Kremlin)late 15th century

The Tartar embassy house inside the Kremlin

There was within the citadel of Moscow a house in which the Tartars lodged for the purpose of learning what was going on at Moscow, and as this also gave great offence to his wife, she sent messengers with liberal presents to the queen of the Tartars, begging her to give up that house to her

Editorial context

Even the mighty Ivan III, Herberstein says, had to go out and stand before seated Tartar envoys. The Tartars kept a house inside the Moscow citadel. His Byzantine wife Sophia, claiming a dream, persuaded the Tartar queen to give the plot for a church, and the Tartars were never given a house in the fortress again.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

CitiesMoscowJuly 1521

Moscow in panic as the Crimean khan approaches

On the 29th of July the Tartars made a farther advance, and devastated the country with fire in all directions ; and such was the terror which they inspired amongst the people of Moscow, that they had little confidence in their security even in the city and the fortress.

Editorial context

In 1521 the Crimean khan Mehmed Giray and his brother, the khan of Kazan, raided up to Moscow. Herberstein, drawing on witnesses including German gunners, describes the Grand Duke fleeing (reportedly hiding under a haystack) and crowds crushing each other at the fortress gates. The city bought off the khan with gifts, above all mead, and a written promise of tribute.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

CitiesAstrakhan1517-1526

Astrakhan, the great emporium of the Tartars

Astrachan is a wealthy city, and the great emporium of the Tartars, which gives its name to all the surrounding country. It lies on this side of the Volga, near to its mouth, ten days' journey below Kazan.

Editorial context

Herberstein calls Astrakhan a wealthy city and the Tartars' main trading hub near the Volga delta, which he pictures splitting into some seventy branches. On the same page he lists forest-dwelling Siberian Tartars and says the Kalmyks are called that because they alone let their hair grow.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1517-1526

'May you stay in one place like a Christian'

They never stay for any length of time in one spot, for they consider it a great calamity to be obliged to remain long in the same place ; hence, when they are angry with their children, and wish to utter a heavy imprecation against them, they are accustomed to say, " may you abide in one place continually like a Christian, and inhale your own stink !"

Editorial context

Herberstein reports that the roving Tartars thought staying put was a misfortune, and that their worst curse on a child was to wish it a fixed home like a Christian's. He is careful to add that Tartars who live in towns and cities live differently, so he is describing the herders, not all Tartars.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)
See the original page (p. 56)

About Nomadic Tartars (contrasted with town dwellers)

Keep in mind

BuildingsKazanJuly 1524

Kazan's wooden fortress burns

In the meanwhile the fortress of Kazan, which was built of wood, was set on fire by some of the Russians who had been bribed for that purpose, and was burnt to the ground under the eyes of the Russian army.

Editorial context

During a Russian campaign against Kazan, bribed Russians set fire to the Tartar fortress, which was built of wood, and it burned to the ground in sight of the Russian army. Herberstein, relying on soldiers' reports, scorns the Russian commander for letting the Tartars rebuild it. It is a clear statement that the Khanate's fortress in the 1520s was timber, not stone.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1517-1526

Drinking their horses' blood, prizing mare's milk

Moreover, if during a long ride they are troubled with hunger or thirst, it is a practice to lance the veins of the horses on which they sit, and relieve their craving by drawing their blood ; and they think that this is an advantage to the animals. As they nearly all wander on uncertain tracks, they are accustomed to direct their course by the observation of the stars

Editorial context

On long rides, Herberstein was told, Tartars open a vein in their horse and drink the blood, believing it does the animal good. They steer by the Pole Star, which they call the 'iron nail'. On the same page he describes their love of mare's milk and how chiefs share out the entrails of a slaughtered animal and lick the knife clean.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

Daily lifeSteppe frontier of Muscovy and Lithuania1517-1526

The feigned flight, which they call a dance

they do not, however, continue this mode of warfare long, but pretending flight, take an opportunity while their enemies are pursuing them to discharge their arrows backwards, and then, when the ranks of the enemy are broken, turn their horses suddenly round and attack them.

Editorial context

Herberstein explains the classic steppe tactic: attack from a distance, pretend to flee, shoot backwards at the pursuers, then wheel round and charge. The Tartars fight in a wheeling circle rather than a battle line and, he says, call this manoeuvre 'a dance'. Without shield, lance or helmet they avoid close combat in narrow places.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

The name1517-1526

One name for many nations

He who attempts to describe the Tartars will have to describe many races ; for they derive this name from one sect alone, while they consist of various nations lying wide apart from each other.

Editorial context

Herberstein warns his readers that 'Tartar' is not the name of one people. It is a label drawn from one group and stretched over many nations living far apart. It is a useful early statement that the word was an umbrella term.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

The nameKalka river (southern Ukraine)legend retold 1549

Moabites from the desert: a Russian origin legend

They say that the Moabites, who were afterwards called Tartars, and who differed from the rest of mankind in language, manners, and dress, came to the river Calka ; but that no one knew whence they came, or what religious doctrine they held.

Editorial context

From the Russian chronicles Herberstein copies a story that the Tartars were 'Moabites' who came out of the north-eastern deserts, driven by a prophet named Gideon who preached the end of the world. The chronicle's date of AD 1025 for the Kalka battle is wrong (it was 1223), and the editor Major notes the attribution to Bishop Methodius is an anachronism.

Sigismund von Herberstein, retelling the Russian annals (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

The name1517-1526

Hordes that hate being called Turks

The word " horde" among them signifies a concourse or multitude. But although each horde has its peculiar name, such as the horde of Savolha, Precop, Nahaisa, and many others, and all are of the Mahometan religion, yet they are highly offended if they are called Turks, and consider it a reproach, but delight in being called Besermani

Editorial context

Herberstein explains that 'horde' means a multitude, that the Tartars are split into hordes (Savolha, Precop, Nogai and others), all Muslim, and that they take offence at being called Turks, preferring 'Besermani' (Muslims). He then describes their looks: broad faces, shaved heads, and black hair kept only by the great men.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)
See the original page (p. 53)

The name1526

Khan, sultan, murza: Tartar titles explained

The titles of dignity amongst the Tartars are nearly as follows : khan, as I have said above, signifies a king ; sultan, the son of a king ; … mursa, the son of a duke ; … said, a chief priest.

Editorial context

Herberstein closes his Tartar chapter with a little glossary of ranks: khan (king), sultan (a king's son), murza (a duke's son) and said (chief priest), with other noble grades in between. The same titles run through later Russian records of Tatar nobles. He also names the four great councillors of the Tartar kings.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)

Strange talesLower Volga (lands of the Savolha khans)reported 1526

The vegetable lamb of the Volga

he had seen in the island a certain seed, somewhat larger and rounder, but not unlike the seed of a melon, from which, when planted, grew up something very like a lamb, of the height of five palms, and that it was called in their language " boranetz", which signifies a lambkin

Editorial context

Herberstein's most famous marvel: a Russian informant said his father, on an embassy to the Tartar king, saw a seed that grew into a lamb-like plant called 'boranetz' (little lamb) with wool, blood and hoofs, which grazed until the grass around it ran out. Herberstein admits it sounds incredible but repeats it because trustworthy people vouched for the wool. Editor Major adds that it was a fern (Polypodium baromez) trimmed to look like a lamb.

Sigismund von Herberstein, reporting Dimitry Danielovich (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)
See the original page (p. 74)

About Tartars of the Great Horde (Savolha)

Keep in mind

Thirdhand (informant's father); Herberstein calls it 'almost incredible'.

Strange talesMoscow to Kaffa (Feodosia)1521

800,000 captives and old men given to boys

But he took with him from Moscow so great a multitude of prisoners as would scarcely be considered credible ; they say that the number exceeded eight hundred thousand, part of whom he sold in Kaffa to the Turks, and part he slew.

Editorial context

After the 1521 raid the Crimean khan is said to have driven off more than 800,000 prisoners, selling many to the Turks at Kaffa. Herberstein repeats the lurid claim that old captives, worth little at market, were handed to Tartar youths for practice in killing. He flags the numbers himself ('scarcely be considered credible', 'they say').

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)
See the original page (p. 65)

About Crimean Tartars

Keep in mind

The figure is a rumour and far larger than is plausible; the slave trade through Kaffa itself is well documented.

Strange talesTrakai (Troky), Lithuania1517 or 1526

Dining with the last khan of the Great Horde

was apprehended in the attempt and taken back to the castle of Troky, four miles from Vilna, where I saw him and dined with him. This was the termination of the reign of the kings of Savolha, and together with them ended the race of the kings of Astrachan, who derived their origin from the same royal line.

Editorial context

Herberstein says the Crimean khans came from the old 'Savolha' royal line and fought its last king, Sheikh Ahmed. Defeated, the khan fled to Lithuania, was held in the island castle of Trakai, and there Herberstein met him at dinner. He writes that this ended the line of the Great Horde kings, heirs of the Golden Horde.

Sigismund von Herberstein (translated by R. H. Major, 1851-52)
See the original page (p. 79)

About Sheikh Ahmed (Scheachmet), khan of the Great Horde (Savolha)

Keep in mind

His claim that the Astrakhan line ended here is contradicted a few pages later, where a king of Astrakhan regains his throne.
