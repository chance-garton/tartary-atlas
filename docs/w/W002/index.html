Strahlenberg's description of Russia, Siberia and 'Great Tartary'

A Swedish officer, prisoner in Siberia for over a decade, maps and describes 'Great Tartary', its peoples, tombs and languages.

Full title An Historico-Geographical Description of the North and Eastern Parts of Europe and Asia

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg

Read the 18 passagesOpen the original book

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (1676-1747), a Swedish officer captured at Poltava in 1709, spent the years to 1721 as a prisoner, mostly at Tobolsk, travelling in Siberia and joining Messerschmidt's expedition. His book (German 1730, English 1738) goes with his famous map of Russia and 'Great Tartary'. It mixes firsthand notes on Siberian peoples, tombs and antiquities with long learned arguments about the origins of names and nations, a history of Russia, an alphabetical glossary, and a 'polyglot table' of 32 'Tartarian' languages. This corpus copy is the 1738 London English translation.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
A deliberately wide label. He sorts the peoples 'called Tartars' into classes: Turkic Muslims (Crimean, Kazan, Nogai, Siberian Tatars, Uzbeks and others), Finno-Ugrians, Samoyeds, Mongols and Kalmyks, Tungus and Manchus, and others. He insists the Mongols and Kalmyks do not call themselves Tartars.
Why read it
The key eighteenth-century source for how 'Great Tartary' was mapped and named, written by someone who lived there and was openly sceptical of the label itself.
Watch out
Much is compiled from books (Abulghazi, Isbrand Ides, Witsen) or hearsay, and his etymologies (Gog and Magog, Behemoth, Kremlin) are speculative. Captivity in Siberia does not make every claim firsthand. The OCR is good but prints the long s as f.

What it says: 18 passages

CitiesMoscow and Crimea1730

'Kremlin' as a Tartar word for fortress

Cremmelin, or Crimmelin; the Name of the Czar's Palace, in the City of Muscow. This is a Tartarian Word, and signifies a Fortress, But not a Flint, as Kremm does in the Sclavonian Language. Thus the Precopensian Tartary is call'd, by the Tartars, Crimm or Cremm, on Account of a Rampart and a Mote, which was there in ancient Times,

Editorial context

In his alphabetical glossary Strahlenberg claims that 'Kremlin' is a Tartar word meaning fortress, and that the Tatars call the Crimea 'Crimm' because of the ancient rampart and ditch at Perekop. He adds that western Tartars and Uzbeks call the Great Wall of China the 'white fortress'. This is his own word-guessing, but it shows how 'Tartar' origins were read into Russian names early on.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

CitiesKemchik River, Tuva1730

A ruined city on the upper Yenisei

Kemtschyk, A River, which falls into the River Yenisei, near its Source, where formerly stood an ancient City, the Ruins of which are yet to be seen.

Editorial context

Under 'Kemtschyk' Strahlenberg notes the ruins of an ancient city where the Kemchik joins the upper Yenisei, and guesses that it was the place in Marco Polo where Kublai Khan's people went to meet the Venetian envoys. Elsewhere he says a Russian from Krasnoyarsk found old writings there, where a chapel once stood.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)

CitiesArgun River region (Dauria), Transbaikalia1730

The ruined city of Taiminzin

But that large and ancient City, which this Tartarian Author calls Alackzin, is the now ruin'd City of Taiminzin; … See the Description which Isbrand Ides gives of the ancient City of Taiminzin, (p. 75.) formerly the Residence of Chan Utay:

Editorial context

Discussing old silver mines on the Argun, Strahlenberg quotes the Dutch envoy Isbrand Ides that the Jurchens and Mongols once mined silver there. He then identifies the 'large and ancient city' of Alackzin in Abulghazi's Tartar history with the ruined city of Taiminzin that Ides described, once the seat of 'Chan Utay'.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg, author's footnote (English translation, 1738)

BuildingsNorthern and central Asia1730

Tartar towns of wood and sun-dried brick

…because most of the Tartarian Nations roving about, and the Residences of their Chams being never fix'd long in one Place, their Cities and Towns being also compos'd of Houses or Huts, built either of Wood or Bricks, slightly dry'd in the Sun, it is, therefore, no Wonder that … their Cities and Towns have been so destroy'd, that even the Ruins of them are no more to be seen.

Editorial context

Explaining why old place names in Marco Polo and Rubruck cannot be found on the ground, Strahlenberg says that most Tartar peoples roamed, their khans never stayed long in one place, and their towns were built of wood or sun-dried brick. Centuries of wars, he says, destroyed them so thoroughly that not even ruins remain. This is his general judgement, not a report of a particular site.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 16)

BuildingsSouthern Siberia (Yenisei, Irtysh and Tobol steppes)1711 to 1721

The 'pyramids' of Tartar kings on old maps

Some are only raised up of Earth, as high as Houses, … that, at a Distance, they appear like a Ridge of Hills. … In some Places these Tombs are entirely built of Stone. Hence we find in the ancient Maps of Tartary the Greater, a Number of Pyramids, with these Words, in Latin; viz. The Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings,

Editorial context

Strahlenberg describes the burial mounds of southern Siberia: some are earth heaps as high as houses, lying so thick that they look like a ridge of hills; others are ringed with rough or squared stones, and some are built entirely of stone. He explains that the 'pyramids' drawn on old European maps of Great Tartary, labelled 'Pyramidal Sepulchres of the Tartarian Kings', must mean these tombs, which are not really pyramids.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 365)

BuildingsBetween the Tobol and Ishim rivers, south of Tobolsk1720

Stone statues and ruined cities he never saw

…and I have also been told, by the Tobolskian Tartars and Russians, that, from this River, farther towards the West, South, and South-west from the City of Tobolsky, between the Sources of the Rivers Toboll and Ischim, whither very few People frequented, there were to be found great Numbers of Images, cut in Stone, of Men and Beasts; and that the Ruins of several Cities were discernible in those Desarts.

Editorial context

In a footnote Strahlenberg records that Russian regiments going up the Irtysh in 1720 found antiquities and 'Temples of Idols', and that Tobolsk Tatars and Russians told him of many stone figures of men and beasts and the ruins of several cities in the little-visited steppe between the Tobol and Ishim. He links them to obelisks said to have been set up by Tamerlane and to the seat of Oghuz Khan, and regrets he could never go to see them.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg, author's footnote (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 4)

Keep in mind

Daily lifeUpper Irtysh1720s

Printed scrolls from Kalmyk temples

…were brought from the Desarts of the Kalmucks, on each Side of the River Irtisch, upwards, out of the Pagan Temples and Tombs; And as to the Manuscripts, … they consist of Tanguthian, Mungalian and Kalmuckian Characters, not upon Parchment, but upon a thick Paper, made of Cotton or Silk, done over with black and blew Varnish; Upon which the Characters or Letters are not wrote, but printed,

Editorial context

Strahlenberg corrects another author: the idols and manuscripts sent to Europe did not come from Samarkand or the Caspian but from pagan temples and tombs in the Kalmyk lands along the upper Irtysh. The writings are in Tangut (Tibetan), Mongol and Kalmyk script, printed rather than written, in gold or silver letters on varnished cotton or silk paper. In a footnote he says he saw a wooden printing block for Kalmyk letters in the governor's house at Tobolsk in 1723.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 325)

Daily lifeYakutia (Lena River)1711 to 1721

Mare's-milk brandy thrown on the fire

…That, at a certain Time of the Year, they make a great Fire, into which they sprinkle a Sort of Brandy distill'd from Mare's Milk, towards the East, and thereby make an Offering to Heaven. I took an Opportunity of enquiring into the Truth of this Relation, of several of the Jakutians themselves, who all confirmed it.

Editorial context

Strahlenberg confirms, from questioning Yakuts himself, Isbrand Ides's report that at a set time of year they sprinkle a brandy distilled from mare's milk into a great fire, towards the east, as an offering to heaven. On the same page he says Yakuts wore their hair long, unlike their neighbours, and until recently buried a faithful servant alive with a dead master, a custom the Russians had forbidden.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 96)

About Yakuts, a Turkic people he counts among the Tartars

Daily lifeMongolia and the Kalmyk steppe1711 to 1730

Jumping through fire after childbirth

It is a Custom, to this Day, with the Mungals and Kalmucks, that when a Woman bears a Child, and is afterwards re-admitted into the Community of the People, she must, in the Presence of their Lama, or Priest, and her nearest Relations, jump Three Times thro' a Fire; Which they call the Purification.

Editorial context

A Mongol or Kalmyk mother, Strahlenberg says, is purified after childbirth by jumping three times through a fire before the lama and her relatives. He adds that they wear or keep a small three-headed idol, made of clay or metal, brought from Tibet and the Dalai Lama. He collects these customs to show a sacred number three.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 97)

Daily lifeKalmyk steppe; Tibet1730

The Kalmyk story of Shakyamuni

They say that 4000 Years ago, he was only a Soveraign Prince, in India; But on Account of his unparallel'd Sanctity, God had taken him up to Heaven alive; But the Place whither he was translated, none of them pretend to know.

Editorial context

In a footnote Strahlenberg records the Kalmyk account of 'Xacamuni', that is the Buddha Shakyamuni: once a prince in India, taken up to heaven alive for his holiness, whose celibate sons explain why lamas may not marry, and one of whom brought the religion to Tibet. It is a garbled but recognisable outline of Buddhist teaching as told to a Swedish captive.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg, author's footnote (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 409)

The nameMongolia1711 to 1730

The Mongols do not call themselves Tartars

But to return to the Mungal Nations: We must know, that these are the People whom the Chinese, with Respect to their Situation, call West Tartars, and others West Mungals: But they do not reckon themselves Tartars, neither would they, by any Means, be thought so by others.

Editorial context

Strahlenberg insists that the Mongols and Kalmyks, whom Europeans call Tartars, do not use the name and dislike it. In his view the name belonged to the 'original Tartars' near China and was stretched to cover the Mongols because they allied with them long ago. He adds that the Chinese used 'Tata' much as the Romans used 'barbarian'.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 51)

About Mongols and Kalmyks (Oirats)

Keep in mind

His explanation of how the name spread rests on his own reading of Abulghazi and Chinese sources, and on guesswork about Oghuz Khan.

The nameEurope and China1730

Blame Marco Polo and the friars

And because Marcus Paulus Venetus, when he was in China, was not rightly inform'd of the State of Things, he, as well as the Monks sent into Tartary, viz. Plan, Carpin, Rubriquis, &c. have themselves taken up, and misled us Europeans, with the Chinese Conceit, that all those People, whether Kalmucks or Mungals, must go under the Denomination of Tartars:

Editorial context

Strahlenberg blames Marco Polo and the thirteenth-century friars (Carpini, Rubruck) for spreading the 'Chinese conceit' that every steppe people is a Tartar. It is an early and explicit statement that 'Tartary' was an outsiders' label, not a nation's own name.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 52)

The nameEastern Siberia and Manchuria1711 to 1721

No river called Tatar

But then his Conjecture as to the Derivation of the Name Tartar, is erroneous, when he supposes they have taken it from a River call'd Tata, which, according to him, runs thro' their Country; … For after all possible Enquiry made among these Nations, I have been able to procure no Intelligence of any such Thing; Their Answer being constantly; that they know of no River call'd either Tata or Tatar.

Editorial context

Medieval writers such as Vincent of Beauvais said the Tartars took their name from a river Tata or Tatar. Strahlenberg asked the Tungus peoples in Siberia and reports that they had never heard of such a river. He himself thought the Tungus and the Manchus were the 'original Tartars' from whom the name spread to others.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 53)

The nameCrimea, Volga, Caucasus, Siberia, Central Asia1730

Turkic peoples who are called Tartars

Secondly, we find, in Europe, the Budziack, and Crimm-Kubann … Casan- and Cassinowan Tartars, and, in little Nagai, Nagaian Tartars: And in Asia and Great Nagai, the Astracan, Ovinian and Baskirian Tartars. … All these are Mahometans: … However, they, like the former, have, as it were, one Dialect with the Turks. All the Nations mention'd in this Paragraph are call'd Tartars.

Editorial context

Strahlenberg lists the Turkic-speaking peoples who 'are call'd Tartars': the Budjak, Crimean, Kuban, Kazan and Kasimov Tatars, the Nogais, the Astrakhan and Bashkir Tatars, the Tatars of Tyumen, Tobolsk and Tomsk, the Uzbeks, Turkmens, Kazakhs and others. On the same page he adds that, according to learned Bukharans, they took the name from the original Tartars without being descended from them.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 36)

Strange talesNorthern and central Asia1711 to 1730

Gog and Magog in Tartar speech

The same Tartars have also told me, that they use, in the like Manner, the Name Ma-Tschudi, … And when they, at the same Time, would denote the Heathen roving Scythian Nations, who inhabited the other Side of that Mountain, they call them Gaed-Tschudi, Ma-schudi or Ja-gougi and Ma-gougi; And these Words then signify the very same as Gog and Magog,

Editorial context

Strahlenberg says the Tobolsk Tartars told him they call peoples on this side of the mountains 'Ma-Tschudi' and those beyond 'Ja-gougi and Ma-gougi', and he concludes that these words are the same as the biblical Gog and Magog. Elsewhere he suggests that Gog was the Tartars' legendary ancestor Oghuz Khan. It is a learned attempt to find the Bible's northern nations among the Tartars.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 42)

Strange talesTibet1730

Prester John is the Dalai Lama

For the sixth Part, I have taken the Kingdoms of Thibet and Tanguth, where Dalai Lama, or Prester John, has his Residence, the Civil Government of which is entrusted to two Viceroys or Chans; but the Soldiers are a Sort of Kalmucks, call'd Coschuith, or Coschioth.

Editorial context

Dividing 'Tartary' into six parts for his map, Strahlenberg makes the sixth Tibet and Tangut, 'where Dalai Lama, or Prester John, has his Residence'. The legendary Christian priest-king of the East has here become the Dalai Lama, head of the Buddhism of the Mongols and Kalmyks.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 13)

Strange talesSouthern Siberia (Tara, Tomsk, Krasnoyarsk)about 1700 to 1710

Governors licensed the looting of tombs

…the Governours of the Cities of Tara, Tomskoi, Crasnoyahr, … used to give Leave to the Inhabitants, to go in voluntary Caravans, to these Tombs, in Order to ransack them, on Condition that … the Governour should have an Allowance, generally the Tenths. These Caravans, … used, … to knock to Pieces these choice Antiquities, and give to each Person his Share, by Weight.

Editorial context

Before the tsars took an interest, Strahlenberg says, the governors of Tara, Tomsk, Krasnoyarsk and other towns let townsmen go out in caravans to rob the steppe graves in return for a tenth of the gold and silver. The looters smashed the finds and shared the pieces out by weight. He owned only a broken fragment of a bell-metal mirror to show for it.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 365)

Strange talesSiberia1730

Mammoth from 'Behemoth'

And these Arabs coming into Tartary, and finding there the Relicks of some monstrous great Beasts, not certain of what Kind they might be, they called these Teeth Mehemot, which afterwards became a Proper Name, among the Tartars; and, by the Russians, is corruptly pronounced Mammoth.

Editorial context

Strahlenberg argues that the word 'mammoth' comes from the Behemoth of the Book of Job, brought into Tartary by Arabs who found the bones of huge beasts and called them 'Mehemot'. He says the Tartars call the animal 'Khir', and cites an exiled Russian priest who said the old Siberian form was 'Memoth'. He admits he could not find out what kind of animal the bones came from.

Philipp Johann von Strahlenberg (English translation, 1738)
See the original page (p. 403)
