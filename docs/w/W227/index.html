Thomas of Split on the Tartars (History of Salona and Split, 1241 to 1242)

A Dalmatian archdeacon's account of the Mongol invasion of Hungary and their pursuit of King Béla to the Adriatic, with a chapter 'On the nature of the Tartars'.

Full title History of the Bishops of Salona and Split, chapters 36-40 on the Tartars (first printed edition, in Lucius, On the Kingdom of Dalmatia and Croatia)

Thomas, archdeacon of Split; Ivan Lučić (Giovanni Lucio), editor

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

Thomas (about 1200 to 1268), archdeacon of Split in Dalmatia, wrote a history of the bishops of Salona and Split in the 1260s. Its chapters on 1241 to 1242 tell how the Mongols destroyed the Hungarian army at the river Sajó (Mohi), sacked Pest and chased King Béla IV to the Dalmatian coast, where Thomas himself saw the refugees and the Tartar army before Split and Trogir. He adds a chapter describing the Tartars' looks, arms and customs, from what he could learn from informants. This corpus has the first printed edition, in Ivan Lučić (Lucius), De regno Dalmatiae et Croatiae (Amsterdam, 1666; several scans and a 1758 reprint), plus Lučić's Italian history of Trogir (Venice, 1674).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongol armies of Batu and Kadan, whom Thomas notes call themselves 'Mangoli' in their own language; 'Tartar' is for him an outsiders' name.
Why read it
A vivid, fairly sober contemporary picture of the Mongols in Europe: their appearance, weapons, felt tents and hide boats, hidden graves and methods of war, alongside eyewitness scenes on the Adriatic coast.
Watch out
Only the Dalmatian scenes are eyewitness; the battle, the sack of Pest and the ethnographic chapter come from informants, and Thomas mixes in stock ideas about barbarians and the end of the world. The 1666 print survives in scans with heavy OCR errors, which have been corrected in the quotes.

CitiesTrogirMarch 1242

Kadan at Trogir: a muddy channel and a herald shouting in Slavonic

Editorial contextHere Thomas was close by. King Béla had taken refuge on an island off Trogir. Kadan rode round the town trying to get under the walls on horseback, but found the channel separating Trogir from the mainland too deep in mud to ford. He then sent a herald to the bridge who shouted in Slavonic, 'Kadan, prince of the unconquered army, says this to you': hand over our enemies. On the king's orders the guards gave no answer, and the army left.

Duke Kadan, having surveyed all the surroundings of the place, tried whether he could pass beneath the walls on horseback; but when he learned that the water by which the city is cut off from the land was unfordable because of the depth of the mud, he withdrew … [the messenger] cried out in a loud voice in Slavonic, saying: 'This says to you Lord Kadan, prince of the unconquered army.'

CitiesSuzdal1238

Suzdal, great Christian city, taken by trickery

Editorial contextThomas sketches the Mongol advance before Hungary: they had been held back by the Russians, circled the northern lands for twenty years, then returned with Cumans and other subject peoples. Their first great prize was 'a very great city of the Christians called Suzdal', which they besieged and took more by guile than force, killing its king George and many of his people. (Grand Prince Yuri of Vladimir-Suzdal died in 1238.)

And first they surrounded and besieged a very great city of the Christians named Suzdal, and having besieged it for a long time they took it, not so much by force as by fraud, and destroyed it, and put to death its king, named George, with a great multitude of his people.

CitiesPest (Budapest)April 1241

Pest overrun and burned

Editorial contextAfter the defeat at the Sajó, crowds gathered in Pest, 'a great township' on the far bank of the Danube, and began to dig a rampart and weave wicker fences, against the advice of Prince Coloman. The Tartars camped around it and shot it to pieces for two or three days, broke in and slaughtered the people; then they set fire to the whole town. Thomas says about ten thousand more burned in the Dominican house where they had taken refuge.

So the Tartar battle lines, having fixed their camps around the whole township, began to attack it from every side … and because the place was not well enough fortified, one day the Tartars burst in with a single rush, and there was no more fighting and no resistance at all.

Buildings1241

Felt tents, and boats of wicker and raw hide

Editorial contextThomas says almost no river could stop them, since they swam their horses across. If a river was too deep, they at once wove wicker baskets like small boats, stretched raw animal hides over them, loaded their baggage and crossed without fear. They lived in tents of felt and of leather. This is the whole of what he says about their dwellings: portable felt and leather, not buildings.

but if an impassable stream meets them, they at once weave baskets of wicker in the manner of skiffs, stretching raw animal hides over them; putting their baggage in, they get in and cross without fear. They use tents of felt and tents made of hides

Daily life1241

Broad chests, short legs, white skin: a Tartar portrait

Editorial contextThomas describes the invaders as terrifying to look at: short legs but huge chests, broad faces, white skin, beardless cheeks, hooked noses and small eyes set far apart. Their armour was made of ox hide in overlapping plates, light but, he says, impenetrable. It is one of the earliest European portraits of the Mongols, and notably it calls their skin white.

They present a very terrifying face: they have short legs but vast chests; the face is broad and the skin white, the cheeks beardless and the nose hooked, the eyes small and set wide apart. Their armour consists of coverings made of ox hides fitted together like plates, yet impenetrable and very safe.

Arma eorum sunt quaedam tegmina ex taurinis coriis laminarum more compacta, impenetrabilia tamen, & valde secura.

Daily life1241

Unshod horses like wild goats; mare's blood with curdled milk

Editorial contextTheir horses, Thomas writes, were small but strong, able to bear hunger and toil, running over rocks without iron shoes like wild goats and content with a little straw after three days of hard work. The men too cared little for food: they disliked bread, ate any meat, clean or unclean, and drank curdled milk mixed with horse blood.

their horses short but strong, enduring hunger and toil … They run over rocks and stones without iron shoes, as if they were wild goats; after three days of continuous labour they are content with a little fodder of straw. The men likewise care almost nothing about taking food; they feed on cruelty alone, loathe the use of bread, eat the flesh of clean and unclean animals alike, and drink curdled milk with horse's blood

Daily life1241

Graves trampled flat so no sign remains

Editorial contextThomas says the Tartars did not like to throw away their lives. If one of them fell in battle, the others at once carried him off, buried him in a very hidden place, levelled the mound and trampled the spot with their horses' hooves so that no sign of a grave could be seen. Hidden burial is also reported for Mongol rulers by later writers.

The Tartars themselves do not willingly expose themselves to death; but if one of them happens to die in battle, they carry him off at once, and taking him to a most hidden place bury him in the earth, levelling the mound and trampling the spot with the feet of their horses, so that no sign of a burial appears

Daily lifeCarpathian passes, HungaryMarch 1241

Forty thousand axemen cut the road

Editorial contextKing Béla had blocked the mountain passes into Hungary with barricades of felled trees. Thomas says the Tartars had forty thousand men with axes marching ahead of the army, cutting down woods, laying roads and clearing obstacles, so that the great barricades of fir and oak were crossed as if they were made of straw, then broken up and burned.

they had forty thousand axe-bearers who went before the army, cutting down forests, laying roads and removing every obstacle from the approaches. Therefore they crossed the barricades that the king had had prepared as easily as if they had been built not of a pile of huge firs and oaks but of light straw

Daily lifeHungary1241 to 1242

Tartar women fought like men

Editorial contextAmong the cruelties of the occupation, Thomas reports that the Tartars' women armed themselves like men and rode boldly into battle. He adds that they treated captive women even more savagely than the men did, killing good-looking ones out of jealousy and disfiguring others to make them servants. Mongol women travelling and sometimes fighting with the army are also mentioned by Matthew Paris's informants.

Moreover the women of the Tartars, girded with arms in the manner of men, charged boldly into battle like men; they raged more cruelly against captive women, for if they saw any with a more beautiful face, of whom they might in any way fear jealousy, they at once drew a sword and killed them

Working translation

Original wording

Praeterea mulieres Tartarorum virili more armis accinctae in pugnam audacter ferebantur, ut viri, hae in mulieres captivas atrocius saeviebant, si quas enim venustiori vultu cernebant, ex quibus zelotypiae motum possent aliquatenus formidare, statim extracto gladio perimebant

The nameMongolia1241

'In their own language they are called Mangoli'

Editorial contextThomas opens his chapter 'On the nature of the Tartars' by saying he will report what he could hear from people who had looked into the matter closely. Their land lies where the East meets the North, and in their own language they are called 'Mangoli', that is, Mongols. He adds that their country is said to border on 'further India' and names their king in a garbled form. So a Dalmatian writer of the 1260s already knew that 'Tartar' was not what these people called themselves.

Now I will tell a few things about the nature and dress of that people, as far as I could hear from those who investigated the matter more carefully. Their region lies in that part of the world where the East joins the North, and those peoples, in the proper usage of their own language, are called Mangoli. They say, however, that their land borders on further India

Working translation

Original wording

Nunc vero de natura, & habitu gentis illius prout ab his audire potui, qui rem curiosius indagarunt, pauca narrabo: Est enim regio illorum in ea parte orbis sita ubi Oriens conjungitur Aquiloni, gentesque illae secundum proprietatem linguae suae Mangoli appellantur. Ferunt tamen quod terra illorum confinis sit ulterioris Indiae

The name1241

'Tartar' from a river, or from a word for 'multitude'

Editorial contextThomas insists that 'Tartar' is not the people's own name. He offers two explanations he had heard: that they were named after a river flowing past their country, or that 'Tatar' means 'multitude'. The river story also appears in Matthew Paris; neither explanation is how historians now explain the name, which came from the Tatar tribe that the Mongols had defeated.

This name Tartars is not the people's own name, but they were so called from a certain river that flows past their region; or, according to some, Tatar means the same as 'multitude'

Hoc autem nomen Tartari non nomen est gentis proprium, sed a quadam aqua, quae illorum praeterfluit regionem sic appellati sunt, vel secundum quosdam Tatar idem sonat, quod multitudo

Strange talesSplitMarch 1242

Rumour: the Tartars will pile up a mountain to overtop the walls

Editorial contextWith the Tartars on the coast, Thomas says the people of Split started 'empty rumours'. Some said the Tartars were building huge siege machines to throw the towns down; others that they were heaping up earth and stones like a mountain, so they could stand higher than the cities and take them easily. Thomas himself calls these rumours vain and baseless: a record of fear, not of what happened.

They began to spread empty rumours, inventing vain and idle opinions. Some said that the Tartars were building huge machines and many instruments of war with which they were trying to throw down these cities; others asserted that they were heaping up a pile of earth and stones like a mountain, and so, rising above the cities, would easily capture them.

Working translation

Original wording

Coeperunt autem vanos agitare rumores, confingentes opiniones vanas, & inanes. Dicebant alii quod ingentes machinas, & plurima instrumenta bellica Tartari fabricarent, quibus Civitates has deiicere conabantur; asserebant alii, quod ad montis instar terrae, ac lapidum congeriem acervabant, & sic Civitatibus supereminentes, facile eas esse capturos.

Strange talesEurope1241

Scholars decide they are the peoples before Antichrist

Editorial contextAfter the Hungarian disaster, Thomas says, the whole world trembled. Even the emperor Frederick II was said to be thinking not of resistance but of a hiding place. Learned men searched old writings and concluded, above all from the prophecies attributed to Methodius, that these were the peoples who must come before Antichrist, and cities began fortifying themselves in case the Tartars marched on Rome.

Even Frederick, the Roman emperor, is said to have thought not of resisting but of a hiding place. Then many learned men, searching the old writings, conjectured, above all from the sayings of Methodius the Martyr, that these were those peoples who must come before the advent of Antichrist.

Working translation

Original wording

Ipse etiam Fredericus Rom. Imperator non de resistendo, sed de latibulo dicitur cogitasse. Tunc litterati plerique viri veteres scrutantes scripturas, conjiciebant maxime ex dictis Methodii Martyris has fore illas gentes, quae praecedere debent antichristi adventum.

Strange talesTrogir1242 (tradition recorded 1674)

The 'Tartars' Road' over Mount Vlaško

Editorial contextWriting four centuries later, the Trogir historian Giovanni Lucio (Ivan Lučić) explains why King Béla did not trust the island next to Trogir: the Hungarians knew that Tartars swam their horses across huge rivers. He then records a local tradition that the Tartars, leaving, rode past in the king's sight along the slope of Mount Vlaško, and that the road there had been called 'the Tartars' road' ever since, just as a nearby rock is still called the King's Rock.

the Hungarians, knowing that the Tartars were accustomed to cross very large rivers by swimming with their horses, feared that they would cross to the island of Bua as well … who, as they went away, passed in his sight along the slope of Mount Vlasko, having on that occasion left their name to the road, which from then on has been called the Tartars' road

Working translation

Original wording

sapendo gl'Ongari, che li Tartari erano soliti passare con Caualli à nuoto grossissimi fiumi, dubitassero, che passassero anco nell'Isola Bua … i quali nell'andar via passarono à vista sua sopra la costa del monte Vlasco, hauendo lasciato con tal occasione il nome alla strada, che d'indi in qua vien detta strada de Tartari

Giovanni Lucio (Ivan Lučić), Memorie istoriche di Tragurio ora detto Traù (Venice, 1674)

The fine print

How we know

firsthand The author saw the places or people described.

firsthand observation for 1241-1242: Thomas witnessed King Béla IV's flight to Split and Trogir and the Mongol pursuit along the Dalmatian coast; includes an ethnographic chapter 'De natura Tartarorum'

Research notes
  • Thomas relies partly on hearsay for the battle of Mohi; the 'De natura Tartarorum' chapter mixes observation with topoi. Modern editions: Rački 1894 (Monumenta spectantia historiam Slavorum meridionalium 26, not located on IA among labelled volumes) and Karbić/Perić/Sweeney CEMT 4 (2006).

Why it matters

Independent Dalmatian eyewitness of the 1241-1242 Mongol invasion, with a description of Tartar appearance, weapons and tactics, and of the pursuit of the Hungarian king to the Adriatic; complements Rogerius.
