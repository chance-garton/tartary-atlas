The History of the Kazan Tsardom: a captive's story of Kazan

A Russian account of the Kazan khanate from its legendary founding to its conquest in 1552, by an author who says he spent twenty years at the Kazan khan's court.

Catalogue title History of the Kazan Tsardom (Kazan Chronicler), Complete Collection of Russian Chronicles vol. 19

История о Казанском царстве (Казанский летописец) (ПСРЛ, т. 19)

Anonymous (author states twenty years captivity in Kazan); ed. G. Z. Kuntsevich

Read the 12 passagesOpen the original book

Written in 1564 to 1565 by an anonymous Russian who says he was taken captive, given as a present to the khan of Kazan, and served at his court for twenty years until Kazan fell in 1552, when he went over to the Moscow tsar, who had him baptized and gave him a small estate. He admits he found little on Kazan's origins in Russian chronicles and asked 'the wisest and most honoured Kazan people' and the khan himself. The book mixes legend (a serpent's nest burned by a sorcerer), Kazan's history under Ulu-Mehmed and his heirs, portents, and a triumphal account of Ivan IV's siege. The corpus copy is PSRL vol. 19 (1903), which prints several redactions in parallel columns; page references below are column numbers.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Kazan Tatars and their khans ('tsars'), whom the author also calls Saracens, pagans and barbarians; also the Golden Horde ('Great Horde'), Nogais, Crimean Tatars and Astrakhan princes. He distinguishes the Tatar ruling class from the Chuvash, Cheremis (Mari), Votiaks (Udmurts) and Mordva of the Kazan land.
Why read it
It is the only Russian book devoted to the Kazan khanate, and it credits the building of Kazan to named khans: a town on Batu's camping ground for tribute envoys, then a new timber fortress built by Ulu-Mehmed. It also shows how a Russian who lived there imagined its past, with serpents, sorcerers and a demon-haunted ruin of the 'old Bulgars'.
Watch out
The captivity claim cannot be checked, the early history is legend with muddled dates (it has Kazan founded in 1172 by a 'Tsar Sain' after Batu), and the whole book celebrates Ivan IV's conquest. Redactions differ; the OCR is fair, and quotes are normalized.

What it says: 12 passages

CitiesKazan13th century (legendary retrospect)

A town on the khan's camping ground, for the tribute envoys

The author explains why a Horde ruler built a town near Rus: after the Rus princes bought him off with gifts, 'Tsar Sain' decided to build a town at his nomad camping ground, for his own glory and as a stopping place for his envoys who rode to Rus every year to collect tribute. The book adds that Kazan was long called 'Sain's yurt' and that the khan often came to live there from his capital, Sarai.

And Tsar Sain gave up ravaging the Russian land, and wished to build a town near it at his nomad camping ground … for the glory of his name, and for the arrival and rest of his envoys going for tribute to Rus every year, and for the governing of the people of the land.

Anonymous author of the History of the Kazan Tsardom, PSRL vol. 19 (1903)

About 'Tsar Sain' (Batu's epithet Sain Khan) of the Horde

Keep in mind

Legendary history; the author confuses Batu (Sain Khan) with a later ruler and elsewhere dates the founding to 1172. Archaeology dates Kazan's origins to Volga Bulgar times.

The Bulgar towns dug up to their foundations

The first Russian capture of Kazan: Prince Iurii, sent by his brother Vasilii I, took the Bulgar towns on the Volga (Kazan, Bolgar, Zhukotin, Kermenchuk), raided the Golden Horde and 'dug all those towns up to their foundations', killing the Kazan tsar. The author says Kazan then stood empty for forty years. The passage lists real Volga towns of the Golden Horde era.

He went and took the Bulgar towns standing along the Volga, Kazan, and Bolgar, Zhukotin, Kermenchuk, and ravaged the Golden Horde, on the advice of the Crimean tsar Azigerei, and dug all those towns up to their foundations

CitiesKazan2 October 1552

Kazan taken, its khan seized alive

After the mines blew open the walls, the Russians stormed the city. The author describes the slaughter, the soldiers taking countless riches and leading away princesses, murzas' wives and children 'from the greatest to the simplest', and the capture of Khan Ediger-Mahmet, found on the wall and nearly killed before some Tatars cried out that he was their tsar.

And in the city the Christian people took uncountable riches, and into captivity a great multitude of princesses and murzas' wives and children; to put it simply, from the great down to the simple, they captured them all.

BuildingsKazanAbout 1438

Ulu-Mehmed's new timber fortress, stronger than the old

After his 'ice town' at Belev melted in the sun, the exiled Golden Horde khan Ulu-Mehmed crossed the Volga and occupied empty Kazan, 'Sain's yurt'. The surviving Bulgars asked him to protect them, and he built a strong wooden town on a new site near the old Kazan that Russian armies had ruined. Settlers came from the Golden Horde, Astrakhan, Azov and Crimea. The builder, material and reason are all named.

And the tsar settled in their dwelling place and built himself a strong wooden town, on a new site, stronger than the old one, not far from old Kazan, which had been ruined by the Russian army. And many barbarians began to gather to the tsar from various lands, from the Golden Horde and from Astrakhan, from Azov and from Crimea

A prefabricated wooden town floated down the Volga

To besiege Kazan, Ivan IV had a whole timber town made in advance upriver, carried it down the Volga on big Beloozero boats, and set it up quickly on a hill at the mouth of the Sviyaga, 15 versts from Kazan. The chronicle adds that churches, monasteries and houses were built inside. The fortress that ended the Kazan khanate was Russian-built and timber.

carrying with them a ready-made wooden town on great Beloozero boats, new that same year and skilfully made. And sailing … they came into the Kazan land to the river Sviyaga, to the place appointed for them

BuildingsKama River (near Yelabuga)1540s to 1551 (while the author lived in Kazan)

The Devil's Fort: an empty town of the old Bulgars on the Kama

The author describes an empty little town on the high bank of the Kama, which the Russians called the 'Devil's gorodishche'. A demon lived there, answering questions through soothsayers, and people of the Kazan land came to sacrifice; travellers on the river threw it some of their goods or risked drowning. He credits the ruin to the 'old Bulgars', whose sacrificial prayer-place it had been. Several manuscripts call it a 'small stone town'.

there is an empty little town standing on the high bank of the river Kama, which the Rus call the Devil's Fort, in which a demon lived, working illusions for many years; and it was a sacrificial prayer-place of the old Bulgars.

Daily lifeKazanAbout 1532 to 1552

Given to the khan as a present: twenty years at court

The author explains how he knew: for his sins he was captured by 'barbarians', taken to Kazan and given to the khan as a gift. The khan took him into his household to stand before him, and he stayed twenty years, until Kazan fell and he left for the Moscow tsar. He says he often questioned the wisest Kazan nobles and heard things from the khan's own mouth. Captives in service at a Tatar court were common.

For my sins it befell me to be captured by barbarians and taken to Kazan, and they gave me to the tsar of Kazan as a gift; and the tsar took me with love into his service, into his court, and set me to stand before his face. And having been there 20 years, after the taking of Kazan I left Kazan, in the name of the Moscow tsar.

Original wording

И бывъ тамо 20 лѣтъ, по взятіи же Казанскомъ изыдохъ исъ Казани, на имя царево Московсково.

Daily lifeKazanAfter 1552

A Tatar convert tears his beard

A lame Tatar from the Arsk district came to Kazan to be baptized, saying his legs were healed the moment he heard that the Moscow tsar had taken Kazan and filled it with churches. Warned not to return to his old faith, he tore out some of his beard and crumbled the hairs, saying he would sooner be crumbled like them. Later his kinsmen hacked him to death. It is a story of conversion and of Kazan Tatars' resistance to it.

The Tatar at once suddenly tore out with his hand part of the hair of his beard and crumbled it into small bits, saying: 'I have so understood the great power and mercy of your God that for his sake I am ready not only to suffer many sorrows, but even if I am crumbled like this, as I have crumbled my hairs, I will never renounce him.'

Original wording

The nameKazanLegendary retrospect

Who lived in the Kazan land: Bulgars, Cheremis and runaway Rostovians

The author says the khan drove the Rus out of the district and brought in 'a fierce pagan people' from the Kama, the Bulgar commoners with their princes and elders, as savage, he adds, as the 'dog-headed Samoyeds'. He also claims the Cheremis, called Votiaks, were Rostov commoners who fled there to escape baptism. It shows a Russian view of Kazan as a mix of peoples under a Tatar ruler, with a dose of prejudice and folk etymology.

and he brought from the Kama a fierce, pagan people, the Bulgar commoners, with their princes and elders, much like in savagery and evil custom to the dog-headed Samoyeds. He filled that land with such people, that is the Cheremis, called Votiaks; these, they say, are the Rostov commoners who fled there from Russian baptism

Original wording

Наполни такими людми землю ту, еже Черемиса, зовемая Отяки, тое жъ глаголють Ростовскую чернь, забежавши тамо отъ крещенія Русково

The nameKazanAugust 1551

The people of Kazan, rank by rank

When Kazan surrendered its boy khan Utemish-Girey and his mother Siuiumbike in 1551, the author lists everyone who submitted: mullahs, seyyids, sheikhs, hafizes and other Muslim clergy; ulans, princes, murzas, ichki (court nobles) and 'rear-court' Cossacks; and the subject peoples, Chuvash, Cheremis, Votiaks, Mordva, tarkhans and Mozhars. It shows Kazan as a Muslim state of many peoples, not a single 'Tatar' mass.

And the Kazan people, the mullahs, and seyyids, and sheikhs … and hafizes, and ulans, and princes, and murzas and ichki, and the rear-court Cossacks, and the Chuvash and Cheremis, and Votiaks, and Mordva, and tarkhans, and Mozhars, and all the Kazan land

Strange talesKazanLegendary founding of Kazan

The two-headed serpent of Kazan

Before the town, 'many say', the site was a serpents' nest ruled by a huge two-headed serpent: one head a snake's, which ate people, cattle and beasts, the other an ox's, which ate grass. The khan's sorcerer drew a line around all the snakes, heaped hay, reeds and wood on them with sulphur and pitch and burned them, and the stench killed many of the army's horses and camels. Then the khan built Kazan there. The author reports it as hearsay.

there lived, having made its nest, a great and terrible serpent with two heads, one a serpent's head and the other an ox's head; with one it devoured people and cattle and beasts, and with the other head it ate grass; and other serpents of every kind lay around it and lived with it.

Strange talesKazan1540s

A holy fool cries: 'Tatars will not live here'

Among the omens of Kazan's fall the author lists a Tatar holy fool who walked the streets shouting that Tatars would not live there, only Russians. The khan forbade killing him and had him shut in an empty house, but he broke out. The next chapters tell of a calf born in human shape that turned into an armed man, and a fish-net that brought up a live old man who urged surrender to Moscow.

A certain Tatar in Kazan, a holy fool, walking through the city, began suddenly to cry out and say without ceasing: 'Tatars will not live here, but Russian people.' The Tatars wanted to kill him; the tsar did not allow them to kill him, but ordered him shut up in an empty house.

Working translation

Original wording

Нѣкто Татаринъ въ Казани уродивъ, по граду ходя, нача напрасно вопити и непрестанно глаголати: «не жить здѣ Татаромъ, но Рускимъ людемъ». Татарове же хотяху убити его; не повелѣ имъ царь убити его, но въ праздну храмину повелѣ затворити его.

Anonymous author of the History of the Kazan Tsardom, PSRL vol. 19 (1903)

Card wording

mixed: author claims firsthand residence in Kazan 1530s-1552 and informant testimony from Kazan Tatars; early history of the Horde and Kazan foundation is legendary retrospective

Cautions
  • Authorship anonymous and claim of captivity unverifiable; uses Nikon chronicle, Stepennaia kniga and oral tales; editions differ by redaction (Kuntsevich 1903 vs Moiseeva 1954). Catalogue year 1903 vs OCR title page 1908 not reconciled (probably OCR error; PSRL 19 is conventionally dated 1903).

Why it matters

The principal Russian narrative of the Kazan khanate from its foundation legend to 1552, including the Horde origin story of Kazan and the claimed testimony of Kazan informants; a key text for the Volga Tatar polity absent from western Tartary literature.
