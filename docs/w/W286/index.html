Falck's contributions to the topography of the Russian Empire

A Swedish naturalist's expedition papers of 1768 to 1774, arranged by Georgi, covering the steppes, their ruins and the Tatar, Bashkir, Kirghiz and Kalmyk peoples.

Catalogue title Contributions to the Topographical Knowledge of the Russian Empire, 3 volumes (vol. 1 itinerary Orenburg steppe and Siberia; vol. 3 the peoples: Tatars, Kalmyks, Kirgis-Kaisaks)

Johann Peter Falck; J. G. Georgi (ed.)

Johann Peter Falck, a Swedish pupil of Linnaeus in Russian service, led an Academy expedition through the Volga, Orenburg steppe, Urals and western Siberia from 1768 until his suicide at Kazan in 1774. J. G. Georgi arranged his unfinished papers, added material of his own and from others, and published them in three volumes in 1785 and 1786. Volume 1 is topography, including a list of old ruins and tombs in the Kirghiz and Kuma steppes; volume 3 describes the peoples, among them the settled Tatars, Bashkirs, Kirghiz-Kaisaks and Kalmyks. The corpus copies are poor Google scans; quotes here were checked against the page images because the OCR is badly garbled.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Turkic Muslim peoples of the Russian Empire: settled Kazan, Astrakhan and Siberian Tatars, the Bashkirs, and the Kirghiz-Kaisaks (Kazakhs), whom Falck describes as wholly Tatar in looks and character; Kalmyks are treated separately.
Why read it
Its list of 'old remains in the steppes' is a rare catalogue of real ruins, tombs and burial mounds on the Kazakh and Kuma steppes as known in the 1770s, told with names, local legends and treasure-hunting stories.
Watch out
Georgi's editing blurs what Falck saw himself and what came from other informants (such as the interpreter Bardanes). The OCR of all three scans is garbled Fraktur, so every quote was read from the page image.

CitiesMadjar (Budyonnovsk), Kuma Riverruins as known in the 1770s

The stone houses of ruined Madjar on the Kuma

Describing the Kuma steppe, the book says the land once had settled people, as shown by ruined towns. On the left bank of the Kuma lie the ruins that Kalmyks call Madsharinka Balgusun, 'the beautiful city Madjar': a ditched enclosure with house foundations, gravestones with inscriptions and seven stone houses still standing. One Arabic inscription was said to record that the people of Madjar left the city for lack of wood and moved into the mountains.

… houses and various gravestones with inscriptions, and also seven standing stone houses of moderate size. The gravestones are said to have partly Arabic, partly unknown inscriptions. On one with an Arabic inscription it is said to read that the people of Madjar left the city because of a shortage of wood and moved into the mountains.

CitiesZungharia and the Kazakh steppeas known in the 1770s

Few old ruins, many new towns

Introducing his list of old remains, the author says that in Zungharia more recently destroyed and newly built towns are known than ancient ruins, though on the Ili stand the ruins of a town with four stone towers. The Kazakh steppes, he adds, seem hardly fit for settled farming, yet he lists the 'remains of former inhabitants' known to him: ruins, stone mosques, tombs and burial mounds.

In Zungharia more recently destroyed and newly built towns are known than ruins of old ones. On the Ili river … are the ruins of a town with 4 stone towers. … The Kirghiz steppes, like the Kuma and Kalmyk steppes, seem hardly capable of European cultivation with permanent dwellings, farming … yet the following remains of former inhabitants have become known to me.

BuildingsKazakh steppe (Kowda and Irgiz rivers)as known in the 1770s

A cube-shaped tomb that nobles wanted to be buried beside

The list describes a masonry tomb of 'father' Baitak shaped like a cube, 3 to 4 fathoms across, where every Muslim prays and every noble ('white bone') wishes to be buried. It adds a stone tomb on the Irgiz said to hold Abulkhair, the Kazakh khan of the Little Horde (killed 1748), and notes the ruins of a town the Kazakhs call Dzhanket on the Syr Darya and of the 'formerly important town' Saraichik on the lower Ural.

On the Kowda (see before) is a masonry tomb of the father (ata) Baitak. It has the form of a cube 3 to 4 fathoms across. Every Muslim performs devotions here, and everyone of white bone, that is of the nobility, wants to be buried here. … On the Irgiz, at [the place of] the elder Barbi, p. 387, is a stone tomb in which Abulkhair is said to rest.

Original wording

Am Kowda (S. vorh.) ist ein gemauert Begräbniß des Vaters (ata) Baitak. Es hat die Form eines Würfels von 3 bis 4 Faden im Durchschnitt. Jeder Muhamedaner hält hier Anbetungen, und jeder mit weißen Knochen oder von Adel, will hier begraben seyn. … Am Irgis des Acksakal Barbi S. 387. ist ein steinern Grabmahl, in welchem Abulchair ruhen soll.

BuildingsKazan region and the Volga1770s

Tatar log houses with a broad bench and a built-in cauldron

The book says the houses of settled Tatars are built of logs and laid out like Russian ones. Inside, a broad bench (urunduk) serves as table, bed and chairs, covered with carpets for the rich and felt for the poor, and there is a hearth (chuval) with an iron cauldron built into it. It adds that the Tatars keep their very simple homes very clean. On the same page the author declines to count the Caucasus 'Mountain Tatars' as Tatars, saying their origin is too uncertain.

The houses of the settled Tatars resemble the Russian ones in being built of logs … Their rooms contain a broad bench (urunduk), which serves as table, bed and chairs and is covered with carpets among the rich and felt among the poor, and a hearth (chuval) with a built-in iron cauldron (tagan), and some chests with clothes, utensils and the like.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Häuser der ansäßigen Tataren gleichen in der Bauart von Blokwerk … den rußischen. Ihre Stuben enthalten eine breite Ruhebank, (Urunduk) die Tisch, Bette und Stühle vertrit und bey reichen mit Teppichen, bey armen mit Filzdecken belegt ist und einen Kamin (Tschuwal) nebst eingemauerten eisernen Kessel (Tagan) und einige Kasten mit Kleidern, Geräte u. d. gl. enthält.

Daily lifeBashkortostan1770s

Bashkir summer evenings on kumys

In summer the Bashkirs lived almost entirely on soured mare's milk. The men, with little to do, went from yurt to yurt emptying the milk skins in cheerful talk, and kumys was strong enough that on warm summer evenings nearly all the men were drunk. The same page says a bride was paid for in livestock: the author saw a young wife who had cost 200 head, namely 60 horses, 40 cows and 100 sheep.

In summer they live almost only on soured mare's milk (kumys), and as they are idle, the men go in company from one yurt to another and empty the milk skins amid cheerful conversation. Yet kumys has so much spirit that on warm summer evenings one finds nearly all the men drunk.

Der Kumiß hat doch so viel Geist, daß man an den warmen Sommerabenden fast alle Mannsleute betrunken findet.

The nameKazakh steppe1770s

'Kir Kasak': the Kazakhs of the steppe

The chapter on the 'Kirghiz' (the Kazakhs) says they are divided into three hordes, and that two of them call themselves Kir Kasak, which the author derives from kir, 'steppe or wilderness', and kasak, 'daredevil'. He describes 'my Kirghiz' as wholly Tatar in looks and temperament, with little courage but a strong taste for raiding, though they spare their captives. It shows how the Russian name 'Kirghiz' was then used for the people now called Kazakhs.

… the two others call themselves Kir Kasak, from kir, wilderness, and kasak, daredevil. … My Kirghiz are entirely Tatar in appearance and temperament, well grown … They have little courage but a great inclination to robbery, yet they are not murderous, but spare their prisoners.

Working translation

Original wording

… die beiden andern nennen sich selbst Kir Kasak, von Kir Wüste und Kasak Wagehals. … Meine Kirgisen sind ganz von tatarischen Ansehen und Gemüthscharacter, wohl gewachsen … Sie haben wenig Muth, aber große Neigung zum Rauben, doch sind sie dabey nicht mörderisch, sondern schonen die Gefangenen.

Strange talesNear the Caspian Sea (exact place not given)1717 (Bekovich-Cherkassky's expedition to Khiva)

Stone towers, idols and Tibetan scrolls by the Caspian

In the same list the author repeats a report that General Bekovich, on his disastrous march to Khiva, found remains near the Caspian made up of four stone towers, stairs, idols and rolled-up 'Tangut' (Tibetan-script) writings. The same page also lists a ruined stone mosque on the Or and a tomb said to have been built by Khan Janibek. The Caspian report is second-hand, but it shows that ruins with idols and Tibetan writings in the steppe were already being reported in the 18th century.

General Bekovich, on his unfortunate march to Khiva, came upon remains near the Caspian Sea which consisted of 4 stone towers, stairs, idols and also rolled-up Tangut writings.

Strange talesNear Irtetskoe outpost, Orenburg line1746

Treasure hunters buried in the ruins, 1746

Thirty versts from the Utva river stood remains of masonry that the Kazakhs called Turalbash, or Charaba, 'brown horse's head'. When Cossacks tried to dig for treasure in them in 1746, four were buried alive, and 'since then they have been left in peace'. It is a small, real-life story of treasure hunting in steppe ruins.

Near the Irtetskoe outpost (p. 176), 30 versts from the Utva, are remains of masonry which the Kirghiz call Turalbash, also Charaba, that is 'brown horse's head'. When in the year 1746 Cossacks wanted to search for treasure in them, 4 were buried; since then they have been left in peace.

Cautions
  • Georgi compiled from Falck's incomplete papers and added material; the third volume on peoples overlaps Georgi's own Beschreibung aller Nationen.

Why it matters

Contains the fullest eighteenth-century German ethnography of the Kirgis-Kaisak hordes and of the Bukharan traders of Siberia, and a systematic topography of the Orenburg frontier line; complements Pallas and Rychkov.
