The Nikon Chronicle: Moscow's grand history of the Horde and the khanates

Muscovy's largest 16th-century chronicle compilation, retelling the Mongol conquest with added tales and recording Kazan, Nogai and Siberian affairs down to 1558.

Full title Nikon Chronicle (Complete Collection of Russian Chronicles, vols. 9-13), Moscow grand compilation to 1558 with Tsarstvennaia kniga

Летописный сборник, именуемый Патриаршею или Никоновскою летописью (ПСРЛ, т. 9-13)

Compiled under Metropolitan Daniil (attribution per Kloss); Archaeographic Commission edition

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

Compiled in Moscow around 1526 to 1530 (traditionally linked with Metropolitan Daniil) and continued to 1558, the Nikon or 'Patriarchal' Chronicle stitches together older chronicles, saints' lives and heroic tales. For the 13th and 14th centuries it is a retelling, sometimes embellished (epic heroes at the Kalka, a sorceress envoy of Batu). For the 1530s to 1550s it is close to official record: Nogai horse traders in Moscow, the siege and mining of Kazan in 1552, and the first envoys from the Siberian prince Ediger offering tribute in 1555. The corpus copies are the Archaeographic Commission volumes 9 to 13 (1862 to 1906); quotes come from vols. 10, 11 and 13.

Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongol invaders and the Golden Horde and its khans ('tsars') for the early centuries; later the Kazan Tatars, Crimean Tatars, Nogais and the Tatars of the Siberian prince Ediger. 'Tsar' is used for any Chinggisid khan.
Why read it
It is the official Muscovite story that New Chronology authors quote and reinterpret, so it is worth seeing what it actually says: foreign khans and their envoys, the Tatars' way of fighting, Moscow's stone walls under siege, Kazan's timber and earth defences and a Siberian ruler counting his taxpayers.
Watch out
A late compilation: for events before about 1500 it is not an independent witness and it adds legends and speeches. It is written from the Moscow court's point of view. The OCR of the 1885 to 1904 volumes is fair to poor; quotes are normalized.

CitiesTver1327

Tver rises against the khan's cousin

Editorial contextUzbek Khan's nephew Shchelkan came to Tver as envoy. The Moscow chronicle says he meant to kill the Tver princes, rule Tver himself, put Tatar princes into the Rus towns and bring the Christians to the 'Tatar faith' (Islam). Tver rose, and Shchelkan was burned in the prince's palace; the Horde merchants in the town were killed too. Uzbek's revenge helped Moscow's rise.

There came to Tver a very powerful envoy, the tsarevich Shchelkan, from the Horde, from Tsar Uzbek; he was Uzbek's nephew, and he wanted to kill the Tver princes and himself sit as prince in Tver, and to set his own Tatar princes over the Russian towns, and he wanted to bring the Christians into the Tatar faith.

Original wording

Пріиде во Тверь посолъ силенъ зѣло царевичь Щелканъ изъ Орды, отъ царя Азбяка; бѣ же сей братаничь царю Азбяку, хотя князей Тверскихъ избити, а самъ сѣсти на княженіи во Твери, а своихъ князей Татарскихъ хотя посажати по Рускимъ градомъ, а христіанъ хотяше привести въ Татарскую вѣру.

CitiesMoscowAugust 1382

Moscow boasts of its stone walls and iron gates

Editorial contextWhen Tokhtamysh marched on Moscow, the townspeople burned their own suburbs so no timber or fence was left for the attackers. Some broke into the boyars' cellars, got drunk on mead and jeered from the walls that the Tatars could not stay long before 'a stone city with iron gates'. The Kremlin walls of white stone had been built in 1367. Four days later the Tatars took the city by a false peace.

And they carried the lords' mead out of the cellars and drank themselves drunk, and reeling about drunk they said: 'We shall not fear the Tatar invasion, for we have a strong stone city and iron gates; and the Tatars will not bear to stand long under our city

CitiesKazan2 October 1552

Kazan's wall blown into the sky

Editorial contextA foreign engineer called 'Razmysl' had dug gunpowder mines under Kazan's walls. As the deacon in Ivan IV's camp church read the Gospel line 'there shall be one flock and one shepherd', the first mine went off 'like thunder': the tsar saw the wall torn out, earth and logs and people thrown high into the air. A second blast followed, and the army stormed the city.

and at once it was as though mighty thunder crashed: the earth shook greatly and trembled. The pious tsar stepped a little out of the church doors and saw the city wall torn out by the mine, and the earth, terrible to look upon, appeared like darkness and rose to a great height, flinging many logs and pagan people up into the air.

BuildingsKazanLate summer 1552

Kazan's earth-filled timber ramparts

Editorial contextDuring the siege Ivan IV's clerk Ivan Vyrodkov built a siege tower six sazhens high opposite Kazan's Tsar's Gate and mounted guns on it. The chronicle explains how the Kazan defenders survived: at every gate beyond the moat they had great 'tarasy', timber cribs filled with earth, and they dug holes and burrows under the gates and walls and fought from them 'like snakes'. Kazan's defences were timber and earth, not stone.

And the pagans hid, digging pits and ditches under the city gates and under the wall, and burrowing holes under the tarasy (for at every gate, beyond the moat, they had great tarasy filled with earth), fighting every day from behind the tarasy and from the holes, like snakes

Daily lifePontic steppe1223

'Swift to battle and swift to flee'

Editorial contextClosing its Kalka story, the Nikon compiler sums up the newcomers: nobody knew where these Tatars came from or where they went. They were quick to fight and quick to run, light and not weighed down by baggage; they fled and then turned back again, the feigned retreat that western writers also noticed. The last phrase, 'like demons', shows the chronicler's fear.

This was the first coming of the Tatars on Rus; they were swift to battle and likewise to flight, being light and not burdened with anything heavy; they flee and then return again, and they are evildoers like demons.

Working translation

Original wording

Се же первое прихоженіе Татарское на Русь; бѣ же скори на бой, такоже и на бѣганіе, легци же суща, не обременяющеся ничимъ же тяготнѣе, бѣгаеть же и паки возвращается, и злодѣиствени суть аки дѣмони.

Daily lifeRyazanWinter 1237

Batu's demand: a tenth of everything

Editorial contextCamped on the river Onuza, Batu sent envoys to the Riazan princes asking for a tenth of everything: princes, people, armour and horses. The princes answered: 'when we are gone, all will be yours.' One manuscript printed alongside says the envoys were 'a sorceress woman and two men with her', a detail that may echo Mongol shamanesses or may be later colour.

And from there they sent their envoys, a sorceress woman and two men with her, to the Riazan princes, asking of them a tenth of everything: of princes, and of people, and of armour, and of horses.

Working translation

Original wording

И оттолѣ послаша послы своя жену чародѣйцу и два мужа съ нею ко княземъ Рязаньскимъ, просяще у нихъ десятины во всемъ: въ князехъ, и въ людехъ, и въ доспѣсехъ, и въ конехъ.

Nikon Chronicle, variant text of the Tolstoy (Golitsyn) copy printed in parallel, PSRL vol. 10 (1885)

Daily lifeMoscowAugust 1382

Tatar archers who never miss

Editorial contextThe account of the 1382 siege describes Tatar archery: their arrows fell on the walls like rain, some shot standing, others ran back and forth, others galloped and shot to both sides, forward and back, 'without a miss'. The defenders answered with crossbows, stone-throwers, boiling water and even the first cannon mentioned in Moscow.

Their assault on the city was very fierce; some of them shot standing, and others ran swiftly here and there, so well were they trained, and shot without a miss; and others, galloping very fast on horses, shot swiftly to both hands, and again forward and backward, without a miss.

Working translation

Original wording

Бѣ же приступъ ихъ ко граду яръ зѣло и ови отъ нихъ стояще стрѣляху, а друзіи скоро рищуще сѣмо и овамо, тако изучени быша, и стрѣляху безъ погрѣха; а иніи на конѣхъ борзо велми гоняюще, и на обѣ руцѣ и паки напреди и назадъ скорополучно стрѣляху безъ погрѣха.

Daily lifeWestern SiberiaJanuary 1555 (7063)

Siberia asks to pay tribute: a sable per man

Editorial contextEnvoys came to Moscow from Ediger, 'prince of Siberia', and from 'all the Siberian land'. They congratulated Ivan IV on taking Kazan and Astrakhan and asked him to take Siberia under his protection and set tribute. They agreed on one sable per 'black' (tax-paying) man, plus a squirrel skin for the collector, and said they had 30,700 such men. This is the first Moscow record of the Siberian khanate that Yermak would later conquer.

they petitioned the sovereign on behalf of Prince Ediger and of all the land, that the sovereign take their prince and all the Siberian land in his name, and defend them from all sides, and lay his tribute on them … and they said they had 30,700 black [tax-paying] people.

Working translation

Original wording

били челомъ государю ото князя Едигеря и ото всей земли, чтобы государь ихъ князя и всю землю Сибирьскую взялъ во свое имя и отъ сторонъ ото всѣхъ заступилъ и дань свою на нихъ положилъ … а черныхъ у собя людей сказали 30 тысящь семъсотъ человѣкъ.

Nikon Chronicle, PSRL vol. 13, part 1 (1904)

Daily lifeMoscowNovember 1539 (7048)

Nogai horses for sale below Simonov

Editorial contextNogai envoys arrived with letters from their mirzas asking for friendship. The grand prince lodged them at the 'Nogai Court' in Moscow and let them sell their horses on the meadow below the Simonov monastery. That year the horses were cheap: early snow had left no grazing, and many barely arrived alive or died. Steppe horse herds for Moscow's cavalry were the Nogais' great export.

And the grand prince favoured them, and wishes to keep his friendship with them, and ordered them lodged at the Nogai Court, and to trade their horses on the meadow below Simonov. And the horses were cheap, they barely arrived alive, and many others died, because snow fell early, for lack of fodder.

Original wording

И князь великій пожаловалъ ихъ, дружбу свою хочетъ къ нимъ дръжати, и велѣлъ ихъ поставити на Нагайскомъ дворѣ, а съ коньми торговати подъ Симановымъ на лугу. И быша лошади дешевы, едва и живы пришли, а иныхъ много помроша, понеже снѣгъ палъ рано, безъкорміа ради.

Strange talesKalka River1223 (dated 6733 in the Nikon)

Epic heroes die at the Kalka

Editorial contextThe Nikon Chronicle adds names from Russian heroic poetry to the Kalka disaster: Aleksandr Popovich, his servant Torop, Dobrynia 'Golden Belt' and seventy great bogatyrs all fell to the Tatars. The same page tells how the captured princes were laid under planks while the Tatars sat on top to eat, crushing them. Older chronicles do not name these heroes; the compilers took them from oral epic.

and in that battle they killed Aleksandr Popovich and his servant Torop, and Dobrynia Riazanich Golden Belt, and seventy great and brave bogatyrs; all were slain by the Tatars through the wrath of God for our sins.

Working translation

Original wording

убиша же на томъ бою: и Александра Поповичя, и слугу его Торопа, и Добрыню Рязаничя Златаго пояса, и седмьдесятъ великихъ и храбрыхъ богатырей, вси побіени быша гнѣвомъ Божіимъ за грѣхи наша отъ Татаръ.

Nikon Chronicle, PSRL vol. 10 (1885)

The fine print

How we know

compilation Assembled from earlier sources. The author did not observe it directly.

retrospective compilation (1520s) of earlier chronicles with added tales; Kazan, Crimean, Nogai and Kazakh (Kazatskaia orda) affairs of 1480s-1550s near-contemporary in vol. 13

Research notes
  • Late compilation combining Sofia I, Novgorod, Trinity and Moscow svods with hagiographical insertions; not an independent witness for pre-1500 events; IA imagecounts (270, 290) look like placeholders and should not be read as page counts.

Why it matters

The largest Muscovite compilation; supplies the official 16th-century Moscow narrative of Horde succession, Kazan wars and first Siberian contacts that New Chronology authors (Fomenko, Nosovsky) cite and reinterpret; useful as a test of what the received compilation actually says.
