Peter Henry Bruce's memoirs of service under Peter the Great

A British-descended officer's memoir of Russian service, including the 1722 Volga voyage and Persian campaign and a 1723 expedition among the Kalmyks.

Catalogue title Memoirs of Peter Henry Bruce

Peter Henry Bruce

Peter Henry Bruce (1692-1757), of Scottish family but raised in Prussia, served in the Russian army from 1711 to about 1724, including Peter the Great's 1722 expedition down the Volga to Astrakhan and into Dagestan, and a 1723 mission from Astrakhan to settle a Kalmyk succession fight. His memoirs were published posthumously in London in 1782. The Tartar material mixes his own observations (Kazan, Astrakhan, Kalmyk camps, Tarki) with long descriptive passages that closely follow earlier travel books, plus reports he heard at court, such as the massacre of the Bekovich expedition in Khiva.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
A broad label for the peoples along the Volga and Caspian: Kazan Tatars, Nogais ('Nagayans') of Astrakhan, Kalmyks, Crimean and Kuban Tatars, the Uzbeks of Khiva, the Kumyks and others of Dagestan ('Dagestan Tartars'), and even the Finnic Mari ('Ceremisse Tartars').
Why read it
A soldier's close look at Kalmyk felt tents and camp life, Tatar towns under Russian control, and the ruined brick city on the Volga that local tradition credited to Tamerlane.
Watch out
Written up long after the events and published by others after his death; some general descriptions of the Volga peoples echo Olearius and other earlier writers almost word for word. Hearsay (Khiva, Siberia) is mixed with what he saw. The 1782 OCR prints long s as f.

CitiesAstrakhan1722

Astrakhan: Tatars kept outside the stone walls

Bruce describes Astrakhan as a Russian city with high stone fortifications, stone towers and wooden houses. The Tartars, the former inhabitants, were no longer allowed to live inside the walls, only in suburbs fenced with palisades. He dates the Russian capture to 1554, after the fall of Kazan.

The city is of a considerable bigness, and at present inhabited almost entirely by Russians ; the former inhabitants of the country, being Tartars, are not permitted to live within the walls, but in the adjacent suburbs, which are only fenced round with pallisades : the fortifications of the city are all of stone, very high

CitiesTarki, DagestanAugust 1722

Tarki, capital of 'Dagestan Tartary'

Peter the Great's army camped below Tarki, seat of the shamkhal. Bruce describes a town of over 3,000 houses on a hillside, without walls, with two-storey flat-roofed houses where women walked in the evening, each with its own garden and spring.

This city of Tarku, being the metropolis of Dagestan, contains above three thousand houses, and is very full of inhabitants ; the houses all two stories high, platformed at top, standing close to each other : the women walk upon them in the cool of the evenings, as the men do in the streets. Every house has a garden stored with all kinds of delicious fruits

BuildingsLower Volga near AstrakhanOctober 1723

How a Kalmyk kibitka is put up

Camped in bitter cold above Astrakhan, Bruce's detachment was lent sixty kibitkas by the Kalmyk prince Dasan. He describes them closely: 24 feet across, folding lattice walls six feet high, a round wooden crown raised on lances with holes for the rafters, and a felt covering laid from the bottom up. He found them warmer and more weatherproof than a house.

they are all round, the sides being made of a kind of checkered wicker-work, and the cross sticks neatly jointed for folding together or extending … they raise with their lances a round board, three feet diameter, with a hole in the middle of it … the large hole serves for their chimney, the small holes receive the ends of so many strait rafters

BuildingsLower Volga between Kamyshin and VolgogradJuly 1722

A ruined brick city 'built by Tamerlane', quarried for Astrakhan

Sailing down the Volga, Bruce passed the ruins of a great city called Czarefgorod. He reports the local belief that Tamerlane built it, says its palace and walls were brick, and notes that the Russians had been carting the bricks away for years to build Astrakhan's walls, churches and monasteries. It was most likely one of the Golden Horde's Volga towns.

About forty miles from this, and at a small distance from the river-side, are to be seen the ruins of a great city, formerly called Czaresgorod, built, as is related, by Tamerlane : its palace and walls were all of brick, and have served the city of Astrachan with materials, these many years, for building their walls, churches, and monasteries.

BuildingsAstrakhan1722

Nogai round huts of bulrushes, each with its hawk

Bruce says the Nogais around Astrakhan lived mostly in small round huts of bulrushes or canes with a smoke hole at the top, and that even the smallest had a hawk, since they were great falconers. On the next page he says they packed the huts onto carts in summer and moved with their herds.

The Nagayans live for the most part in round huts made of bull-rushes, or canes, and seldom exceed twelve or thirteen yards in circumference, with a hole at the top to let out the smoak ; yet the least of these huts has a falcon, or hawk, as those Tartars are great masters of this sport

BuildingsKazan1722

Kazan Tatars who 'live all in houses'

Bruce contrasts the Kazan Tatars with the nomads: they all lived in houses, farmed and supplied the south with food, which made them the most civilised of the Tartars in his eyes. He adds they were mostly Muslims, though many townspeople were Orthodox, and were forbidden to enter Russian fortifications. On the previous page he describes Kazan itself: wooden houses, a stone castle, and a population of Tatars and Russians.

The kingdom of Casan lies on the left side of the Wolga, and its inhabitants live all in houses, and subsist by agriculture : they supply the southern provinces with all sorts of provisions, and by this means they are the most civilized of all the Tartars.

BuildingsSiberiac. 1717

'Vagabond Tartars' with huts by the rivers

Bruce reports what an envoy sent by Peter the Great to the 'northern Tartar princes' found on a journey toward the Lena. He said they were all wandering Tartars in tents, rich only in cattle, but that along the rivers there were some houses or huts and a little corn, where their khans usually lived. The prince of the Yakuts turned him back.

He reported, that they were all vagabond Tartars, living in tents, and shifting their residence from place to place for pasturage, as all their riches consisted in herds of cattle ; but he observed a sort of houses, or huts, on the sides of rivers, and some corn, and in these situations their chams generally resided.

Daily lifeLower Volga, Saratov to AstrakhanJune 1722

Not a single house between Saratov and Astrakhan

Below Saratov Bruce entered Kalmyk country on the left bank of the Volga. He says the whole huge tract had not one house: the Kalmyks lived in tents, moved with horses, camels, cattle and sheep, grew no crops and ate meat (especially horse), fish and game, with plenty of milk and a strong clear spirit made from mare's milk.

in all which immense tract there is not so much as one single house to be seen, as they all live in tents, and remove from one place to another in quest of pasturage for their large herds of cattle, consisting of horses, camels, cows, and sheep ; they neither sow, nor reap, nor make hay for their cattle, so that they live without bread

Daily lifeLower Volga near Astrakhan1723

Left behind with a sheep and a jar of water

Bruce says the Kalmyks feared smallpox like the plague. When someone caught it they struck camp and fled, leaving the sick person in their worst kibitka with a killed sheep, water and firewood; few survived. On the same page he describes being served roast and boiled suckling foal by Prince Dasan, and calls it delicious.

The small-pox is as much dreaded among the Kalmucks as the pestilence amongst us : when any of them are seized with it, they immediately break up their camp and fly, leaving the sick person in one of their worst kibbets with a killed sheep, part of which is roasted and part raw, and a jar of water and some wood for fire

Daily lifeMiddle Volga1722

A skin on a pole to plead with God

Bruce calls the Mari (Cheremis) of the middle Volga 'Tartars' and describes them as heathens without churches or priests. When they sacrificed a horse, cow or sheep they hung its skin on a high pole and prayed to it to intercede with God for more cattle. He adds details on naming, polygamy and dress.

when they offer a sacrifice to God, they kill a horse, cow, or sheep, and extend its skin on a high pole, which they implore to intercede for them with God, that he may increase the number of their cattle. They have a high veneration for the sun and moon … They make use of no churches, priests, or books

Who Mari ('Ceremisse Tartars')

Read itPeter Henry Bruce (published 1782)

Keep in mind. The Mari are a Finnic people, not Tatars; this account closely matches Olearius and is probably borrowed.

The nameAstrakhan1722

Nogais said to be Indians, and Astrakhan named after King Astra

In his 'short account of the Tartars', Bruce says the ancients knew them only as Scythians and Sarmatians, and that the Tartars were really several nations. He repeats a story that the Nogais and Kazan Tatars were Indians who rebelled around 1212 and migrated to the Sea of Azov, and that Astrakhan was named after its founder, a Tartar king called Astra.

The Nagayans, with the Tartars of Casan, and some others inhabiting between the Wolga and the Don, or Tanais, are said to have been Indians, who revolting from their sovereigns about the year 1212, emigrated … Astrachan, their principal city, they relate to have been built by a Tartar king, whose name being Astra, gave his city the name of Astra chan, or king.

Who Nogai and Kazan Tatars

Legend or rumourPeter Henry Bruce (published 1782)

Keep in mind. Both stories are origin legends reported as hearsay ('are said', 'they relate'); they appear in earlier writers such as Olearius.

Strange talesKhiva1717

The khan who wanted no gold: the Khiva massacre

Bruce tells how Prince Alexander Bekovich, sent to find gold on the Amu Darya, was lured into quartering his men among the Uzbeks. The khan assured him he had no use for gold or silver, since his people owned only herds and tents and had no castles or towns to lose. That night the troops were massacred and Bekovich cut to pieces. The story reached Peter through a surviving captive officer.

for his own part, he neither valued gold nor silver, as it was of no use in their country, for they lived without that, or even bread, consequently had no use for either ; their whole riches consisting in herds of cattle, which, with their tents, they could remove at pleasure ; and, consequently, could not fear having either castles, towns, or villages, rifled or taken from them

Who Uzbeks of Khiva ('Usbeck Tartars')

Heard itPeter Henry Bruce, reporting the khan's words via a surviving officer (published 1782)

Keep in mind. Hearsay and dramatised; the khanate of Khiva in fact had walled towns, so the khan's speech (if spoken) was a ruse.
