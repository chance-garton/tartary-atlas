Hammer-Purgstall's History of the Golden Horde (1840)

The first full western history of the Mongol khanate on the Volga, pieced together from Arabic, Persian, Turkish, Russian and European sources.

Catalogue title History of the Golden Horde in Kipchak, that is, of the Mongols in Russia (Pest 1840), with nine appendices of translated Arabic, Persian and Turkish sources and a coin plate

Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall

Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall, the leading Viennese orientalist of his day, wrote this history of the Golden Horde (the Jochid khanate of Kipchak, ruled from Sarai on the lower Volga) as a prize essay for the St Petersburg Academy. When the Academy's judges rejected it, he published it himself at Pest in 1840, printing their objections (above all Fraehn's and Schmidt's marginal notes) in his footnotes with sharp replies. It covers the Horde's peoples and towns, its tents, food, dress, religion, court and army, its rule over Russia, its links with Egypt and Byzantium, Timur's destruction of Sarai and the successor khanates, with appendices of translated Ottoman, Persian and Arabic extracts. Corpus copies: three scans of the 1840 edition.

Compiled from others
Who “Tartar” means here
Hammer uses 'Tataren' and 'Mongolen' almost interchangeably for the people of the Golden Horde: a Mongol ruling house over mostly Turkic (Kipchak) subjects. He explains that 'Tatar' began as one tribe's name and became a catch-all, and that the Turkic peoples of Russia in his own day are 'wrongly' called Tatars.
Why read it
The best single guide, as of 1840, to what the Islamic, Russian and European sources said about Sarai, Özbeg Khan's court, the Horde's felt tent-carts, its farming and its religion, with a named source behind almost every sentence.
Watch out
A compilation: most details are Hammer's loose German versions of Rubruck, Carpini, Marco Polo, Ibn Battuta, Barbaro, Rashid al-Din, Wassaf and the Russian chronicles, and he mixes evidence from different centuries and peoples. His philology was faulted by the Academy's reviewers.

Sarai, the Horde's capital, and its ruins on the Akhtuba

In his survey of the towns of Kipchak, Hammer places Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, on the Akhtuba channel of the Volga, where ruins stretched forty versts into the steppe and at least ten versts along the river. He takes from the Persian historian Wassaf that Batu founded it, and from coins that there were several Sarais: the old and the new (the second only an extension of the first), with Gülistan apparently a suburb, and a third called Sali Serai. Elsewhere (p. 140 to 141) he says Batu built Sarai in his last years, as he had earlier built Kazan.

Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans of the Golden Horde, whose ruins [lie] forty versts into the steppe from the bank of the Volga and at least ten versts along the Akhtuba … There were, as the coins show, three Sarais: the old and the new, the latter being only an extension of the old, just as Gülistan, which often appears on coins, seems to have been only a suburb of the residence Sarai

CitiesAstrakhan and Sarai, lower Volga, RussiaWinter 1395 to 1396

Walls of ice at Astrakhan, and Timur's razing of Sarai

After defeating Tokhtamysh, Timur resolved to level Astrakhan. Its people used the hard winter to build walls of ice blocks cemented by pouring water over them, but Timur marched through the deep snow, emptied the town, razed it and had its headman (kelanter) Mahmudi drowned under the ice that was meant to protect him. He then went on to Sarai, capital and residence of the khans, and laid it waste, as revenge for the Horde's earlier destruction of a city of the Chagatai realm. The loot of both cities was divided among the army.

In spite of the severe winter, which still gave the inhabitants of Astrakhan the advantage of fortifying themselves with walls of ice, joined together by pouring water over them, and in spite of the deep snow, he marched on Astrakhan, emptied the town of its inhabitants and had it razed … From Astrakhan Timur moved to Sarai, the capital of Kipchak and residence of the khans, and laid it waste

CitiesKasimov, Ryazan region, RussiaAbout 1450 (Fraehn dated the building to 1452)

Kasimov: a Tatar prince's town on the Oka

When 'wind-swift' Tatars of Sayyid Ahmad's horde raided Muscovy, Sultan Kasim drove them off, and in gratitude the grand prince granted him a yurt (here, a domain) on the Oka in the Murom district, and 'there he built a town'. Hammer adds that the district town that still bears his name, on a height at the mouth of the Babinka, ringed by an earthen rampart, was in his day home to about five hundred Tatars with their own quarter and mosque. A rare case of a Tatar town founded inside Russia with Russian consent.

Out of gratitude the grand prince granted Kasim a yurt on the Oka, in the district of Murom. There he built a town … His name is still borne today by the district town at the mouth of the Babinka into the Oka, which, lying on a height and surrounded by an earthen rampart, and still inhabited today by half a thousand Tatars, preserves the memory of its origin through its Tatar quarter and mosque.

Felt houses on wheels, drawn by twenty oxen

Hammer's chapter on dwellings, food and women pulls together Rubruck, Carpini and Marco Polo. The tents were of felt and set on wheels, like the wagon-houses of Horace's Scythians; the chimney-like top was of white felt whitened with lime or powdered bone to shine from afar against the dark tent; the south-facing door was closed by a felt embroidered with vines, trees, birds or beasts. The largest, rectangular and up to thirty feet long, were drawn by twenty-odd oxen and called chargah; round ones were called tscherke. A footnote adds that Timur's chargah was made of two hundred gilded, jewelled tents.

Their tents were of felt and on wheels, like the tents of the Scythians in Horace; their top, shaped almost like a chimney, was of white felt, whitewashed with lime or with white dust of crushed bone … These tents were of different shape and size; the largest, an oblong rectangle, often up to thirty feet long and drawn by twenty-odd oxen, were called chargah

Only craftsmen spared; 'houses of the dead' for nobles

Describing Mongol ways of war and burial, Hammer says they killed all prisoners of war except the craftsmen, whom they dragged off as slaves: a key point for who built things in the Mongol empire. Princes' funerals included the killing of concubines and captives; burial places were kept secret, but those of the great were marked by stone monuments or 'houses of the dead'. A stallion, a mare and a foal went into the grave so the dead would ride and have mare's milk in the next life, and a stuffed horse hide was set up over the grave.

Prisoners taken in war they killed, all except the craftsmen, whom they dragged off with them as slaves. … Their burial place was kept secret, but that of the nobles was marked by stone monuments or houses of the dead. A stallion, a mare and a foal were given to the dead man in the grave, so that in the other life he would at once have a mount and not lack mare's milk.

Ein Hengst, eine Stute und ein Füllen wurden dem Todten ins Grab mitgegeben, damit er im andern Leben sogleich beritten sey, und dass es ihm an Stutenmilch nicht fehle.

Daily lifeMongol empire (general)13th century

Felt idols, the god Natagai, and a smiths' New Year

Hammer gathers the old Mongol religion from Rubruck, Marco Polo and Rashid al-Din: idols of felt and silk, offered the heart of each slaughtered animal; prayer facing south; worship of sun, stars and elements, with food and drink dedicated before meals; a chief god Natagai honoured with his whole family; libations to the four quarters outside the tent. He lists three yearly feasts: the smiths' festival recalling the exodus from Ergene Kun, the khan's birthday, and the herd festival with mare's milk poured for Khubilai's ten thousand white horses. In a footnote he quotes Rashid al-Din that on New Year's night Chinggis Khan's family hammered iron on an anvil and gave thanks.

The Mongols had idols of felt and silk. When they slaughtered an animal, the heart was offered to the idols, and they prayed facing south, as the Muslims face the Kaaba. They worshipped the sun and the stars together with the elements … Their principal god was called Natagai, and he was worshipped with his whole family by all Tatars.

Die Mongolen hatten Götzenbilder aus Filz und Seide. Wenn sie ein Thier schlachteten, wurde den Götzenbildern das Herz dargebracht, und sie betheten gegen Mittag gewandt, wie die Moslimen gegen die Kaaba. Sie betheten die Sonne und die Sterne sammt den Elementen an … Ihr vorzüglicher Gott hiess Natagai, und ward mit seiner ganzen Familie von allen Tataren verehrt.

Daily lifeÖzbeg Khan's camp, North Caucasus steppe; Sarai1333

Ibn Battuta at Özbeg Khan's court: unveiled, honoured wives

Hammer retells Ibn Battuta's visit. Every Friday Özbeg sat under a golden vault called the golden alcove, on a silver-plated, jewelled throne, with his sons on either side and his daughter before him. Whenever one of his wives came in he rose, took her by the hand and led her to her seat; all were unveiled. Each wife had her own house and household, and Ibn Battuta marvelled at the freedom and standing of women among the Tatars, so unlike Arab and Persian harem seclusion.

Whenever one of his wives came in, he stood up, took her by the hand and led her to her place; they were all unveiled. … Özbeg's wives were highly honoured. Each had her own house and her own household; in general Ibn Battuta wonders at the freedom and esteem which women enjoyed among the Tatars (quite unlike the compulsion of the Arab and Persian harem).

Daily lifeGolden Horde steppe near Tana (Azov), Russia1430s

The nomad Horde sows its grain

From the Venetian Josafa Barbaro, who lived at Tana, Hammer reports that Ulu Muhammad kept his camp moving for eleven years without letting the Horde sow in March and wait for the harvest, so it suffered grain shortage and lived on meat and milk alone. Normally, in February the camp announced the sowing day; those who wanted to grow grain drove out with wagons and seed to fields a day or two from the camp. The harvest was rich: fifty-fold for grain and a hundred-fold for millet. Nomads, in other words, also farmed.

Otherwise it was the custom that in the month of February the day on which sowing should take place in March was proclaimed in the camp. On that day those who wished to grow grain drove with their wagons and seed to the fields, usually one or two days' journey from the court camp, and tilled them. The harvest was plentiful, for the soil gave fifty-fold of the grain

The nameMongolia and the steppe world12th century to Hammer's day

Tatars wanted to be Turks, Turks wanted to be Tatars

Hammer notes that the Black Tatars near Baikal and the Water Tatars on the Kerulen appeared at the end of the 12th century under the name Mongols. When Chinggis Khan raised this small people to world rule, Turkic and Tatar peoples claimed the name for themselves, just as earlier, while Turks and Tatars ruled, each had wanted the other's name. Only later, once that power had faded, did Mongols and Kalmyks refuse to be called Tatars and Ottomans refuse to be called Turks; his note cites Rubruck's remark that the Mongols did not want to be called Tartars. On p. 22 he adds that the Turkic peoples of Russia are called Tatars, 'though the name is incorrect'.

When the small people living south-east of Lake Baikal … was raised by Chinggis Khan to be the ruling tribe of world-conquering armies, Turkic and Tatar peoples laid claim to this name, just as, when the Turks and Tatars held power, Turks wanted to be called Tatars and Tatars Turks. Only in more recent times … do the Mongols and Kalmyks no longer want to be called Tatars, nor the Ottomans Turks.

Strange talesInner Asia ('east of the 26th clime')Before 1275 (story); Ottoman compilation about 1600

Trial by goat and sieve among the pagan Tatars

Hammer's first appendix translates the Ottoman geography Menazir ul-awalim, which here copies al-Qazwini's Athar al-bilad (1275). The Tatars are described as a hard, bloodthirsty Turkic people who worship the sun; their coming is counted among the Prophet's foretold wonders, with a saying about broad-faced, small-eyed 'Turks' who will tie their horses to the pillars of the mosques. A woman once held captive tells how, when her captor fell ill, his kin suspected her of poisoning him, set her on a goat and had a sorceress swing a sieve over her while they stood round with drawn swords; when the goat bleated she was freed. The Ottoman author adds that these pagan Tatars differ from the Crimean and Nogai Tatars, long since Muslims.

I was for a time a captive among the Tatars, when the man who had taken me prisoner fell ill. … One day they held a great assembly, brought a goat and set me on it. A sorceress came with a sieve in her hand, swung it and spoke some words … While this was happening, the goat bleated under me. They said: it is not as we believed, and let me go.

Cautions
  • Rejected by the St Petersburg Academy (Frähn's critique) for philological errors; Hammer published it independently with the correspondence. Translations are Hammer's own and often loose.

Why it matters

First monographic history of the Golden Horde in any western language, giving German readers direct extracts from the Islamic sources on the Mongols in Russia; documents which oriental texts were available in 1840 and how the Horde was distinguished from Tartary at large.
