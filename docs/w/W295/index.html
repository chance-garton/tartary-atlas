Schrenk's journey through the Samoyed tundra to the Arctic Urals, 1837

A Baltic German botanist's detailed account of the Russian far north and the Nenets (Samoyeds), with almost nothing on Tartars.

Full title Journey to the Northeast of European Russia, through the Tundras of the Samoyeds, to the Arctic Ural Mountains, undertaken in 1837 for the Imperial Botanical Garden, part 1 (Dorpat 1848)

Alexander Gustav von Schrenk

Read the 1 passageOpen the original book

Alexander Gustav von Schrenk, a Baltic German botanist sent out by the Imperial Botanical Garden in St Petersburg, travelled in 1837 from Archangel province through the Mezen and Pechora country and the Bolshezemelskaya tundra to the Arctic Urals and the approaches to the Kara Sea. Part 1 (Dorpat 1848) is a long, learned travel narrative: Russian villages and trade, the Komi (Zyrians), and above all the European Samoyeds (Nenets), their tents, reindeer, marriage, burial and idol sites, with essays on place names left by the vanished Chud. Tartars barely appear; the one item of interest here is a footnote quoting Witsen's 'Noord en Oost Tartarye'. Corpus copy: IA scan of part 1 (1848).

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Almost absent. Tatars appear once as a Volga people using a shared distance measure, and 'Tartary' only in the title of Witsen's 1692 book on 'North and East Tartary', which Schrenk quotes for the Pechora region. He describes the Samoyeds as having a 'Mongolian' facial type but does not call them Tartars.
Why read it
Shows how far the old label 'Tartary' once stretched: Witsen's book on Tartary was still the oldest printed source for an oil seep in the European Arctic that Schrenk discusses.
Watch out
Marginal to Tartary. Schrenk's racial descriptions of the Samoyeds reflect the typology of his time, and his special transliteration letters for Russian sounds are often mangled by the OCR.

A stone that 'burns like a candle' in Witsen's Tartary book

Editorial contextAmong the rocks of the district Schrenk lists a black, combustible shale soaked with bitumen from the Ukhta, known locally as Domanik, and in a footnote he notes that the naphtha springs there were already known to Witsen. He translates the passage from the first edition of Witsen's 'Noord en Oost Tartarye' (Amsterdam 1692): a shallow in the river drives a black oil to the surface, and a stone found there burns like a candle with a black light. Schrenk calls it the oldest report of the Domanik, a real oil-bearing rock, and goes on to later Russian accounts of an old timber enclosure built over the oil spring.

the river Oegta [Ukhta] is a day's journey from the Pechora; on this river … there is a shallow in it (drooghte die daer in is) which drives a fattiness onto the water, which is black naphtha; which stone is also found there, and burns like a candle, giving off a black light.

Working translation

Original wording

der Fluss Oegta [Uhta] ist eine Tagereise vom Petschorastrom entfernt; an diesem Flusse … findet sich darin eine Untiefe (drooghte die daer in is), die eine Fettigkeit aufs Wasser treibt, welche schwarze Naphta ist; welcher Stein dort auch gefunden wird, und brennt wie eine Kerze, ein schwarzes Licht von sich gebend.

Nicolaes Witsen (Noord en Oost Tartarye, 1692), quoted in German translation in a footnote by Alexander Gustav von Schrenk
See the original page (p. 207)
Keep in mind

The 'black light' is Witsen's wording, and his source for it is not given here. Schrenk confirms that the shale exists and burns, but not Witsen's exact description.

Why it matters

Marginal to Tartary proper but the fullest pre-1850 German ethnography of the Samoyeds on the European side of the Ural, useful for the northern limit of the peoples Strahlenberg and Georgi grouped with Siberia.
