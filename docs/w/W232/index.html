Michalo the Lithuanian on the customs of the Tartars, Lithuanians and Muscovites

A Lithuanian envoy who had been in the Crimean khanate holds up the Crimean Tatars as a mirror of sober virtue to shame his own nobles, c. 1550.

Catalogue title On the Customs of the Tartars, Lithuanians and Muscovites: Ten Fragments

Michalo the Lithuanian (pseudonymous Lithuanian nobleman and envoy); J. J. Grasser, editor

The author, writing as 'Michalo the Lithuanian', was a Lithuanian nobleman and envoy who says he was present in the Crimea in the late 1530s. Around 1550 he wrote a treatise for King Sigismund Augustus comparing the Crimean (Perekop) Tatars and the Muscovites with the Lithuanians, mostly to the Lithuanians' shame. Johann Jacob Grasser printed it at Basel in 1615 as ten fragments from a manuscript, with Jan Lasicki's tract on Samogitian gods. The corpus copy is that 1615 edition, plus the 1627 Elzevir Respublica Poloniae, which reprints extracts from Michalo on Lithuania. The fragments cover Crimean geography, the Giray dynasty, nomad life, law, marriage, religion and, at harrowing length, the slave trade in Lithuanian captives.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Chiefly the Crimean Tatars, whom he calls 'Precopenses' after Perekop, the fortified gate of the peninsula; also the other hordes (Nogai, Volga, Kazan, Bukhara, Samarkand) in passing, and the Volga ('Zavolhenses') Tatars who once ruled Muscovy.
Why read it
A rare Latin insider's view of the sixteenth-century Crimean khanate that praises Tatar sobriety, justice and charity while describing the slave markets of Perekop and Kaffa where his own countrymen were sold. It also records a Lithuanian claim that the Giray khans owed their throne to Lithuania.
Watch out
The book is a moralising tract, so it idealises the Tatars to scold Lithuanians; it survives only as fragments chosen by a later editor; and the author's identity is disputed. The OCR of the 1615 print is fair but has many broken words.

CitiesFeodosia (Kaffa)c. 1540

Kaffa, 'a whirlpool of our blood'

Michalo describes the sale of captives from his own country in the Crimean markets: led in chained rows of ten, auctioned by criers who shout that they are fresh, 'royal' not Muscovite slaves, stripped and checked for flaws, dressed up to fetch a better price, and resold for profit. This happens in all the peninsula's towns, 'but above all in Kaffa', whose port sends the bought captives straight onto ships. He calls Kaffa an insatiable place, a 'whirlpool of our blood'.

These things go on in the other towns of that peninsula too, but above all in Kaffa, and there it happens that crowds of those wretches, once bought, are driven straight from the market to the ships. … by its site that insatiable … city, but a whirlpool of our blood.

BuildingsCrimea (Solkhat, Chersonesos)c. 1550

Crimea's ruined Greek cities with a thousand churches

Michalo says the Crimea was once so densely peopled by Greeks that single cities had a thousand churches, and that some cities, though ruined, still show by their walls and ruins how grand they were: Solkhat (called Krym by the Muscovites and once Theodosia by the Greeks) and old Chersonesos (Korsun), where the Rus' were baptised. He is clear about who built them: the Greeks, not the Tatars, who came later as settlers.

And even now some cities there, though ruined, show by the size of their circuits and of their ruins that they were once splendid, above all the one we call Solkhat, the Muscovites Krym, and the Greeks once called Theodosia, and the ancient metropolis of Korsun, which first gave baptism to the Ruthenian people

BuildingsCrimea and the Black Sea steppec. 1550

No houses, only portable huts of wicker and reeds on carts

Michalo describes the Tatars' pastoral life: they follow their herds across the plains, have no enclosures and no houses except portable tents made of wicker and reeds covered with felt and rush mats, which they carry on carts full of wives and children. They do not farm even rich land, live on milk without bread or beer, and own nothing fixed except wells shared by the clan.

They have no enclosures and no houses, only portable tents of wicker and reeds joined together, covered with felts and mats and made fast with rush matting, which, filled with wives and children, they carry with them on carts.

Daily lifeCrimeac. 1550

Horses never harnessed to a cart, even for the khan

Tatars train to ride from the cradle and keep riding into old age, despising carriages even when ill. Michalo says even a leading woman going to the khan's court rides in a closed cart drawn by one or two oxen, glittering with gold and jewels inside, because it is forbidden to harness even the worst horse to a vehicle, though a man may own a thousand horses. He sets this against Lithuanian ladies driven in carriages drawn by six or eight horses.

And among them even a woman of the first rank, even one close to the khan, going to the palace, is not ashamed to ride shut in her closed cage, gleaming inside with gold and jewels, drawn by one ox, or by two if the cart is heavy. But to yoke even the worst horse to a vehicle is sacrilege and forbidden to every one of them

Daily lifePerekopc. 1540

A Jewish toll-keeper asks if anyone is left in Lithuania

At Perekop, the single gate into the Crimea, Michalo met a Jewish toll collector who watched endless columns of Lithuanian captives being brought in and asked whether there were still any people left in their lands, and where so many came from. Michalo then describes how the stronger male captives were mutilated, branded and chained to work by day and locked up by night, while young women were kept for pleasure.

So a certain Jew there in Taurica, in charge of the toll at its one and only gate, and always seeing an innumerable multitude of our people brought in as captives, asked us whether there were still any people left in our lands or not, and where so great a supply of mortals came from.

Daily lifeCrimeac. 1550

Judges who are clerics, and no one carries a sword at home

Michalo contrasts Tatar order with Lithuanian lawlessness: among the Tatars no one overeats and no one starves, there are no beggars, thieves, false witnesses or corrupt judges, and it is forbidden to carry a sword while travelling inside their land. Justice is given free by religious judges (qadis), with no fee taken, and everyone from nobles to commoners lives under one law and the same dress and food.

So there is no need among them for anyone to worry anxiously about guarding his goods, or to load himself with weapons at home for his private defence. For it is outrageous and unlawful for anyone travelling within their land to carry a weapon.

Daily lifeCrimeac. 1550

Eighty blows for a taste of wine

Attacking drunkenness in Lithuania, Michalo says more Lithuanians are executed for theft and robbery in a single month in one district than in one or two hundred years across all the Tatar and Muscovite lands where drink is forbidden. Among the Tatars, anyone who so much as tastes wine gets eighty blows with a stick and a fine of as many coins.

Certainly among the Tartars, whoever has merely tasted wine is struck eighty blows with the cudgel and fined as many coins.

Daily lifeCrimea; a Tatar village on the Vaka river (Lithuania)c. 1550

Forty brothers, one father: polygamy and a Tatar village

Michalo argues that polygamy is why the Perekop Tatars, so often slaughtered by the Lithuanians, keep springing back. He cites a troop of forty full brothers in the army of Sultan Islam Giray, all sons of one Omeldesz by wives and concubines within little more than a year, and a Tatar village on the Vaka river in Lithuania still called 'Forty Tartars', that is, forty brothers. He adds that both Tatars and Muscovites keep their women without freedom.

this multitude of wives is why our neighbours the Perekop Tartars, so often cut down by us to the last man, spring up again. Not long ago in the forces of Sultan Islam there were forty brothers, his comrades, forty sons at once of a certain Omeldesz

Daily lifePodolia and Kyiv frontierc. 1550

The Tatars' mocking war cry: 'John, you sleep, I tie you up'

Michalo complains that Lithuanian soldiers idle in taverns while Muscovite and Tatar frontier troops stand watch in turns, and that the Tatars mock Lithuanian carelessness with a well-known jibe: 'John, you sleep; I am binding you.' He adds that Lithuania even pays yearly gifts to the Perekop khan.

our enemies the Tartars mock this lazy carelessness of ours with their usual jibe, falling upon us when we lie buried in sleep after our feasting, with these words: 'John, you sleep; I, tying you up...'

Daily lifeCrimeac. 1550

Tatar religion: one God, Christ a prophet, prayer morning to night

Michalo summarises Tatar Islam as a Christian of his day understood it: a law shared with the Turks and other 'Saracens', professing one simple God, Christ as holy prophet and final judge born of a virgin but not crucified, and circumcision at the age Ishmael was circumcised. On the next page he praises their prayer morning, afternoon and evening, their fasting and their alms, and says they laugh at Christian fasts and churches.

It professes one simple God, and believes Christ to be a holy Prophet and the last judge of the world, born of a pure Virgin, but not to have suffered. It observes circumcision.

The nameCrimeac. 1550

Proud of their Scythian descent and divided into hordes

The opening of the work: the Tartars, though Lithuanians think them barbarous and poor, pride themselves on their restraint and on the antiquity of their 'Scythian' race, which they claim never served anyone, and are divided into hordes ('Ordas, that is, nations'). Michalo lists the Perekop (Crimean) Tatars and their Budjak neighbours, the Nogai, the Volga horde (homeland of Batu, 'once ruler of the Muscovites and all Ruthenians'), Kazan, Kazakh, Bukhara and Samarkand hordes, and links their twelve rulers to the twelve princes promised to Ishmael.

Although the Tartars are held by us to be barbarous and poor, they nevertheless glory in the restraint of their life and in the antiquity of their Scythian race, asserting that it is a seed … that has never served anyone … and that it is now divided into various Hordes, that is, nations.

The nameCrimea14th to 16th centuries

The Giray khans: a dynasty 'planted' by Lithuania

Michalo claims the Crimean Christians, weak and luxurious, invited a band of Tartars from the Volga horde to defend them, and that this Tatar power grew until it took the peninsula. He adds that the Crimean khans came from Lithuania: the last, 'Aczkirei' (Haci Giray), born near Trakai and sent by Grand Duke Vytautas to rule, whose descendants all bear the name Giray. He calls the dynasty a stock 'planted there by the brave right hand of Your Majesty's ancestors' that now repays Lithuania with endless trouble.

The last of the khans from Lithuania, Aczkirei, was born near Trakai and sent from here by the late Vytautas to that throne; ruling there in Taurica he fathered a son, Mengli Giray. … This, then, is the stock of the Girays, planted there by the strong right hand of Your Majesty's ancestors

Card wording

firsthand observation: the author served as Lithuanian envoy to the Crimean khanate (c. 1538-1540) and describes Tatar society at Perekop, Kaffa and the Dnieper steppe from experience; written c. 1550 for Sigismund Augustus, printed in fragments 1615

Cautions
  • Pseudonymous author; text survives only as ten fragments selected by the 1615 editor; idealises the Tatars to criticise Lithuanian mores.

Why it matters

Rare insider Latin description of the Crimean khanate's society, law and economy in the 1540s, framed as a moral comparison; a primary source for the 'Tartary' of the northern Black Sea.
