Fischer's Siberian History

An Academy historian's 1768 history of the Russian conquest of Siberia, with a long introduction on who the Tatars and Mongols were.

Catalogue title Siberian History from the Discovery of Siberia to the Conquest of this Land by Russian Arms, read in the sessions of the Academy of Sciences, 2 volumes

Johann Eberhard Fischer

Johann Eberhard Fischer (1697-1771), a German historian at the St Petersburg Academy, travelled in Siberia from 1740 to 1747 with the Second Kamchatka Expedition. His 'Sibirische Geschichte' (St Petersburg 1768, 2 volumes) tells the story of Siberia from its discovery to the Russian conquest, drawing heavily on G. F. Müller's unpublished archive work, which Müller resented. Its long introduction compares Chinese, Persian, Arabic and European sources and Siberian observations to sort out Mongols, Tatars and their religions. This corpus has two BSB copies of the 1768 first edition and the 1774 Russian translation.

Who “Tartar” means here
Fischer insists 'Tatar' was misused by Europeans for all peoples of inner Asia; he separates Mongols from Tatars and uses 'Tataren' for Turkic peoples such as the Siberian, Kacha, Chulym and Baraba Tatars.
Why read it
A sharp, sceptical discussion of the word 'Tatar' itself, including the Tartarus pun and the Chinese 'Ta-ta', written by someone who had lived among Siberian peoples.
Watch out
The narrative history leans on Müller's work, often without credit; the introduction is book learning with some firsthand comparison. Only the introduction was read here.

Daily lifeSouthern Siberia (Kacha, Chulym, Baraba)1768

'Kam', the Tatar word for a shaman since Rubruck's day

Comparing medieval reports with what he saw in Siberia, Fischer notes that the soothsayers Carpini and Rubruck described among the Mongols, who beat drums and went out of themselves, can still be seen among the Yakut shamans. In a footnote he adds that Rubruck's word 'Cham' for such a seer is still used by the Tatars of the Kacha, the Teleuts, and the Chulym and Baraba Tatars for their sorcerer-priests.

Rubruck says, chapter 19, that the word Cham means a soothsayer, and that all people of this sort are called Cham among the Mongols… This name is still in use today among the Tatars on the Kacha river, among the Teleuts, the Chulym people and the Baraba people, and is given to their sorcerer-priests.

The nameAsia1768

'When a European speaks of Tatars, no one knows who is meant'

Fischer complains that Europeans mispronounce the name as 'Tartar', perhaps imagining it comes from Tartarus, an allusion he says is common in old and new histories. Worse, they give it to every Asian people beyond Persia and India up to the Pacific, however different in looks, way of life, religion and language. He even doubts whether any single people ever properly bore the name, noting that Batu's army that invaded Hungary were Mongols, not Tatars.

Perhaps they imagined that the name comes from Tartarus. … So it happens that when a European speaks of Tatars, one does not know whom he means. Because this name is so very general, I have often hesitated whether there was ever a particular people on earth that bore the proper name Tatar.

The nameChina's northern frontier1768

The Mongols disliked the name; the Chinese called all outsiders 'Ta-ta'

Fischer notes that in Europe and western Asia 'Tatar' (or 'Tarter') is better known than 'Mongol', although Chinggis Khan was a Mongol and not a Tatar. The mix-up, he says, is as old as the Mongol empire, and the Mongols themselves did not like to be called Tatars. He suggests the usage may come from the Chinese, who called all peoples living beyond their Great Wall 'Tata'.

Although the Mongols themselves did not like to hear it when they were given the name Tatar … The cause of such naming may well come from the Chinese, who are accustomed to give the name Tata to all peoples living outside their great wall and bordering on China

Strange talesTibet and Mongolia1768

Prester John was a mishearing

Like Strahlenberg before him, Fischer explains the legend of Prester John as a European mistake: the first Europeans in Tibet and Mongolia misheard a title, their fancy shaped it into 'Prêtre Jean' or 'Juan', and so it spread through the world. They forgot that Tibetans and Mongols spoke neither French, Italian nor Portuguese, and were pleased to find what they hoped for; later came strange etymologies and fables of an Indian 'Johannes Presbyter'.

the name Prêtre Jean or Juan was wrongly heard by the first Europeans who came there, then badly shaped by their fancy, and so sent out into the world. It did not occur to these good people that the Tibetans and Mongols among whom they found themselves understood neither French, nor Italian, nor Portuguese

Why it matters

The standard eighteenth-century synthesis of the Russian conquest of Siberia and, in its long introduction, an early comparative-ethnographic attempt to sort out who the Tatars, Mongols and Kalmyks were; frequently plagiarizes Müller, so must be read against the Sammlung.
