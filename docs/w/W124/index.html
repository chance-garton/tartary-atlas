Pallas's journey through the provinces of the Russian Empire

A German naturalist's day-by-day journal of the Academy expedition from the Volga to Siberia and back, 1768 to 1774.

Catalogue title Journey through Various Provinces of the Russian Empire (Reise durch verschiedene Provinzen des Russischen Reichs), 3 parts in 5 volumes

Peter Simon Pallas

Read the 20 passagesOpen the original book

Peter Simon Pallas (1741-1811), a Berlin-born naturalist in the service of the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences, led one of Catherine II's great scientific expeditions from 1768 to 1774. His journal runs from Moscow down the Oka and Volga, through the Orenburg steppe and the Urals, across western Siberia to the Altai, Krasnoyarsk and Lake Baikal, and back by the Volga delta. It mixes botany and geology with close descriptions of Kalmyks, Kazakhs, Bashkirs and many Tatar groups, and with measured drawings of ruins at Kasimov, Bolgar, Saraichik and the Akhtuba. This corpus holds the first edition (St Petersburg, 1771-1776, three parts in five volumes) and a later abridged edition.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Pallas uses 'Tataren' for Turkic-speaking Muslim or shamanist peoples: Kasimov, Kazan and Tomsk Tatars, the Nogai Kundurov Tatars, and the Siberian Kachin, Beltir and other Khakas 'Tatars'. He keeps Kalmyks, Kazakhs (Kirgis) and Bashkirs as separate peoples, though he calls some of their customs 'Tatar'.
Why read it
A trained scientist who measured ruins and described dwellings with plates: he credits stone and brick buildings to Tatar khans, Bolgars, Nogais, Kalmyks and Bukharans, and shows living Tatars in log houses, felt tents and birch-bark yurts.
Watch out
Some sections come from his students (Sokolov, Zuev) or from informants, and he sometimes judges peoples harshly. The Fraktur OCR is damaged and the printed page numbers in the scans do not always match the catalogue page numbers.

CitiesKasimov1768

Kasimov, the old seat of Tatar khans

Pallas notes that the Russian town of Kasimov is built entirely of logs, even its streets are paved with timber, although good building stone lies all along the Oka. The Tatar rulers who once lived here did use the stone, and he found the remains of their court: a gate with Arabic inscriptions, a long stone residence and a mausoleum. The descendants of the old Tatar townspeople still lived in their own quarter as prosperous fur traders.

In the days when Kasimov was still the residence of Tatar rulers, this store of stone was not left so wholly unused, and the town still has fine remains of Tatar buildings which deserve to be preserved. … This place really seems to have been the court of the local khans.

CitiesBolgar1768

Tombstones of Bolgars or Tatars at Bolgari

In December 1768 Pallas visited the ruins of Bolgari on the Volga, which he calls the former capital of the Bolgars, with a rampart about six versts round, a stone minaret and vaulted buildings. The old gravestones there carry mostly Arabic and a few Armenian inscriptions, dated by the Muslim calendar from 619 to 742 (about 1222 to 1341). He reads them as the graves of Bolgars or Tatars of every rank, and notes that some of the dead came from Shemakha and Shirvan, a sign of long-distance trade.

At Bolgari a great number of old gravestones have been found, mostly with Arabic but also some with Armenian inscriptions… The inscriptions contain nothing but a Muslim motto, … the name of the deceased and his descent or rank. They are partly noble Bolgars or Tatars of both sexes, partly clergy, partly common people and perhaps merchants.

Original wording

Man hat bey Bolgari eine Menge alter Grabsteine mehrentheils mit arabischen, jedoch auch einige mit armenischen Aufschriften gefunden… Die Innschriften enthalten nichts, als einen mahometanischen Denkspruch, … den Namen des Verstorbenen und dessen Abstammung oder Würde. Es sind theils vornehme Bolgaren oder Tataren beyderley Geschlechts, theils Geistliche, theils Gemeine und vielleicht Kaufleute.

CitiesSelitrennoye1773

Ruins of a great 'Nogai city' on the Akhtuba

Near the old saltpetre works of Selitrennoi Gorodok on the Akhtuba, Pallas found a stretch of ten versts covered with brick rubble, building traces and walled graves of what he calls a large Nogai town. On the highest mound stood the ruins of a walled palace or castle and of a khan's mausoleum, from whose vaults treasure hunters were said to have dug riches and coffins covered in silver. Modern archaeology identifies this site with Sarai, the capital of the Golden Horde.

in which, along the Akhtuba, for a width of one or even two versts, one sees nothing but brick rubble and traces of buildings or of walled graves of a considerable Nogai town that once stood here.

CitiesSaraichik1769

The brick ruins of Saraichik on the Ural River

At the Cossack outpost of Saraichik on the Yaik (Ural) River, Pallas walked the ruins of what he calls a Nogai city, whose people were said to have migrated to Khiva. Inside a rampart four to five versts round he saw foundations and vaults of brick houses, fragments of glazed, porcelain-like pottery in white, blue and yellow, and small copper and silver coins ruined by the salty soil.

Within the rampart there are many traces, foundations and vaults of masonry buildings, from which it appears that some very handsome dwellings must have stood in the middle of the town. The bricks used are oblong and moulded fairly large… Of old vessels one now finds almost nothing but pieces of broken porcelain-like earthenware with a strong and fine glaze.

BuildingsKasimov1768

A stone minaret and a khan's tomb at Kasimov

In the Tatar quarter Pallas measured a tall round tower (a minaret, which he calls Misgir) of dressed limestone, all that stood of a ruined mosque (Metsched) built of large bricks. On the next page he describes the plain stone mausoleum of the khans with a small prayer room, and reports that the owner of the land had pulled down the carved gate and the khan's house and burned much of the stone for lime.

there still stands in the highest part of the town a tall, strong, round-built tower or Misgir, from a ruined house of prayer… The old house of prayer, or Metsched, was of bricks more than 13 inches long; but the tower is built entirely of smoothly dressed and fairly large blocks of stone.

BuildingsSelitrennoye1773

Glazed tiles and bricks carted off to Astrakhan

Pallas says he had never seen such splendour in any Tatar ruin: brick walls laid with great regularity, the joints inlaid with green, yellow, white and blue glazed ornaments, and a facade with mosaic tiles. He blames time and plunder for the damage, adding that whole boatloads of bricks had been shipped to Astrakhan, and that to get a thousand whole bricks the workers smashed two thousand because the mortar was so hard.

The outer face of the walls is not only inlaid, panel by panel in all the joints, with glazed clay ornaments of green, yellow, white and blue colour in triangular and other figures… But time, and still more the greed of the rabble, long ago turned these memorable remains of antiquity into heaps of stones.

Original wording

Die äussere Seite der Mauern ist von aussen nicht nur Felderweise in allen Steinfugen mit glasurten Thonzierrathen von grüner, gelber, weisser und blauer Farbe in dreyeckigten und andern Figuren eingelegt… Aber die Zeit und noch mehr der Geitz des Pöbels hat diese denkwürdigen Ueberbleibsel des Alterthums vorlängst in Steinhaufen verwandelt.

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)

BuildingsAkhtuba River1773

Tatar tents that ride on two-wheeled carts

Along the Akhtuba Pallas saw the camps of the Kundurov Tatars, Nogais from the Kuban who had stayed behind when many Kalmyks fled east in 1771. Unlike Kalmyk tents, their round felt-and-reed huts could not be taken apart and were small enough to set whole onto a tall two-wheeled cart (arba), which they covered like a parasol. He compares them to the wagon-dwelling 'Hamaxobii' of the ancient geographers and quotes Horace on the Scythians whose carts drag their wandering homes.

Their yurts are very different in shape and construction from those used by the Kalmyks and other Asian nomads. They cannot be taken apart piece by piece, but are all the more lightly built and only so large that they can be set over a big cart.

Working translation

Original wording

Ihre Jurten sind in der Gestalt und Bauart, von denen bey Kalmücken und andern asiatischen Nomaden gebräuchlichen sehr verschieden. Sie können nicht stückweise aus einander genommen werden, sind aber desto leichter gebaut und nur so groß, daß sie über einen grossen Karren gesetzt werden können

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)

BuildingsVolga steppe1769

How a Kalmyk felt tent is put together

Pallas gives a step-by-step account of the Kalmyk tent, keyed to an engraved plate: folding lattice walls of willow rods tied into a circle, a wooden crown ring lifted on long roof rods, the frame painted red, and a large cut felt thrown over the roof like a raincoat. In summer the sides stay open; in cold weather they are wrapped in felt or reed mats.

Then the frame, whose parts are usually painted red, is finished. Over its roof a large felt, cut to fit, is laid round like a raincoat and fastened with ropes slung over it. The sides stay open in summer, but when it is cold pieces of felt or reed mats, or both, are laid around them.

Working translation

Original wording

Alsdenn ist das Gerüste, dessen Theile roth angefärbt zu seyn pflegen fertig. Ueber das Dach desselben wird ein grosser, darnach zugeschnittener Filz, wie ein Regen-Mantel herum gelegt, und mit darüber geschlungenen Seilen befestigt. Die Seiten bleiben im Sommer offen, wenn es aber kalt ist, so werden Stücken Filz, oder Schilfmatten, oder auch beyde darum gelegt

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)

BuildingsKazakh steppe between the Ui and Tobol1770

A brick 'Tatar prayer-house' whose builders were forgotten

Pallas rode out into the steppe beyond the Orenburg line to see a building the Russians called the Tatar Koshena. It was a square brick shrine with a pointed Gothic portal and a twelve-sided funnel dome, standing in a Kazakh cemetery. He thought it was not very old, yet neither the Kazakhs nor the Bashkirs remembered who had built it. On a following page he notes it is all of red brick, much like ordinary European brick.

It is still in perfectly good condition and, at least to judge by the woodwork inside, seems to be of no extraordinary antiquity. Nevertheless the memory of its building has been lost among the Kirgis as well as the Bashkirs.

Original wording

Es ist noch in vollkommen gutem Zustand und scheint, wenigstens dem darinn befindlichen Holzwerk nach, von keinem ausserordentlichen Alterthum zu seyn. Gleichwohl ist unter den Kirgisen sowohl, als Baschkiren das Angedenken ihrer Auferbauung verloschen.

BuildingsSemipalatinsk1771

The 'Seven Palaces' were mud-brick and Bukharan

The fortress of Semipalatinsk ('seven palaces') on the Irtysh was named after ruins twelve versts upstream, famous in earlier travel books. Pallas went to see them: the remains of a few buildings of slate slabs set in clay and of unburnt brick, one a windowless hut, laid out without order. He judged the work poor and plainly Bukharan, and counted the seven 'palaces' only by including rubble heaps.

At present only decayed masonry of three buildings remains; the position of the rest can only be inferred from the rubble; the building style is poor and easily recognised as Bukharan, and the buildings were laid out without any order.

Working translation

Original wording

Gegenwärtig ist nur noch von dreyen Gebäuden verfallenes Mauerwerk vorhanden; die Lage der übrigen läst sich nur noch aus dem Schutt schliessen; die Bauart ist schlecht und leicht vor Bucharisch zu erkennen, auch sind die Gebäude ohne einige Ordnung angelegt gewesen.

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)

About Bukharans (Central Asian settlers), per Pallas

Keep in mind

The identification as Bukharan is Pallas's judgement from the building style; modern scholars link the site to a Zunghar (Oirat) Buddhist monastery.

BuildingsAblaikit (near Ust-Kamenogorsk)1771

Ablaikit, a walled Kalmyk temple in the steppe

Pallas sent his student Sokolov to measure the ruins of Ablaikit, seventy versts from Ust-Kamenogorsk, and printed an abstract of his report with plans. It was a Buddhist temple and residence built by the Kalmyk prince Ablai, surrounded by a wall of stacked slate slabs that climbed over the crags, with a temple on a burnt-brick platform. Kazakhs and Russian hunting parties had since wrecked much of it.

Ablaikit lies outside the border of the Russian Empire, in the desert formerly inhabited by the Zunghar Kalmyks but now fallen to the Kirgis of the Middle Horde… The historical circumstances of its building by a Kalmyk prince, Ablai, and of the later destruction of this residence

BuildingsKrasnoyarsk region (upper Chulym basin)1771

Birch-bark summer yurts and earth-covered winter huts

East of Tomsk, Pallas describes the dwellings of baptised Tatars: summer yurts of poles or bent birch rods shaped like a Kalmyk tent but covered with boiled and stitched white birch bark, and winter huts of thin birch logs shaped like a box, heaped with earth for warmth, with a clay-and-brushwood fireplace and a square smoke hole.

The baptised Tatars here live in summer in wretched yurts made partly of poles leaned together to a point, partly of birch rods fixed and bent from the ground up around some large hoops, which take the shape of a Kalmyk tent and are covered with strongly boiled, sewn-together white birch bark.

Original wording

Die hiesigen getauften Tataren wohnen im Sommer in elenden Jurten, die theils aus spitz zusammen gestüzten Stangen, theils aus Birkenstäben die von der Erde herauf um einige grosse Reife befestigt und gebrochen, die Gestalt eines Calmückischen Zeltes vorstellen, und mit stark gekochten und zusammen genehten weissen Birkenrinden gedeckt werden, bestehen.

Daily lifeKhakassia (Yenisei-Abakan steppe)1772

A Kachin shaman in ribbons and printed cotton

Pallas saw a young Kachin shaman perform at the Karysh mine. The man was said to have been mad, or to have played mad, for years before taking up the craft, and still sometimes 'witched' all night until he collapsed. As a beginner he had no drum, only a paddle-shaped rattle stick; a shaman earned the right to a drum by sacrificing a horse and stretching its hide over it.

I saw the costume of a young Kachin sorcerer, who for many years before taking up the trade had been mad or had pretended to be mad, at Karysh… He performed his tricks without a cap and wore only brightly embroidered leather stockings and on his body a tight, very dirty smock of brightly printed cotton cloth (kitaika).

Daily lifeVolga steppe1769

Kalmyk women pitch the tent and saddle the horse

Pallas describes the inside of the tent, with its iron tripod over the fire and cast-iron pans bought from Russian ironworks, then the division of labour. Men only make and mend the tents; women milk, dress hides, sew, strike and raise the tent, and even saddle the husband's horse.

Housework falls on the female sex alone. The men busy themselves with nothing but mending and making the huts… The women must take down the hut, pack up everything and put the hut up again. The wife must even saddle her husband's horse and lead it to the hut when he wants to ride out.

Working translation

Original wording

Die häusliche Arbeit liegt bloß dem weiblichen Geschlecht ob. Die Männer bemühen sich in nichts, als in Ausbesserung und Verfertigung der Hütten… Das Weibsvolk muß die Hütte abnehmen, alle Sachen aufpacken, und auch die Hütte wieder aufstellen. Sogar muß das Weib dem Manne das Pferd satteln und vor die Hütte führen, wenn er verreisen will.

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)

Daily lifeKazakh steppe between the Ui and Tobol1770

Horsehair and bullets left at a holy place

At the same brick shrine birch poles were hung with tufts of horsehair and rags, and the ledges held pebbles, lead bullets, an arrow and gunpowder. Pallas explains that the Kazakhs believe leaving hair from a horse's tail at a holy place helps the horse, and that leaving some small thing is a general Tatar custom. The Tatars of his own escort added offerings after praying.

For the Kirgis believe that it is good for their horse if some hair from its tail is left at so holy a place; and in general it is a Tatar custom, at such prayer-houses or other holy places they visit out of devotion, to leave behind something they carry with them.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Kirgisen nehmlich glauben, daß es ihrem Pferde zuträglich ist, wenn etwas Haar von deßen Schweif an einem so heiligen Ort zurück gelassen wird, und überhaupt ist es eine tatarische Gewohnheit bey solchen Bethäusern, oder andern heiligen Plätzen, die sie der Andacht wegen besuchen, irgend etwas, so sie bey sich führen, zurück zu lassen.

Daily lifeKhakassia (Yenisei-Abakan steppe)1772

Kachin weddings and the bride who must lie flat

Pallas reports that the Kachin Tatars, rich herders who had not taken up farming or Christianity, keep one wife, court for years, and hold two weddings. The groom's father must set up a new yurt for the couple, while the bride's father supplies the household goods. After the betrothal the father-in-law never sees his son's wife again, and if they meet she must throw herself flat on the ground until he has passed.

The first wedding is held at the bride's father's, the second at the groom's, where the groom's father must set up a new yurt for the young couple… Except at the first wooing the father-in-law never sees his son's wife again, never goes into the son's yurt, and if he does meet his daughter-in-law, custom requires that she throw herself flat on the ground.

Working translation

Original wording

Die erste Hochzeit wird beym Brautvater, die andre beym Bräutigam gefeyert, wo des Bräutigams-Vater dem jungen Paar eine neue Jurte aufstellen muß… Ausser bey der ersten Freywerberey sieht der Schwiegervater seines Sohns Frau niemals wieder, geht nie in die Jurte des Sohns und geschieht es ja, daß er seiner Schwiegertochter begegnet, so will die Gewohnheit, daß sich diese platt auf die Erde niederwirft

Daily lifeIzh River, lower Kama1773

A model farm run by a Tatar general

Passing through villages owned by Major General Tevkelev, a Muslim of Tatar descent whose serfs were all Tatars, Pallas praises the order and cleanliness of the grain stores, sheaves, fields and even the peasants' households as an example of good husbandry, unusual in the region. The general was away celebrating the Tatar ploughing festival, Saban.

The last three villages belong to Major General Tevkelev, who is of Tatar descent and an adherent of the Muslim religion, and has nothing but Tatars among his numerous subjects.

The nameTomsk1771

The Tomsk Tatars call themselves Chat

Stopping in the Muslim Tatar village of Kaltai near Tomsk, Pallas notes that the six or seven hundred Tatar families of the Tomsk area call themselves Tschaty and differ little from the Kazan Tatars in dress, customs and religion. Their women wear a closed cap over the veil, and girls wear up to thirty braids at their wedding.

The Tatars living here, of whom about six to seven hundred families in all live in and around Tomsk, call themselves Tschaty and differ little from those of Kazan in their dress and customs, as in their religion.

Original wording

Die hier wohnenden Tataren, deren in und um Tomsk in allem etwan sechs bis sieben hundert Familien wohnen, nennen sich Tschaty und sind in ihrer Tracht und Sitten, so wie in der Religion, von den Casanischen wenig unterschieden.

Strange talesAbakan steppe near Askiz1772

Feeding butter to a stone woman's mouth

On the Abakan steppe, crowded with ancient graves fenced with stone slabs, Pallas found carved standing stones which, like other stone images on the Yenisei steppes, were said to have been set up long before the time of the Kirgis. One, a tall bellied stone with a large open-mouthed woman's face, was called Kurtejak-tash, the 'woman stone'. Hunters heading out for sable prayed to it for luck and smeared fat or butter into its mouth.

This stone is known among the heathen Tatars of this region by the name Kurtejak-tash (woman stone) or Ulu-Kurtejak (the great woman), and especially in autumn it is earnestly asked for luck by the heathens travelling past to the sable hunt, and fed with a little fat or butter, which the worshippers devoutly smear into the open mouth of the face.

Working translation

Original wording

Diese Wake ist unter den Nahmen Kurtejaktasch (Weiberstein) oder Ulu-Kurtejak (das grosse Weib) bey den heydnischen Tataren dieser Gegend bekannt, und wird sonderlich im Herbst, von den auf die Zobeljagd vorbey reisenden Heyden, fleißig um Glück angesprochen und mit etwas Fett oder Butter, welches die Anbeter in den ofnen Mund des Gesichts andächtig schmieren, gefuttert.

Strange talesSelitrennoye1773

A saint's mound that heals the sick

Half a verst from the ruins stood a large, dug-out burial mound where the local Tatars went on pilgrimage, saying a Muslim saint lay buried there. They told Pallas the saint still worked miracles, healed the sick and appeared to them in dreams. Pallas reports this as their belief and dismisses it as the fruit of imagination.

The said Tatars maintain that the saint still works miracles, heals the sick and sometimes appears to them in their sleep; which will be the same case as with so many other holy apparitions and miracles that imagination bore so fruitfully among men in unenlightened times.

Working translation

Original wording

Obgedachte Tataren behaupten, daß der heilige noch itzt Wunder thue, Kranke heile, und ihnen zuweilen im Schlaf erscheine; womit es eben so, wie mit so vielen andern heiligen Erscheinungen und Wundern, welche die Einbildungskraft unter den Menschen in wenig aufgeklärten Zeiten so fruchtbar gebahr, beschaffen seyn wird.

Peter Simon Pallas (Reise, first edition, St Petersburg 1771-1776)

Why it matters

The core Academy expedition journal for the Volga-Ural-Siberia belt in 1768-1774: eyewitness description of Kalmyk hordes, Bashkirs, Kazakh steppe frontier lines, Siberian Tatars, Buryats and the Kiakhta border, with plates and maps; the German original of the material the English pass only knows through Pallas's later southern journey (W005).
