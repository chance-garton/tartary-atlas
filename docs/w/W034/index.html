Baron von Haxthausen's travels through Russia in 1843

A Prussian expert on peasant life tours Russia in 1843 and visits the Tatars of Kazan, the Volga ruins and a Nogai village.

Catalogue title The Russian Empire: Its People, Institutions and Resources

August von Haxthausen

Read the 13 passagesOpen the original book

August von Haxthausen (1792-1866), a Westphalian baron who studied rural institutions, toured Russia in 1843 with the backing of Nicholas I's government. He travelled from Moscow and Yaroslavl down the Volga to Kazan and Saratov, then south to the Mennonite colonies and the Crimea. He writes as an eyewitness, helped by officials and local informants such as the Kazan naturalist von Fuchs. His Tatar material covers the Tatar quarter of Kazan, Tatar villages, the ruins of Bolgar, reports about Sarai, and a Nogai village rebuilt on a German model. This corpus copy is volume I of Robert Farie's English translation (London, 1856) of the German original (1847 onward).

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Muslim Volga Tatars of Kazan and its villages, plus Tatar military settlers near Penza and the Nogai Tatars of the southern steppe. In history passages he uses 'Tatars' and 'Mongols' together for the Golden Horde.
Why read it
A calm, close-up picture of settled Tatar townspeople and farmers in the 1840s: their houses, food, schools, marriage customs and mosques, and what a traveller then saw of the ruined Tatar and Bulgar cities of the Volga.
Watch out
Haxthausen travelled as a guest of officials, pushed his own theories about Russian communal life and Christian mission, and uses the race language of his time. His remarks on Sarai and Bolgar history are secondhand.

What it says: 13 passages

CitiesSarai (lower Volga, Akhtuba)13th century (reported 1843)

Sarai, the lost capital of the Golden Horde

In Saratov a local official showed Haxthausen coins and ornaments dug from the 'supposed ruins' of Sarai. He then retells its history: Batu founded the city, the khans built magnificent palaces, and Russian grand dukes came to kneel there. On the next page he marvels that nobody could yet say for certain where this great capital had stood.

Batu Khan, the Grand Khan of the Golden Horde, who ruled over the half of two hemispheres, founded in the thirteenth century a powerful city, Sarai, and fixed his residence there. He and his successors built palaces of fabulous magnificence. Princes, among others the Grand Dukes of Russia, were obliged to come and bend the knee before their lord and swear fealty to him

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Khans of the Golden Horde (Mongols and Tatars)

Keep in mind

The history is Haxthausen's summary of received accounts, not something he saw; 'palaces of fabulous magnificence' is his phrase, not a description of surviving remains.

CitiesKazan1843 (fire of 1842)

Kazan, a city of log houses, burned to the ground

Arriving in June 1843, Haxthausen calls Kazan 'the gloriously situated old Tatar city', but finds it still in ruins from the great fire of 1842. His count is striking: only about one house in nine was stone, the rest were timber, and wooden plank walkways carried the flames from street to street.

Kazan still lay for the most part in ruins ; a great fire the year before had reduced the vast city to ashes. … Of 4500 houses in Kazan only about 500 were built of stone; the rest were log-houses. The streets, with few exceptions, were unpaved

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

CitiesKazan1843

Once the rulers, now pushed to the suburbs

Haxthausen notes that the Tatars, who ruled Kazan before the Russian conquest of 1552, now live in their own quarter outside the Russian town proper. They are mostly traders, and he met rich merchant families among them.

The Tatars in Kazan live mostly together, in a separate quarter of the town. Formerly the ruling people, they have been driven into the suburbs, whilst the town proper is inhabited by Russians. They are traders rather than artisans ; among them are merchants of all the guilds.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

BuildingsLower Volga (Akhtuba valley, Kazan to Astrakhan)1843 (describing the 13th to 15th centuries)

Ruined Tatar towns along the Volga

On the left bank of the Volga, Haxthausen says, the Tatars were not only nomads: they had fixed towns and farms, as shown by ruins and by place names. The Akhtuba valley where Sarai stood is covered with ruins for about 85 versts (roughly 90 km). When the Horde broke up, the towns were destroyed and the settlers driven off by Kirghiz and Bashkir raiders, leaving only ruins and broken mosques.

This is evident not merely from the ruins of cities (the whole district on the Akhtuba, where Sarai stood, for eighty-five versts, is covered with ruins), but the names of nearly all denote that the Tatars had fixed settlements here. When the Mongol-Tatar empire was dissolved, the cities were destroyed … only here and there a ruin or dilapidated mosque bore witness to their former existence.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Tatars of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

He reports this from local knowledge and place names while passing through the Saratov region; he did not visit the Akhtuba ruins himself.

BuildingsBolgar (Bolghari), Tatarstan24 June 1843

The minaret of Bolgar, built by a vanished people

Haxthausen climbed the surviving minaret at Bolgar, which he credits to the old Bulgarian state destroyed by the Mongols and Tatars. The ruins stand beside a Russian church, Kazan Tatars still make pilgrimages to the saints' tombs there, and Alexander von Humboldt had scratched his name on the minaret wall.

The powerful and civilized Bulgarian people who founded this important city have quite disappeared, and the Russians, Tatars, Tchuvash, etc. have taken their place ; all trace of their physiognomy, customs, and language is lost. … We ascended the lofty minaret, which commands an extensive view over boundless forests.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Volga Bulgars; Kazan Tatar pilgrims

Keep in mind

His claim that the Bulgars left no trace is his own opinion; the Kazan Tatars themselves (see his p.323) were often described as partly descended from them.

BuildingsYepan Ashino, near KazanJune 1843

Russian stoves, Tatar benches: a village house

Lodging in a large Tatar peasant house, Haxthausen found it split into a men's half and a women's half. He noticed it looked much like a Russian house and honestly admits he cannot tell who borrowed from whom. The rooms mixed Russian stoves, Tatar sleeping benches, a samovar, European chairs and a Black Forest clock, with 'God is great' written over the door.

This was divided into two parts ; the right half is the dwelling of the men (our host lived with four of his brothers), the left that of the women and children. It bore much resemblance to Russian arrangements : whether the Russians borrowed of the Tatars, or the latter of the former, cannot be determined. In the rooms the stoves were Russian, the sleeping-benches Tatar

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

BuildingsAkeima, Molochna river, southern Ukraine1843

A Nogai village built on a Mennonite plan

Near the Mennonite colonies on the Molochna, Haxthausen visited a Nogai Tatar village that looked 'perfectly German'. The Mennonite farmer Johann Cornies had persuaded the formerly nomadic Nogais to build this way and, Haxthausen was told, had already settled 17,000 of them. Solid houses with chimneys, poplars, gardens and Mennonite ploughs had replaced the old steppe life.

The houses in this village were all built with regularity and solid structure ; they had chimneys and enclosed courtyards. In front of the house-doors generally stand two poplars, with small flower-beds on each side ; in the gardens I noticed a great many fruit-trees, and in the courtyard ploughs, harrows, and carts of Mennonite construction

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Nogai Tatars

Keep in mind

The figure of 17,000 settled Tatars was told to him, not counted.

BuildingsKazan19 June 1843

Inside a Tatar merchant's house in Kazan

Hoping to see a 'genuine national household', Haxthausen is taken to the home of two merchant brothers and finds the rooms furnished in European style, with Chinese porcelain, mirrors, a Persian carpet and a stone bowl inscribed with Koran verses. He glimpsed a six-foot-wide family sleeping bench, but the women's rooms were closed to him.

I wished to see a genuine national household, but this no longer exists, at least in Kazan, as the people here too are already affected by European cultivation. The furniture of the rooms into which we were conducted was quite European … In one of the glass cases was a stone bowl, inscribed with texts from the Koran.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

Daily lifeKazan1843

Horseflesh, honey mead and the tea trade

Kazan Tatars, Haxthausen reports, avoid pork as Muslims but prize horseflesh above all, brew mead, and control much of the tea trade. On the same page he admires Kazan leather boots embroidered with gold and silver and calls Tatar peasants excellent beekeepers.

They live principally upon animal food, avoiding pork, which is forbidden by the Koran ; horseflesh is considered by the common Tatars the choicest food. They are very fond of honey, and make good mead from it. The higher classes drink much tea ; and a large proportion of the tea-trade being in their hands, the best sorts are found among them.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

Daily lifeYepan Ashino, near Kazan1843

Bride price and wives in Tatar villages

In the village he notes that women walked about unveiled, that village men rarely had more than one wife, and that a bride price (kalym, which he writes 'halim') was paid, sometimes 500 roubles even among peasants. He adds that the Tatars kept some village arrangements secret from Russian officials.

In the Tatar villages the men have rarely more than one wife ; in the towns, particularly among the rich merchants, bigamy frequently occurs, but to have more than two wives is very rare. The women are sold, and the halim paid for them, which even in the case of peasants frequently amounts to five hundred roubles.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Kazan Tatars

Keep in mind

'The women are sold' is his gloss on the bride-price custom, based on what villagers told him.

Daily lifeKazan1843

Tatar, the French of the East

Haxthausen was impressed that nearly all Kazan Tatars could read and write, that their village schools were run by mullahs, and that students went to Orenburg and Bukhara. In a footnote he claims their Turkic language worked as a common tongue from Persia to China and even Tunis.

The Tatar language occupies the same place in the East as the French in the West. Eastwards from Persia to China, westwards throughout all the Turkish countries, and even in Tunis, any one can make himself understood by means of it.

August von Haxthausen, footnote (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Kazan Tatars and Turkic speakers generally

Keep in mind

An enthusiastic generalisation: related Turkic languages were widely understood, but not one single 'Tatar' language everywhere he names.

The nameKazan1843

Bulgars and Mongols merged into Tatars

Haxthausen explains who the Kazan Tatars are: a mixed people in whom the old Volga Bulgar population and the Mongol conquerors have blended. He quotes Herberstein calling them 'half Mongols', but his own description (oval faces, curved noses, light complexions) mostly contradicts a Mongol look, apart from the eyes.

The Kazan Tatars belong, both mentally and physically, to the nobler races of mankind ; they are a mixed people, and possess high mental qualities, but Mohammedanism only permits their development in a limited degree. The ancient Bulgarian and Mongol population has been completely merged in the Tatar. The aspect of the Tatars clearly indicates their consanguinity with the Mongols : Herberstein describes them as half Mongols.

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Kazan Tatars

Keep in mind

Uses the racial ranking language of the 1840s; his origins theory is his own opinion.

Strange talesMoscow1554 (legend reported 1843)

The cathedral for Kazan and the blinded architect

St Basil's Cathedral on Red Square was built to celebrate Ivan the Terrible's capture of Kazan, the Tatar capital, in 1552. Haxthausen compares it to a rainbow-coloured dragon and repeats the tale that Ivan had the architect blinded so he could never build anything finer. He clearly marks it as hearsay ('is said to').

Ivan Vasilievitch ordered it to be erected in 1554 by an Italian, to commemorate the conquest of Kazan. After it was completed, he is said to have asked the architect whether he would undertake to make a plan for a still more singular building ; and when the latter in his vanity answered in the affirmative, he ordered his eyes to be put out

August von Haxthausen (translated by Robert Farie, 1856)

About Ivan IV; the conquered Kazan Tatars

Keep in mind

Russian tradition names the builders as the Russian masters Barma and Postnik, not an Italian, and the blinding story is a legend with no contemporary evidence.
