Two Venetians among the Tatars: Barbaro at Tana and Contarini at Astrakhan

Venetian merchant-diplomat Giosafat Barbaro's sixteen years at Tana (Azov) from 1436, and envoy Ambrogio Contarini's perilous return through Tatar Astrakhan in 1476.

Full title Travels to Tana and Persia

Giosafat Barbaro; Ambrogio Contarini

Read the 19 passagesOpen the original book

Giosafat Barbaro lived as a merchant at the Venetian colony of Tana on the Don from 1436 to about 1452, dealt daily with the Golden Horde, rode out to its camp, and later went as envoy to Persia (1473 to 1478). He wrote his account in 1487. Ambrogio Contarini, another Venetian envoy to Persia, crossed the Crimean Tatar steppe in 1474 and came home in 1476 via Derbent, Astrakhan and Moscow. This 1873 Hakluyt Society volume, edited by Lord Stanley of Alderley, prints Barbaro in William Thomas's Tudor English translation (c. 1550) and Contarini in a new translation by S. A. Roy.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Tatars of the Golden Horde and its successors: the great camp ('Lordo') of khans like Küchük Muhammad on the Don and Volga steppe, the khans of Astrakhan and Kazan, the Crimean Tatars, and the Nogai-like 'wild Tartars' beyond the Volga. Barbaro also lumps northern forest peoples in with 'Tartariens'.
Why read it
The best fifteenth-century eyewitness pictures of a Tatar horde on the move: houses on carts, a camp like a city, nomads who still sowed grain, their justice and food, and what Tatar towns such as Astrakhan looked like.
Watch out
Barbaro wrote decades after the events, and Thomas's Tudor English is abbreviated and hard to read in OCR (quotes here expand marks like 'w^th'). Contarini travelled frightened and hostile, and his disgust colours his descriptions.

CitiesAzov1438

'He that is afraid builds towers'

Editorial contextBarbaro insists the horde was not a band of vagabonds: it carried clothiers, smiths and armourers, and its camps looked like great fair cities, only without walls. When he showed a Tatar merchant the fine tower over Tana's gate, the man smiled and said towers are built by the fearful. Barbaro thought he was right.

In this armie are many artisanes, as clothiers, smythes, armorers, and of all other craftes and things that they neede. … For (saving that they are not walled about) they seeme verie great and faire cities. … I saied vnto a Tartarien marchant … howe thinkest thou, is not this a faire thinge ? But he, smiling, againe answered, he that is afearde buyldeth towres

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

CitiesAstrakhanc. 1440s (written 1487)

Astrakhan, great before Tamerlane

Editorial contextBarbaro calls Astrakhan ('Cithercan') a little town almost destroyed, but once great and famous: before Timur (Tamerlane) sacked it, spices and silk came up through it to Tana and were shipped to Venice. It is a clear statement that a Tatar trading city existed, flourished and was ruined by a known conqueror.

the ryver Ledil, whereon standeth Cithercan, which at this present is but a litle towne in maner destroied ; albeit, that in tyme passed it hath been great and of great fame. For, before it was destroied by Tamerlano, the spices and silke that passe nowe through Soria came to Cithercan, and from thense to Tana

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

CitiesKazan1487

Kazan, the 'cauldron' town of furs

Editorial contextBarbaro notes the turn of the tide: the Russians once paid tribute to the Tartar emperor, but Moscow had now subdued Kazan on the Volga. He calls Kazan a town of great trade, the source of most furs sent to Moscow, Poland, Prussia and Flanders, and says its name means 'cauldron'.

nowe they haue subdued a towne called Cassan (which, in our tonge, signifieth a cawldron), that standeth on the ryver Ledil, on the lefte hande as you go towards the Sea of Bachu … This is a towne of great merchaundise. From whense cometh the most parte of the furres that are caried to Musco and into Polonia, Prusia, and Flandres

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 33)

About Kazan Tatars; Russians of Moscow

Keep in mind

Moscow's 1487 capture of Kazan installed a client khan; the city was not annexed until 1552.

CitiesCrimeac. 1440s

The Crimean Tatars' two walled places

Editorial contextBarbaro says the Crimean plain was ruled by Tatars who could raise three or four thousand horse and had two walled but weak places: Solkhat, also called Crimea ('Incremin', meaning fortress), and 'Cherchiarde', meaning forty places. These are Eski Kırım and Kırk Yer (Chufut-Kale), early seats of the Crimean khans.

The champaigne of this He of Capha is vnder the Tartariens domynion … they haue twoo places walled, but not stronge, thone whereof is called Sorgathi, which they also called Incremin, that signifieth a forteresse ; and thother Cherchiarde, which signifieth xl placs.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 27)

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

'He' is the OCR's reading of 'Ile' (isle), Barbaro's word for the Crimean peninsula; the identifications with Eski Kırım and Kırk Yer are modern.

BuildingsDon steppe near Azov1438

Tall carts carrying round felt houses

Editorial contextBarbaro watched the whole horde pass Tana and describes its vehicles: two-wheeled carts taller than Italian ones, closed with reed mats and covered with felt or cloth depending on rank. Some carried a house built on a timber ring with half-hoops above, filled with reed mats and covered in felt, which was lifted off when they camped.

This people carie with them innumerable cartes of twoo wheeles higher than ours be, which are closed with mattes made of reades, and parte covered with felte, parte with clothe … Some of these cartes carie their houses vpon them which are made on this wise. They take a cercle of tymber, whose dyameter is a pase and an halfe, crossed within foorthe with other halfe cercles

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

BuildingsDon steppe near Azov1437

Burial mounds with stone crosses, credited to the Alans

Editorial contextDescribing the land around Tana, Barbaro says the countless mounds on the steppe are graves, each topped with a pierced stone holding a stone cross. He credits them to the Alans, a Christian people destroyed by the Tartars, not to the Tartars themselves.

These have been Christen men, and were chased awaie and destroied by the Tartares. In that region are hills, ryvers, and plaines : wheare are to be seene an infinite nombre of little hills forced in signe or steede of sepultures, and on the toppe of everie of them a great stone with an hole : wherein standeth a crosse of one peece made of an other stone.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 5)

About Alans (As), 'chased awaie and destroied by the Tartares'

Keep in mind

The crosses may be later Christian additions or Barbaro's reading of stone markers; most steppe kurgans are far older than the Alans.

BuildingsAstrakhan1476

Astrakhan: a low wall and a few brick houses

Editorial contextContarini spent May to August 1476 at Astrakhan ('Citracan'). He says it belonged to three nephews of the Tatar emperor who wintered there and roamed the steppe in summer. The town was small, with a low wall and a few brick houses, but showed signs of larger buildings not long before, and was said to have once been a great trading place.

These three brothers remain in Citracan a few months in the winter, but in the summer do like the rest. Citracan is a small town situated on the Volga, and surrounded by a low wall. The few houses it contains are built of bricks ; but it is evident that it possessed several edifices at no very distant period.

Ambrogio Contarini (translated by S. A. Roy, 1873)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppec. 1436 to 1452

Nomads who sowed and reaped

Editorial contextBarbaro answers the obvious question of where so many people got their grain. Each February the horde announced where sowing would happen; volunteers went with seed, carts and families, ploughed and sowed, and later returned to harvest while the khan's camp circled nearby. He says the yields were huge.

About the mooneth of Februarie they make proclamacons throughout the Lordo, that he which woll sowe shall prepare his things necessarie against the mooneth of Marche, to sowe in such a place. … And there do they eare, sowe, and tarie, till they haue furnisshed that they came for, which doon they retorne to their Lordo.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Daily lifeLower VolgaAugust 1476

Unwashed lamb and mare's milk that 'stank most horribly'

Editorial contextHiding from the khan's search on a Volga island, the starving Contarini was fed by his Tatar guide's people. They killed a lamb for him and cooked it without washing it, saying washing spoils the flavour. They pressed mare's milk on him as a strengthening drink, and were offended when he refused it.

a lamb was killed in my honour, which was partly roasted and partly boiled, but no trouble whatever was taken to wash the flesh, as they say that washing takes all the flavour away … Mare's milk was also brought, which they hold in great esteem … as they say it gives great strength to man; but, as it stank most horribly, I refused to taste it

Ambrogio Contarini (translated by S. A. Roy, 1873)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1438

Justice on the spot

Editorial contextIn the camp, Barbaro says, quarrels were settled without violence or paperwork: the parties walked off together and asked the first respectable man they met to judge, and his verdict was accepted, with bystanders as witnesses. He also describes petitioners kneeling repeatedly as they approached the lord's pavilion.

Whan a difference groweth betwene partie and partie, and wordes multiplied (not aftre the maner of our quarters, for these do vse no violence), thei both or moo (if they be moo) arise and go what waie they thinke good : and to the first man of any estimacon that they meete they saie : Master, do vs right, for we here are in controversie

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppec. 1380s to 1430s

Islam arrives, idols linger

Editorial contextBarbaro says Islam had taken hold among the Tartars only about a hundred years before he wrote, in the time of the warlord Edigu. Before that, some were Muslims but each believed what he liked, and some worshipped idols of wood and rags carried on their carts. On another page a Tatar tells him there are still plenty of secret idolaters.

The faith of Macomett beganne to take place amonge the Tartariens about an C yeres past. In dede some of them were Macomettanes before, but everie man was at his libertie to believe what hym best liked ; so that some worshipped ymags of woode, and of ragges

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 8)

About Tatars of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind

Historians usually date the Golden Horde's official conversion to Khan Uzbek (1313); Barbaro's timeline reflects what he was told.

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppec. 1436 to 1452

The 'valiant fool'

Editorial contextThe bravest Tatar warriors, Barbaro says, earned the title 'Tulubagator', which he translates 'valiant fool', an honour like 'the wise' or 'the handsome' in Italy. The editor explains it as tulu bahadur; bahadur is the root of the Russian bogatyr, hero. He follows it with the story of a Tatar merchant who chased forty Circassians alone.

The hablemen are verie valiaunt and hardie, in such wise that some of them for their excellencie are called Tulubagator, which signifieth a valiaunt foole : being a name of no lesse reputacon amongst them than the sernames of wisedome or beaultie with vs

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 16)

Daily lifeDnieper RiverMay 1474

Rafts towed by swimming horses

Editorial contextTravelling to Kaffa with a Lithuanian embassy escorted by Crimean Tatar horsemen, Contarini had to cross the broad river dividing Russia from Tartary. The Tatars built a raft of lashed timbers and branches, then swam their horses across holding their necks, with the raft tied to the horses' tails. He thought he had never been in greater danger.

the Tartars began to cut timbers, which they tied together, and covered with branches to form a raft, and our things being placed on it the Tartars entered the river holding on to their horses' necks, while our raft was attached to their tails by cords. Thus mounted, the horses were driven across the river, which we passed by the help of God.

Ambrogio Contarini (translated by S. A. Roy, 1873)
See the original page (p. 114)

Daily lifeAzovc. 1436 to 1452 (letter of 1491)

Baltracan, the plant that fed travellers

Editorial contextIn a letter to the Bishop of Padua, Barbaro describes 'Baltracan', a wild plant the Tartars depended on when crossing the steppe. When it sprouted, merchants set out saying 'Let us go, for the Baltracan has grown', and no one chased a runaway slave because he could live on it. He says he ate it himself and later found it in Albania and near Padua.

The Tartars have a plant in their country which they call Baltracan, the want of which would cause them great suffering, and prevent them from going from place to place, especially across those great deserts and solitudes, where they find nothing to eat except this plant, which supports them and gives them vigour.

Giosafat Barbaro, letter of 1491 (translated 1873)

The nameBeyond the Volga1476

The 'wild Tartars' with waist-long hair

Editorial contextContarini separates the Tatars of the 'Lordo' between the Don and Volga, whom he calls brutes who live on milk and meat, from another numerous people beyond the Volga, called 'wild Tartars', with hair to their waists. He admits he did not visit the Lordo himself and relies on what he was told.

But there is said to be another tribe of Tartars living beyond the Volga, in an east-north-easterly direction, who are supposed to be very numerous. They wear long hair reaching to their waists, and are called wild Tartars. They wander in search of pasturage and water like the others; and, in the winter, when there is much cold and ice, they are said to come as far as Citracan

Ambrogio Contarini (translated by S. A. Roy, 1873)

Strange talesDon steppe near Azov1437 to 1438

Venetian treasure-hunters dig a kurgan

Editorial contextTipped off by a story that a Tatar woman in Cairo had told of Alan treasure, Barbaro and six partners hired 120 men and dug into a great mound. Instead of gold they found layers of ash, millet husks and fish scales, then stone vessels, glass-covered brick beads and part of a silver ewer. The dig was so big that locals renamed the mound 'the cave of the Franks'.

we founde in the botome certein vessels of stone, some of them with asshes, some with coles, some emptie and some full of fishe back bones. We founde also v or vi beadestones as bigge as oranges made of bricke and covered with glasse … We founde also halfe the handle of a little ewer of sylver, made with an adders hedde on the toppe.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 8)

About Alans; Venetian merchants of Tana

Keep in mind

The treasure story came at third hand; Barbaro repeats the local explanation that the Alan lord Indiabu faked a grave to hide his wealth from the Tartar emperor.

Strange talesVenice1455

A rescued Tatar slave in a Venetian wine cellar

Editorial contextIn a Rialto wine cellar Barbaro found two chained men who looked like Tartars, runaway slaves of the Catalans recaptured by the vintner. He had them freed by the magistrates and took them home. One turned out to be a servant of the khan's customs officer at Tana, who recognised Barbaro's Tana name, Jusuph, and fell to the ground.

I asked him what was his name. He answered, Chebechzi, which signifieth a bulter of meale. And whan I had well behelde him, I saied vnto him, doest thou knowe me ? He answered, no. But, as soone as I mentioned Tana and Jusuph (for so they called me there), he fell to thearthe, and wolde haue kissed my feete : saieng vnto me, thou hast saved my lief twies

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)

Strange talesBeijingc. 1436

Cut in half for stealing a pail of milk

Editorial contextA Tartar ambassador returning from Cathay lodged in Barbaro's house at Tana. His servant told 'wonders' of Chinese justice: a man who drank a widow's milk in the market was seized and cut in two on the spot. Barbaro passes it on as the ambassador's report, not something he saw.

being on a daye in Madian, which signifieth the markett place, they did see a woman carieng a payle of mylke on her heade, to whom one came that tooke it from her : and beginneng to drynke, she beganne to crye out … Wherevpon, he was incontinently taken, and with a swearde cutt in sonder by the myddest

Giosafat Barbaro, reporting a Tartar ambassador's servants (Tudor translation by William Thomas)

Strange talesMiddle Volga15th century

A horse stuffed with straw and worshipped

Editorial contextBarbaro says the northern lands of Zagatai and Moxia were inhabited by Tartars who were mostly idolaters, then describes a Moxii rite: a horse staked down and shot with arrows, eaten, its hide stuffed with straw and propped up as if alive. On the next page he says it was set on a scaffold in a tree and worshipped, with furs hung as offerings, and that other idolater Tartars worship images carried on their carts.

At a certein tyme of the yere they vse to take a horse : which they laie alonge on the plaine. … Than they stufe the hyde so full of strawe, that it seemeth hole again ; and in every of his legges putt a pece of woodde ; and so sett him afoote againe, as though he were on lyve.

Giosafat Barbaro (Tudor translation by William Thomas, c. 1550)
See the original page (p. 33)

About The Moxii (Moksha Mordvins), grouped by Barbaro with northern 'idolater' Tartars

Keep in mind

The Moksha are a Finnic people, not Tatars; Barbaro says he 'learned' this, not that he saw it.
