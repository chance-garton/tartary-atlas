Gerhard Friedrich Müller's History of the Siberian Tsardom

The first scholarly history of Siberia, written from archives, chronicles and Tatar informants gathered on the Academy expedition of 1733 to 1743.

Catalogue title Description of the Siberian Tsardom and of all that happened in it from the beginning, especially from its subjection to the Russian power (book 1; 1750, accessed 1787 reissue)

Описание Сибирского царства и всех происшедших в нем дел от начала, а особливо от покорения его Российской державе по сии времена

Gerhard Friedrich Müller

G. F. Müller, a German historian of the St Petersburg Academy, spent ten years in Siberia copying archives and interviewing Tatars, Mongols and others. Book 1 of his Description of the Siberian Tsardom (1750) covers the Siberian Tatar khanate from its legendary origins to Kuchum and Yermak's conquest. He compares Tatar, Mongol and Chinese traditions with Abu al-Ghazi and European travellers, visits the ruins of Tatar towns, and argues openly with legends he thinks unfounded. This corpus copy is the 1787 reissue of the Russian text.

Who “Tartar” means here
Müller uses 'Tatars' for the Turkic Muslims of the Tobol, Irtysh, Ob and Tom (Siberian Tatars), and also for the realm of Chinggis Khan's heirs, whom he treats together with the Mongols. He notes that the Chinese call all lands beyond the Great Wall 'Tartary'.
Why read it
A careful 18th century scholar who visited the ruins of Tatar towns, recorded Tatar oral traditions, and explained where names like Isker, Sibir and 'Tartary' came from.
Watch out
Müller wrote with the Russian state's archives and outlook; his account of the khanate depends partly on late chronicles like the Kungur and Esipov texts. OCR of the 1787 type is poor and quotes are normalized.

CitiesIsker (Kashlyk), near Tobolsk15th to 16th century (written 1740s)

Isker, the real Tatar name of the town of Sibir

Müller says Khan Makhmet moved the princely seat from the Tura to a high headland on the Irtysh and fortified it, because the old capital Chimgi (Tyumen), though protected by ravines, a rampart and ditches, lay on flat ground. The ruins could still be seen above Tobolsk. He rejects the idea that 'Sibir' is a Tatar word for 'capital': the Tatars call the fortress Isker, and the name Kashlyk used in the Tobolsk chronicle was, he says, used by no people he knew.

This place is the so-called old Tatar town and fortress of Sibir, whose remains can still be seen upriver of Tobolsk … The true Tatar name of this old fortress is Isker, by which name the Tatars call it to this day.

BuildingsSouthern Siberia13th to 14th century (written 1740s)

Stone markers, idols and gold from the graves: whose were they?

Müller points to the traces of old towns on the steppes, the many stone markers and 'idols' (stone statues), old grave mounds, and the gold and silver objects dug out of them. He attributes them to the earlier Tatars, reasoning that the treasures of China, Persia, Russia, Poland, Bohemia and Hungary had fallen to them as conquerors. It is a scholar's inference, not a report from the graves themselves.

How many traces of small towns are seen in various places on the steppes? What a multitude of stone markers set up as memorials, idols, old graves and other such things are found everywhere? What precious objects, of gold and silver, have not been dug out of the graves? Where could they have come from, if not from the former Tatars

The nameNorthern frontier of Chinawritten 1740s

Tartary: the Chinese name for everything beyond the Wall

Discussing a Siberian Tatar story that Chinese once lived in Siberia, Müller suggests that at most the Chinese may have built some towns on the Daurian and Mongol frontiers and toward Liaodong, because in China all lands beyond the Wall are called Tartary. The name, in his view, is a broad outsiders' label, not the name of one country.

By all the circumstances one may judge that they perhaps meant certain towns built on the Daurian and Mongol borders and toward Liaotung, of which there are examples in ancient times, because in China all the lands lying beyond the Wall are called by the general name Tartary.

Strange talesTobolsk and the Irtyshrecorded 1670 and in the 1730s

The Tatar tale of Chinese who fled when the forest grew

Müller reports a story still told orally by the Tobolsk Tatars: Siberia was once a bare steppe where the Chinese lived, but when forest began to grow everywhere the people took fright, 'as the land became as if horned', and moved away to their present home. He says it was also written down in 1670 on the orders of the Tobolsk governor Pyotr Godunov. Müller calls it a false tale that no reasonable person can believe, and notes that forest could easily grow over abandoned sites in 450 years without any miracle.

They say that at first all Siberia was a bare steppe without forest, and when at that time the Chinese lived there, forest little by little began to grow in various places, at which the people took fright, because the land became as if horned, and moved to their present dwelling. This tale can still be heard by word of mouth from the Tobolsk Tatars.
