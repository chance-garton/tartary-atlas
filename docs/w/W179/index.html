Bichurin's Description of Dzungaria and Eastern Turkestan

A Russian translation (1829) of Qing Chinese geographies of the region Europeans called 'Chinese Tartary' or 'Little Bukharia'.

Full title Description of Dzungaria and Eastern Turkestan in ancient and present condition (2 parts, 1829)

Описание Чжуньгарии и Восточного Туркистана в древнем и нынешнем состоянии

Bichurin, translator; from Xiyu wenjian lu and Qing gazetteers

Read the 3 passagesOpen the original book

Father Iakinf Bichurin translated Qing gazetteers of the 'Western Region' (Dzungaria and the Tarim Basin oases), written after China conquered the area in 1759, into Russian, with a translator's preface and notes. The book gives the Qing view of the towns, garrisons, taxes, products and peoples of Kashgar, Yarkand, Khotan, Aksu, Turfan and Ili. In his preface Bichurin discusses the name 'Great Tartary' and what 'Tatar' meant to Central Asians he met in Beijing. The corpus also holds his separate History of the Oirats or Kalmyks (1834) under this record. This corpus copy of the Description is the 1829 St Petersburg edition, part 1.

Who “Tartar” means here
In the translated Qing texts the peoples are Dzungars, Turkestanis, Kazakhs and Kyrgyz. In his preface Bichurin reports that Turkestani Muslims used 'Tatars' only for their Turkic kin in Tobolsk, Kazan and Astrakhan, and that no people in Central Asia called itself Tatar.
Why read it
A rare first-hand testimony, gathered in Beijing, on how Central Asians themselves used the word 'Tatar', plus the Qing record of brick-built oasis cities in the heart of so-called Chinese Tartary.
Watch out
The Qing texts were written by the conquerors after 1759 and Bichurin abridged them; his preface remarks are his own. OCR of the 1829 print is patchy.

Kashgar, the 'many-coloured brick' city

The Qing gazetteer describes Kashgar as one of the great cities of Turkestan, so famous that foreigners called all East Turkestanis 'Kashgarians'. It lists its Manchu and Chinese garrison, taxes paid in cash, grain and cotton cloth, silk and gold-thread products, and skilled jade polishers and goldsmiths. A note explains the name as 'many-coloured brick building', because the city had many buildings of coloured brick.

Kash means many-coloured, gar a brick building. There are many buildings of coloured brick here, which is where the name comes from. It is also said that the word Kashgar means rich.

Whom do you call Tatars? A question put to Turkestanis in Beijing

Bichurin notes that Europeans called the belt from the Caspian to the Pacific 'Great Tartary'. While living in Beijing he asked people from Eastern Turkestan whom they called Tatars. Their answer: a people of their own stock living in Tobolsk, Kazan and Astrakhan. The Tatars themselves, he adds, said the name was given them by neighbours, and no people or clan in Central Asia used it of itself.

The nameTobolsk, Kazan, Astrakhan1808 to 1821

'Now we call Tatars our kin in Tobolsk, Kazan and Astrakhan'

The answer Bichurin got from Turkestanis in Beijing, and his conclusion. It shows 'Tatar' as a name used by outsiders for particular Turkic communities under Russian rule, not the name of a vast state.

I asked them whom they call Tatars. Here is their answer: 'Now we call Tatars a people of the same stock as us, living in Tobol, Kazan and Astrakhan.' The Tatars themselves, on the contrary, assert that this name was given them by neighbouring peoples. … but now there is not a single people, nor even a clan, in Central Asia that calls itself by this name.
