Martino Martini's history of the Tartar War in China

A Jesuit missionary's history of the Manchu ('Tartar') conquest of Ming China, much of it witnessed, first printed in Latin and English in 1654.

Catalogue title Bellum Tartaricum, or the Conquest of … China

Martino Martini

Read the 12 passagesOpen the original book

Martino Martini (1614-1661), an Italian Jesuit, worked in China from 1643 to 1651, mostly in Zhejiang, and lived through the fall of the Ming and the Manchu advance. Sent back to Europe in 1651, he published De bello Tartarico (Antwerp, 1654), a short history of the wars from the Ming expulsion of the Mongols in 1368 to 1651, plus an appendix from later letters. It was translated into English the same year. It includes a sketch of Manchu dress and manners, military organisation, and the fight over the head-shaving order. This corpus copy is a scan of the 1654 London English edition (catalogued by the archive as 1939).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Manchus (the 'Eastern' or 'Oriental Tartars' of Niuche, that is the Jurchen), and also the Mongols ('Western Tartars'), whom he treats as one ancient nation north of the Great Wall. He explains that the Chinese call them 'Tata' because they cannot say R.
Why read it
The first widely read European book on the Manchu conquest, by a man who was there. It shows what 'Tartar' meant in 1654 to European readers: not a lost global empire but the new rulers of China, with their shaved heads and queues.
Watch out
Martini saw some episodes himself (he says so) but reports most of the campaigns second hand. He writes as a missionary, praising those who favoured Christians. The OCR prints the long s as f and has marginal notes mixed into the text.

What it says: 12 passages

CitiesLiaoyang1618 to 1621 (as Martini tells it)

Storming a city behind a wooden wall

Martini describes how the Tartars, armed only with sabres and bows against Chinese muskets, took 'Leaotung', which he calls the chief city of the province of Liaodong. The front ranks carried thick boards as a moving 'wooden wall' to soak up the musket fire, ladder-men followed, and cavalry came behind. Before the Chinese could reload, the Tartars were over the walls. He credits their speed and nimbleness, 'in which they excel all Nations'.

the scaling ladders being applied, before they could charge again, they were upon the Walls and enterd the City ; for such is the quickness and nimbleness of the Tartars (in which they excel all Nations, and in which also they place their chief art) that in a trice, they either prevail in their Designs, or retire :

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars under Nurhaci

Keep in mind

An event before Martini reached China, told from report; he runs several Liaodong campaigns together.

CitiesLiaoyangabout 1621

Nurhaci pulls down unlucky walls and builds new ones

After taking 'Leaotung' and then Guangning, Martini says, the Tartar king returned to Liaodong rich with plunder. His soothsayers told him the old walls were unlucky, so he pulled them down, built new ones, and proclaimed himself emperor of China though he held only its edge. A page earlier Martini explains that Chinese soldiers and citizens fled their houses because the Tartars destroyed any city that resisted but only plundered those that surrendered.

And because his Southsayers had perswaded him that the standing of the old Walls were unfortunate, he beat them down, and compassed them about with new, fortifying them with new Munitions, and there proclamed himself Emperour of China:

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars under Nurhaci

Keep in mind

Martini's report; historically Nurhaci moved his capital to Liaoyang in 1621 and to Shenyang in 1625.

CitiesShaoxing1646

Shaoxing, the cleanest city in China, surrenders

Martini, who knew Zhejiang well, pauses as the Tartars cross the Qiantang to praise Shaoxing: not the largest city but the cleanest and most comely in China, circled by fresh water, with wide streets paved in white square stone, a navigable canal down the middle, stone bridges and arches. On the next page he says its houses are built of the same square stone, 'which I observe no where else in China'. It submitted without resistance.

This City in bigness yields to many others, but in cleaness and comeliness it surpasses all: it is so invironed with sweet waters as a man may contemplate its beauty by rounding it in a Boat ; it hath large and fair Streets paved on both sides with white square stones, and in the middle of them all runs a Navigable Chanel,

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars; Chinese of Zhejiang

Keep in mind

A Chinese city, not a Tartar one; it matters here as a city the Tartars took.

CitiesBeijing1649 to 1650

Martini watches the Tartars ride back into Peking

After putting down Jiang Xiang's revolt in Shanxi, the Tartars plundered Datong and burned Puzhou, where the Christians' church was lost. Martini says he saw them return to Peking loaded with riches. Dorgon ('Amavangus') went on to the Western Tartars (Mongols) to ratify the young Shunzhi emperor's marriage and brought back a huge herd of horses.

But yet they Plundered the City Taitung, and burned the City of Pucheu, where the Church of the Christians also perished. From hence the Tartars returned to Peking, where I saw them enter overloaden with Riches and triumphant Laurels ;

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars under Dorgon (Amavangus)

Keep in mind

Martini says he saw the return himself; the campaign in Shanxi he knew by report.

BuildingsChina1640s to 1650s

Beds on the ground, horses in the house, beautiful tents

In a sketch of how the Tartars trained for war, Martini describes their great ring hunts and their camp habits. They sleep on the ground on a horse-cloth and care nothing for houses; if they must live in houses they keep their horses inside and knock holes in the walls. Their tents, he says, are very beautiful and put up and struck so skilfully that they never slow an army's march.

The earth covered with their Horse-cloath is their Bed ; for they care not for Houses, and Chambers ; but if they be forced to dwell in Houses, their Horses must lodge with them, and they must have many holes beaten in the Walls; but yet their Tents are most beautiful, which they fix and remove with such Art and dexterity,

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

Keep in mind

Daily lifeLiaodong1621 (described 1654)

Shaved heads, queues and horse-hoof sleeves

When the Tartars took a city in Liaodong, they proclaimed that no one would be killed who cut his hair and wore Tartar dress. Martini then breaks off to describe their manners: head and beard shaved except the moustache, a long braided tuft at the back, a low round cap trimmed with beaver or sable. The next pages add long robes with sleeves cut like a horse's hoof, a sabre hung point forward, silk or horse-skin boots, broad saddles, and the remark that they are like 'our Tartars of Europe' but not so barbarous.

For the Tartars (that I may say something of their Manners, as my subject gives me occasion) doe shave both the Head and Beards reserving only the Mustachoes, which they extend to a great length, and in the hinder part of their heads they leave a Tuft, which being curiously woven and plated, they let hang down carelesly below their shoulders;

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

Daily lifeZhejiang1645 to 1646

Fighting harder for their hair than for their emperor

The Manchus took the south of Zhejiang easily, Martini says, until they ordered everyone to cut their hair in Tartar fashion. Then soldiers and townspeople alike rose up, fought 'more desperately for their Hair' than for king or kingdom, and drove the Tartars back across the Qiantang river. Elsewhere (p. 108 to 109) he says the Tartars made refusing the haircut high treason, and many Chinese chose death instead.

But when they commanded all by Proclamation to cut off their Hair, then both Souldier and Citizen took up Arms, and fought more desperately for their Hair of their Heads, than they did for King or Kingdom, and beat the Tartars not only out of their City, but repulst them to the River Cienthang

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars and the Chinese of Zhejiang

Daily lifeLiaodong1618 (described 1654)

Servants and horses burned with the dead

When the Chinese ignored his complaints, the Tartar king (Nurhaci) vowed to celebrate his father's funeral with the lives of 200,000 Chinese. Martini explains that when a Tartar of rank died, servants, women and horses, with bows and arrows, were thrown into the funeral fire to serve him in the next life, a custom he says they gave up after conquering China.

For it is the custom of the Tartars when any man of quality dyeth, to cast into that fire which consumes the dead Corps, as many Servants, Women, and Horses, with Bows and Arrows, as may be fit to attend and serve them in the next life: Though now since they conquered China, they have left off this barbarous custome,

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars (Nurhaci's people)

Keep in mind

Reported, not witnessed; Manchu funeral sacrifice (of wives and servants) is attested, but the scale is probably exaggerated.

Daily lifeBeijing1640s to 1650s

The Eight Banners and the ring hunt

Martini gives an early European description of the Eight Banners: white (the imperial banner), red, black and yellow, and four more made by mixing colours. Every soldier knows his colour and where in the city his arms and horse are kept, so the whole army is ready in half an hour at a horn call. On the next pages he adds that they carry no baggage, eat meat half cooked or their own horses and camels, and hunt by encircling a whole mountain.

under these eight colours are comprehended all the Forces of the Kingdom of China, whether they be Natives or Tartars ; the first of which is White, called the Imperial Banner ; the second is Red, the third is Black, the fourth is Yellow;… so as every Souldier knows his own colours, and to what part of the City to repair, where they have ever their Arms and Horses ready

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars (the Eight Banners)

Keep in mind

The real banner colours were yellow, white, red and blue (plain and bordered); Martini's 'black' may be a mistake.

The nameGreat Wall of China1654

Why the Chinese say 'Tata'

Martini defines his subject: the Tartars are simply the people living north of the Great Wall, which for 300 German leagues has served as a rampart against them. He adds that the Chinese, lacking the letter R, have always called them 'Tata', and that the name covers both the Eastern Tartars, unknown in Europe, and the Western ones stretching from Kashgar to the sea above Japan.

I call that Nation Tartars, which inhabiting the Northern parts, behind that famous Wall which stretching out above 300. German Leagues from East to West, hath ever served for a Rampart to hinder their irruptions into the said Empire. This Country the Chineses having a defect of the letter R. antiently called Tata:

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Tartars in general: Eastern (Manchu/Jurchen) and Western (Mongol)

Keep in mind

Chinese 'Dada' (韃靼) is a real name for the Tatars; the 'missing R' explanation is Martini's.

The nameManchuria1368 to about 1550

From Mongol refugees to the kingdom of Niuche

Martini tells how, after the Ming founder drove the Yuan out in 1368, he carried the war into Tartary itself, and the storm fell chiefly on the province of Niuche 'whither the Tartars of China being expelled were retired'. These Tartars then came to Liaodong every year to trade ginseng, furs and horsehair, multiplied into seven 'Hordes', and about 1550 formed the kingdom of Niuche. Like Grosier later, he blends the Yuan Mongols and the Jurchen into one Tartar story.

This Storm of War fell chiefly on the Tartars of the Province of Niuche, whither the Tartars of china being expelled were retired.

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Tartars of Niuche (Jurchen, ancestors of the Manchus)

Keep in mind

The idea that the Yuan refugees became the Jurchen of Niuche is Martini's mistake.

Strange talesBeijing1645

A Ming emperor hanged with a bowstring

Martini says the Hongguang emperor, first ruler of the Southern Ming at Nanjing, was betrayed to the Tartars after less than a year on the throne, sent to Peking, and publicly hanged on the town walls with a bowstring, 'which kind of death the Tartars esteem most noble'.

This unfortunate Prince being thus betrayed before he had reigned full one year, was sent to Peking, and there upon the Town Walls was hanged publickly in a Bow string, which kind of death the Tartars esteem most noble.

Martino Martini (English translation, London 1654)

About Manchu Tartars and the Ming claimant Hongguang

Keep in mind

Reported from a distance; other sources give different details of his death, which came in 1646.
