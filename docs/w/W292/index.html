Ledebour's botanical journey through the Altai and the Kazakh steppe, 1826

Three Dorpat botanists describe Altai 'Kalmyk' camps, Kazakh (Kirghiz) life, Tatar traders and the ruined Zunghar temple of Ablaikit in 1826.

Catalogue title Journey through the Altai Mountains and the Zungharian Kirghiz Steppe, undertaken in 1826 at the expense of the Imperial University of Dorpat, 2 parts with plates and maps (Berlin 1829-1830)

Carl Friedrich von Ledebour, with C. A. Meyer and A. von Bunge

Carl Friedrich von Ledebour, a botany professor at Dorpat (Tartu), led a plant-collecting expedition to the Russian Altai in 1826 with Carl Anton Meyer and Alexander von Bunge. Part 1 (Berlin 1829) is Ledebour's own diary of the Altai mountains and the mining town of Barnaul; part 2 (1830) prints Bunge's diary of the eastern Altai and the Chuya steppe and Meyer's diary of a ride through the Kazakh steppe to Lake Zaysan and the Chinese frontier, ending with a long sketch of the Kazakhs of the Middle Horde. All three travelled and saw most of what they describe. This corpus copy is the Bavarian State Library scan of both volumes.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The authors rarely use 'Tartary'. 'Tataren' are Muslim Turkic townspeople and traders (Siberian and Kazan Tatars) in the Irtysh forts; the nomads are called Kirghiz (in fact Kazakhs) and Kalmyks (here the Turkic-speaking Altaians of the mountains), and Meyer argues the Kirghiz are of Tatar rather than Mongol origin.
Why read it
A sober 1820s eyewitness picture of the steppe frontier: felt yurts, burial customs, wooden mosques, and above all what happened to the one real brick monument in the region, the Zunghar temple of Ablaikit, which the local Kazakhs were quarrying for tombs.
Watch out
Three different diarists: check whether a passage is Ledebour (part 1), Bunge or Meyer (part 2). Much is botany; some ethnographic remarks are hearsay from Cossacks and officials, and the tone towards Kalmyks and Kazakhs is often condescending.

CitiesSemey (Semipalatinsk), Kazakhstan1826

A wooden frontier town with Tatar minarets

Meyer describes the town beside the Semipalatinsk fort: all its houses, its four mosques and even its trading hall were of wood, with Kazakh yurts on the edges. The Tatars' dress, veiled women and minarets with the call to prayer gave it an 'oriental' look. A good picture of what a 'Tatar' town on the Irtysh really looked like in the 1820s.

It is fairly large, has only wooden houses, most of them small and plain, 4 wooden mosques, a trading hall likewise of wood, the very dilapidated customs buildings and, especially towards the north, many Kirghiz yurts. … The foreign dress of the many Tatars, the veiled women, the slender minarets from which the monotonous call to prayer so often rings out, give the town an oriental air.

BuildingsAblaikit ruins, near Ust-Kamenogorsk, East Kazakhstan1826 (temple built 1654)

A Zunghar brick temple quarried for Kazakh tombs

Meyer reached the ruins of the 'Ablakit palaces' about 70 versts from Ust-Kamenogorsk. He credits the building to the Zunghar prince Ablai in 1654 (following Pallas) and reports that the local Kazakhs had carried off its large fired bricks to build tombs for their sultans, leaving only foundations and the outer wall. On the same page he notes that the builders carried the enclosing wall up to the highest crags with piled granite blocks.

A description of this soongorian [Zunghar] temple, now completely destroyed, which was built in 1654 by the soongorian prince Ablai, can be found in Pallas's Travels … The Kirghiz have broken away the fine, large, solid bricks to build tombs for their dead sultans and other rich people, so that now only the foundations and the wall enclosing the whole site still stand.

Original wording

Eine Beschreibung dieses, jetzt ganz zerstörten soongorischen Tempels, der im Jahre 1654 vom soongorischen Fürsten Ablai erbaut worden ist, findet man in Pallas Reisen … Die Kirgisen haben die schönen großen festen Backsteine weggebrochen, um ihren verstorbenen Sultanen und andere Reichen Grabmäler zu bauen, so daß jetzt nur noch die Fundamente und die den ganzen Platz einschließende Mauer steht.

BuildingsAblaikit ruins, near Ust-Kamenogorsk, East Kazakhstan1826 (dig about 1800)

Brick kilns and a two-month treasure dig at Ablaikit

Continuing at the ruins, Meyer says some of the surrounding graves look like the so-called Chud graves but many were made by the Zunghars. He records that Russian soldiers had dug there for two months around 1800, and that traces of kilns where the temple's bricks were probably fired could still be seen nearby: a plain clue that the building material was made on the spot.

About 25 years ago searches were carried out here with 100 soldiers for almost 2 months, and many graves were opened. … About 5 versts to the south-west traces of kilns are said to be still visible, where the bricks used for the buildings were probably fired.

Original wording

BuildingsSemey (Semipalatinsk), Kazakhstan1826

The vanished 'seven palaces' of Semipalatinsk

Semipalatinsk means 'seven-palace town' in Russian. Meyer notes that by 1826 the old 'palaty' that gave the place its name were almost gone: barely visible foundations of three buildings beside the new fort, and faint traces of the other four about 14 versts west. He does not say who built them.

This new fortress is laid out on the high, very steep right bank of the Irtysh. Only a few paces from it lie the barely recognisable foundations of 3 old buildings, and about 14 versts further west are the likewise faint traces of the 4 other 'palaces'.

BuildingsCharysh valley, Altai MountainsJune 1826

Inside a felt yurt in the Altai

Ledebour's guides took him into a 'Kalmyk' yurt: leaning poles covered with felt, a felt flap for a door and a small fire burning straight on the ground in the middle. On the same page he describes the huge unwashed leather bag in which all spare milk was fermented into kumiss, and the still used to turn it into milk brandy (araku).

The simple construction of these yurts is well known: several poles leaning against one another and covered with felt blankets make a dwelling that has to give shelter and protection even against winter frost and autumn storms in these harsh mountain regions. When I entered the yurt through the door-like opening, which is hung only with a felt blanket, a small fire was glowing in the middle of the yurt, simply kindled on the ground.

Working translation

Original wording

Die einfache Construction dieser Jurten ist bekannt, wo mehrere gegeneinander geneigte Stangen von Filzdecken überzogen, eine Wohnung bilden, welche selbst gegen Winterfrost und Herbststürme in diesen rauhen Gebirgsgegenden Obdach und Schutz geben müssen. Als ich in die Jurte durch die Thürähnliche Oeffnung trat, die nur mit einer Filzdecke verhängt wird, glimmte ein kleines Feuer in der Mitte der Jurte, ohne Weiteres auf dem Boden angeschürt.

BuildingsCharysh river, Altai MountainsJuly 1826

Opening the 'Chud graves'

Russians in Siberia credited old stone-covered burials to a vanished people they called the Chud. Ledebour describes them as low oval cairns, some edged with upright slate slabs, and had one dug open. It held two skeletons at different depths, and his own workmen insisted these were Kalmyk and not Chud bodies: a nice example of how uncertain the attribution of such monuments was on the spot.

…they can be recognised from outside by heaps of stones of elliptical shape, whose greatest diameter is 2 fathoms, whose smaller diameter is one and a half fathoms and whose height is 2 to 2.5 feet. … The workmen assured me, however, that these must be Kalmyk and not Chud corpses,

BuildingsKurchum mountains, East KazakhstanMay 1826

Felt yurts in winter: children kept in warm ash

Meyer declines to describe Kazakh dwellings in detail because they are the same as the Kalmyk yurts Pallas had already described. Pleasant in summer, they were bitterly cold in winter: he could hardly bear a May night in one, and says parents sometimes buried small children in pits of warm ash to stop them freezing. Smoke from dung fires was a constant nuisance.

I consider it superfluous to describe the dwellings of the Kirghiz, since they differ in nothing from the Kalmyk yurts that Pallas has described so well. … The children have it worst, and to protect them from freezing the parents are often forced to bury them in pits filled with warm ash.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Wohnungen der Kirgisen zu beschreiben, halte ich für überflüssig, da sie sich in Nichts von kalmückischen Jurten unterscheiden, die Pallas so gut beschrieben hat. … Am schlimmsten haben es die Kinder, und um diese gegen das Erfrieren zu schützen, sind die Aeltern oft genöthigt, sie in Gruben, die mit warmer Asche angefüllt sind, zu vergraben.

BuildingsBarnaul, Altai Krai1826

Felt, straw mats and a birch-bark Tatar yurt

In Barnaul the mining chief Frolov had two model yurts set up for Ledebour. The rich Kazakh one had a collapsible lattice wall lined with clean straw mats in fine weather and wrapped in felt in bad weather, with a hanging felt flap as the door. The second was made of birch bark, of the kind used by the Tatars around Krasnoyarsk, a reminder that 'Tatar' dwellings in Siberia were not all felt.

In good weather the inner side of the lattice is hung with a row of clean straw mats; in bad weather, on the other hand, felt blankets are fastened on the outside, and a hanging piece of such a blanket closes the entrance as a door. The second yurt was made of birch bark, as the Tatars in the region of the town of Krasnoyarsk usually have them.

Working translation

Original wording

Bei gutem Wetter behängt man die innere Seite des Gitterwerkes mit einer Reihe sauberer Strohmatten; bei schlechtem Wetter hingegen befestigt man auf der äußeren Seite Filzdecken, und ein herabhängendes Stück einer solchen Decke verschließt den Eingang als Thüre. Die zweite Jurte war aus Birkenrinde gemacht, wie sie die Tataren in der Gegend der Stadt Krasnojarsk zu haben pflegen.

Daily lifeCharysh valley, Altai MountainsJune 1826

A wooden idol with glass eyes facing the door

In the same yurt Ledebour saw the household idol hanging opposite the entrance, a roughly carved piece of wood with a head and eyes of glass or coral, surrounded by offerings of squirrel and ground-squirrel skins and eagle claws. The woman of the house served milk from the kumiss bag to his Kalmyk guides but boiled milk to his Russians.

Opposite the entrance hangs an idol, carved as crudely as possible from a piece of wood, at whose upper end a head has usually been attempted, with inset eyes of glass or coral. Beside it hang various offerings, such as a squirrel or a ground-squirrel skin, and especially often an eagle's claw.

Daily lifeChuya steppe, Altai MountainsMay 1826

The dead hung in trees for birds and wolves

Bunge asked his Kalmyk neighbours how they buried their dead, since the stone mounds nearby (the 'Chud graves') were far older. He was told that burial depended on the dying person's wish and the shaman (kam): a few were burned or buried, but most were wrapped in felt or a horse hide and hung in a tree in the forest, with a horse as the usual offering.

The dead are either burned, though rarely, or scraped into the earth, or finally, which is said to be the commonest case, they are wrapped in a felt or a horse hide, hung on a tree in a remote part of a forest, and left to the birds and wolves. Usually this is accompanied by some ceremonies, and a horse is the usual funeral offering.

Working translation

Original wording

Die Todten werden entweder, jedoch selten, verbrannt, oder sie werden in die Erde verscharrt, oder endlich, was der häufigste Fall seyn soll, sie werden in einen Filz, oder ein Pferdefell eingewickelt, an einer abgelegenen Stelle eines Waldes an einen Baum aufgehängt, und den Vögeln und Wölfen preis gegeben. Gewöhnlich ist dies mit einigen Ceremonien verbunden, und ein Pferd ist das gewöhnliche Todtenopfer.

Daily lifeKazakh steppe (Karkaraly district)1826

Buried beside ruins they hold sacred

Meyer reports that the Kazakhs like to bury their dead near other graves or near ruins of old buildings they regard as holy, and that rich sultans were carried all the way to holy places near Tashkent. In winter the body was sewn into cloth and leather and kept in a separate yurt until it could be moved. This helps explain why the Ablaikit bricks ended up in Kazakh tombs.

They like to bury their dead near other graves or ruins of old buildings which they hold to be holy. Above all (at least for the Kirghiz around Karkaraly) several places near Tashkent are holy to them, and the corpses of rich sultans are often carried there.

Working translation

Original wording

Ihre Todten beerdigen sie gerne in der Nähe anderer Gräber oder Ruinen alter von ihnen für heilig gehaltenen Gebäude. Vor allen sind ihnen (wenigstens den Kirgisen der Umgebungen von Kar-Karaly) mehrere Orte in der Nähe von Taschkent heilig, und die Leichen reicher Sultane werden oft dorthin abgeführt.

Daily lifeKazakh steppe1826

Women strike the yurts and saddle the horses

Meyer lists the division of labour in a Kazakh camp. Women took down and put up the yurts, gathered fuel, milked, cooked, wove and sewed, and even saddled their husbands' horses. Men watched the herds, carved wooden goods (including the lattice frames of the yurts, as the same page says), worked as smiths and went raiding. Grain was grown only by slaves.

Most of the work is done by the women. They must take down and put up the yurts again, gather wood and fuel, milk the cattle, prepare food and drink, weave cloth, sew clothes and boots; indeed they must also saddle the horses and lead them to their husbands. The men, by contrast, busy themselves almost only with guarding the herds, carving wooden goods, smithing and so on, and with robbery.

Working translation

Original wording

Die meisten Arbeiten werden von den Weibern verrichtet. Sie müssen die Jurten abbrechen und wieder aufbauen, Holz und Feuermaterial zusammensuchen, das Vieh melken, die Speisen und Getränke zubereiten, Zeuge weben, Kleider und Stiefeln nähen; ja sie müssen auch die Pferde satteln und dem Manne vorführen. Die Männer dagegen beschäftigen sich fast nur mit Bewachung der Heerden, mit Schnitzen der Holzwaaren, mit Schmieden u. s. w. und mit Räubereien.

The nameKazakh steppe1826

Are the Kirghiz Tatars or Mongols?

Meyer thinks a Tatar origin for the 'Kirghiz' (the Kazakhs) far likelier than a Mongol one, judging by faces and language. He then gives the three hordes and adds that the Great Horde is often called the 'golden horde', a striking reuse of the old name. A few lines on he notes that the Russians call some of its tribes the wild, stone or black Kirghiz.

Yet the assumption of a Tatar origin is much more probable, and it is better supported by their facial features and language than the opinion that they descend from the Mongols. The Kirghiz are divided into three main hordes, the great, middle and little horde. The great horde is also often called the golden horde

Working translation

Original wording

Doch ist die Annahme eines tatarischen Ursprungs wohl viel wahrscheinlicher, und sie wird durch die Gesichtszüge und Sprache besser unterstützt, als die Meinung, daß sie von den Mongolen herstammen. Man theilt die Kirgisen in drei Haupthorden, die große, mittlere und kleine Horde. Die große Horde wird auch oft die goldne Horde genannt

A stone sphinx from a Siberian grave

In the new mining museum at Barnaul, Ledebour was struck by a crudely carved stone 'sphinx' on a squared block, said to have been found in a Chud grave. He found it remarkable to meet 'this form of the highest antiquity' in Siberia. He reports the find-spot as he was told it and gives no further detail.

In the anteroom stood a sphinx hewn from stone, resting on a squared, dressed block, almost 4 feet long … most interesting to me, since this monument had been found in a Chud grave. The workmanship was indeed crude
