Hayton's Flower of the Histories of the East

An Armenian prince who knew the Mongol court dictates, in 1307, a geography of Asia and a history of the Tartar khans from Genghis to his own day.

Full title The Flower of the Histories of the East (French original dictated 1307 and Latin version), in Recueil of Crusade Historians, Armenian Documents vol. II

Hayton of Corycus (Het'um), Armenian prince and Premonstratensian; Charles Kohler, editor

Read the 13 passagesOpen the original book

Hayton (Het'um) of Corycus, a prince of Cilician Armenia and nephew of King Het'um I, served alongside the Mongol Ilkhans, became a Premonstratensian canon, and in 1307 at Poitiers dictated this book in French to Nicolas Falcon, who also made the Latin version. Book I surveys the kingdoms of Asia, Book III tells the history of the Tartars, and Book IV proposes a crusade allied with them. Hayton himself says he knew Book III in three ways: from the Tartars' own histories up to Mongke, from his uncle King Het'um's accounts through Hulegu, and from his own eyes from Abaqa onward. The corpus copy is Charles Kohler's edition of both texts with variants, in Recueil des historiens des croisades, Documents armeniens II (Paris, 1906), in four scans.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongols as a whole: the 'seven nations called Mogols', of which the Tartars proper are the first, and the realms of Genghis Khan's heirs (the Great Khan in Cathay, Chagatai lands in Turkestan, the Golden Horde in Comania, the Ilkhans in Persia). The Latin text usually spells them Tatari.
Why read it
The most influential medieval European overview of 'Tartary' as a system of kingdoms, from an Armenian insider who attended khan elections, with famous stories (the black felt, the owl, the drowned khan) that later books copied for centuries.
Watch out
Hayton wrote to win papal support for an Armenian-Mongol alliance, so he flatters the Tartars' friendship with Christians (he calls Qubilai a Christian) and garbles early Mongol history (his 'Octota Can' is made Genghis's heir and Jochi's father). The OCR mixes the main text with dense variant footnotes.

CitiesBeijing1260s to 1294

Qubilai's new city 'Jong', bigger than Rome

Editorial contextAfter Mongke's death the Tartars made his brother Qubilai lord. Hayton says Qubilai founded a city called Jong, 'bigger than Rome', and lived there to the end of his life. The editor identifies Jong with Khanbaliq (Marco Polo's Cambaluc), today's Beijing, the Mongol capital that Qubilai built beside the ruined Jurchen city of Zhongdu.

they made his brother Cobila Can lord, who held the lordship of the Tartars for forty-two years, and was a Christian, and built a city called Jong, which is bigger than Rome. And in that city Cobila Can lived

CitiesBaghdad1258

Baghdad taken, the caliph and his treasure

Editorial contextHayton's account of the Mongol sack of Baghdad: Hulegu took the city by force, put men and women to the sword, and found such riches that it was a marvel to see. Asked why he had not spent his treasure on an army, the caliph answered that he thought old women alone were enough to defend his land. Hulegu then shut him in a room with his treasure to eat it, and 'never again was there a caliph in Baghdad'.

The caliph was brought alive before Hulegu, and so much wealth was found in the city of Baghdad that it was a great marvel to behold. … And the caliph answered that he thought old women alone were enough to defend the land.

About Hulegu's Tartars; the Abbasid caliph

Keep in mind

Hayton says his knowledge of Hulegu's reign came from his uncle King Het'um; the starving-among-treasure ending is a widely told story, not a verified fact.

CitiesSarai (lower Volga)c. 1300

Sarra, capital of Comania, 'almost all laid waste'

Editorial contextIn his geography of Comania (the Kipchak steppe, the Golden Horde's land) Hayton names its chief city Sarra, 'once a very good city', which the Tartars have almost entirely ruined. The editor identifies Sarra with Sarai on the Volga, and later in the book Hayton says the Tartar king Toqta holds his seat in a city called 'Sera'.

The chief city of the kingdom of Comania is called Sarra, which was once a very good city, but the Tartars have laid it almost entirely waste.

About Tartars (Golden Horde)

Keep in mind

Sarai is usually credited to the Golden Horde itself (founded under Batu in the 1240s to 1250s), so Hayton's 'once good, now wasted' is puzzling; he may mean another Kipchak town or be confused.

Conquerors who would rather live in tents and carts than towns

Editorial contextHayton's summary of the Tartar character includes a line that answers the question of Tartar towns directly: they know how to conquer foreign lands but not how to keep them, because they prefer to live in tents and wagons rather than in towns. The Latin version says the same, that they 'do not know how to live in castles and cities'.

The Tartars know well how to conquer foreign lands, but they do not know how to keep them, for they would rather be in tents and in carts than live in towns.

Raised on black felt: a khan election Hayton saw twice

Editorial contextHayton says the Tartars still make their lord the way they made Genghis: the whole people gathers in a field, the chosen man is seated on a black felt, the nobles and Genghis's kin lift him high and set him on a rich throne, and all kneel to him. He adds that he himself was present at two such elections, and marvels that for all their conquests they never changed their first custom.

When they want to elect their lord (and I have been twice at the election of the emperor of the Tartars, and saw how all the Tartars gathered in a great field), they seated the one who was to be their lord on a black felt, and set a rich throne in their midst.

Daily lifeUighur lands (Hayton's 'Tarse')early 13th century

Tartars learned letters, and idols, from the Uighurs

Editorial contextHayton says that before their expansion the Tartars had no letters at all, which is why he cannot give dates for their early history. When their armies reached the kingdom of 'Tarse' (the Uighurs), they learned writing there, and because the people were idolaters they also began to worship idols, while always confessing one immortal God above the others.

In those parts the Tartars learned letters, for before they had no letters at all. And because the inhabitants of those lands were all idolaters, the Tartars began to worship idols; but they always confessed the immortal God, greater than the others.

Daily lifeMongol landsc. 1300

Killing a man is no sin, but leaving the bridle on is

Editorial contextIn his chapter on Tartar customs Hayton says they name God simply and immortal but honour him with no prayers, fasts or penances. A Tartar would not think it a sin to kill a man, yet would think he had sinned mortally if he left the bit in his horse's mouth while it grazed. When they cross lands short of food they drive cows and mares with them, living on the milk and eating horse meat, which they think good.

A Tartar would not reckon it a sin to kill a man; but if he left the bridle in his horse's mouth when it should graze, he would think he had sinned mortally. … and they live on the milk, and eat the flesh of horses, and it seems good meat to them.

Original wording

Le Tartar ne reputaroit avoir fait pechié d'oucivre I home; e se il laissoit le frein en la bouche de son cheval, quant il deüst paistre, il quideroit pechier mortelment. … e vivent du lait, e la char des chevaus mangent, e lur pert bone char.

Daily lifeMongol landsc. 1300

Shooting backwards while they flee

Editorial contextHayton, who fought beside Mongol armies, describes their tactics: when they flee they do so in ordered squadrons, and pursuing them is dangerous because they shoot backwards as they ride, wounding men and horses, then wheel round on careless pursuers. Their army looks smaller than it is because they ride packed close, so a thousand Tartars look like five hundred.

When the Tatars turn to flight, they all flee together in ordered squadrons and lines; and it is very dangerous to follow them, because as they flee they shoot arrows backwards and wound and kill horses and men. … The Tatar army does not look large, because they all ride packed together, so that a line of a thousand Tatars does not look like five hundred.

Working translation

Original wording

Quando Tatari vertunt in fugam, fugiunt omnes simul per turmas et acies ordinatas; et valde periculosum est sequi eos, quoniam retrograde sagictas jaciunt fugiendo, et equos et homines vulnerant et occidunt. … Exercitus Tatarorum non est magne apparencie, quia omnes simul congregatim incedunt, ita quod acies mille Tatarorum quingentorum apparenciam non habet.

Hayton of Corycus, Flos historiarum terre Orientis (Latin version by Nicolas Falcon, 1307), ed. Ch. Kohler (1906)

The nameMongoliabefore 1206

The Tartars were one of seven Mongol nations

Editorial contextHayton says the Tartars first lived beyond the great mountain of Belgian, 'like beasts' without faith or law, serving other nations, until several tribes 'called Mogols' banded together. He then lists seven nations, and the first is named Tartars. So for Hayton 'Tartar' is both the name of one tribe and the name for the whole confederation, a distinction that matters for anyone asking where the word came from.

The first of these seven nations is named Tartars, the second Tangot, the third Eurach, the fourth Jalair, the fifth Sonit, the sixth Mengli, the seventh Tebet.

Working translation

Original wording

La primiere de [ces] VII nacions est nomée Tartars, la secunde Tangot, la tierce Eurach, la quarte Jalair, la quinte Sonit, la sime Mengli, la setime Tebet.

Hayton of Corycus, La Flor des estoires de la terre d'Orient (French text, 1307), ed. Ch. Kohler (1906)

About Tartars and the other Mongol tribes

Keep in mind

The editor notes that 'Mengli' should read Merkit; the list mixes real tribes (Tatar, Jalayir, Sunit, Merkit) with Tangut and Tibet.

The nameBeijing; Turkestan; Sarai; Tabriz1307

One great khan in Cathay and three Tartar kings

Editorial contextHayton closes his history with the Tartar world as it stood when he wrote: a great emperor, Temur Khan, sixth of the line, ruling from the city of Jong in Cathay, and three other Tartar kings, Chapar in Turkestan, Toqta in Comania and Kharbanda (Oljeitu) in Tabriz, who all owe him reverence and take their quarrels to his court. It is a clear picture of 'Tartary' as a family of Mongol states, not a single city or civilisation.

The great emperor of the Tartars, the one who now holds the lordship, is named Temur Khan, and he is the sixth emperor. He holds the seat of his empire in the kingdom of Cathay, in a very great city called Jong … There are also three other kings of the Tartars, who have very great power, and all pay reverence and obedience to the great emperor.

Original wording

Le grant empereor des Tartars, celui qui ores tient la seignorie, est només Tamor Can, e est le VI empereor. A poor old blacksmith and the white knight

Editorial contextHayton's origin legend of Genghis Khan: a poor old blacksmith named Canguis saw in a dream a white knight on a white horse, who told him that God willed him to rule the seven Tartar nations called Mogols and free them from servitude. The chiefs laughed at him until they saw the same knight the next night. A second vision later sends him west over the mountain.

an old man, a poor smith, named Canguis, saw a vision in a dream: he saw an armed knight on a white horse who called him by his name and said: 'Canguis, the will of the immortal God is that you should be governor and lord over the seven nations of the Tartars, which are called Mogols'

About Genghis Khan and the Mongols

Keep in mind

A legend; the 'smith' idea echoes Genghis's personal name Temujin, which was linked with 'smith' in later tellings. Hayton says this part comes from the Tartars' own histories.

Strange talesMongoliac. 1200

The owl that saved Genghis Khan

Editorial contextUnhorsed and alone after a lost skirmish, Genghis hid in a bush. As his enemies came to search it, an owl (the 'duc', eagle owl) landed on the bush, and they left, reasoning that no bird would sit where a man was hiding. Hayton says this is why every Tartar who can get an owl feather wears it on his head. Compare Riccoldo (W228), who tells a quite different owl story to explain the same feathers.

and at that a bird called the duc (eagle owl) came and sat on the bush … The Tartars gave thanks to God, and from then on held that bird, called the duc, in such reverence that everyone who can get a feather of it wears it on his head.

Working translation

Original wording

et sur ce un oisel, qui avoit non duc, vint e s'asist sur ce boisson … Les Tartars rendirent graces à Deu, e dès adonques orent en tante reverence cel oisel, qui est apellez duc, que chascun qui poet avoir de la plume de cel oisel la porte sur sa teste.

Hayton of Corycus, La Flor des estoires de la terre d'Orient (French text, 1307), ed. Ch. Kohler (1906)

The Great Khan sunk by enemy swimmers

Editorial contextHayton's account of the death of the Great Khan Mongke: besieging an island in the sea of Cathay, he was sunk when the clever local people sent swimmers under his ship to bore holes in its hull until it filled and he drowned. The fine print

How we know

mixed Part firsthand observation, part drawn from other sources.

mixed: firsthand observation of Ilkhanid Persia and Cilician Armenia, informant testimony from the Armenian court, and retrospective history of the Mongol khans from Genghis to Ghazan

Research notes
  • Two textual states (French dictation and Latin translation, both 1307); Hayton is partisan for the Armenian-Mongol alliance and for a new crusade; his khan chronology contains errors. Same volume contains Dardel, Pseudo-Brocardus, Guillaume Adam, Daniel of Tabriz.
  • Latin first print Haguenau 1529 verified via MDZ manifest (bsb10159908); other MDZ copies bsb11744303, bsb11228078. French Paris 1517 not verified.
  • Further copies: api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/presentation/v… (Latin first print: Liber historiarum partium Orientis sive Passagium terrae sanctae, Haguenau 1529, MDZ, 152 canvases, manifest retrieved); MDZ bsb10358896 (Haithoni Armeni Historia orientalis quae eadem et De Tartaris inscribitur, Cologne 1671)

Why it matters

The first systematic Western geography of Asia organised by kingdom (Cathay, Tarse, Turkestan, Khwarazm, Cumania) and a history of the Mongol khans by an Armenian insider; a key source for the medieval European picture of 'Tartary' before Marco Polo circulated widely.
