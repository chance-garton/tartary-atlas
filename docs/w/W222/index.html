Four Ottoman state papers on the end of the Crimean khanate

An Ottoman copy (c.1775 to 1780) of memoirs and a treaty text about Russia, Crimea and the Tatar khans in the 1770s.

Catalogue title Four Ottoman documents on Crimean affairs, 1763-1780: a memoir on Russia since 1763 (Beyan-name), a memoir on Russian exactions (1773), the Russo-Crimean treaty text on frontier changes (Ahd-name, Turkish version by the reis ul-kuttab), and a memoir on the Tatars and their khans

بیان نامه؛ عهدنامه (مجموعه چهار قطعه در باب امور قریم)

Anonymous Ottoman chancery copyist; Abd al-Razzaq Efendi

This small manuscript in the BULAC library, Paris, gathers four Ottoman chancery pieces from the Russo-Turkish war of 1768 to 1774 and its aftermath: a memoir on Russia since 1763, a memoir of 1773 on Russian demands (apparently translated from French), a treaty text on the Crimean frontier, and a memoir on the Tatars and their khans, the last two put into Turkish by the reis ul-kuttab Abd al-Razzaq Efendi. The copy is anonymous and undated, perhaps by a European hand. The corpus text is the library's scan on Internet Archive, whose OCR is unusable, so passages were read from the images. A diary of Pierre Ruffin, French interpreter at Bakhchysarai in 1767, is also filed under this record but was not read.

How they knew
Official record
Who “Tartar” means here
The Crimean Tatars and the Nogay hordes (Budjak, Kuban, Yedisan, Jamboyluk, Yedichkul): Muslim subjects of the Genghisid Giray khans, whose status was being fought over by Russia and the Ottomans.
Why read it
It shows 'Tartary' as it looked to Ottoman officials in its last years: a Muslim khanate with Genghisid rulers, real fortresses, and a religious tie to the sultan as caliph.
Watch out
Some pieces are translations of Russian or French texts, so the Ottoman voice is partly mediated. They are diplomatic documents with a political aim, and the transcriptions from a handwritten copy may contain small errors.

What it says: 3 passages

CitiesKerch Strait (Kerch, Yenikale, Taman)1773

Kerch and Yenikale for Russia, a fortress on a non-existent island for the Ottomans

The memoir lists Russia's peace terms as the Ottomans understood them: the fortresses and places on the Crimean, Taman and Kuban side would be handed to the Tatars, while Yenikale and the port of Kerch, with their districts, would go to Russia. At most, it says with open sarcasm, the Ottomans would be allowed to build a fortress 'on an imaginary island, whose name and body do not exist' between Yenikale and Taman. The Tatar towns of the strait are real fortresses bargained over by two empires.

All the fortresses and places on the side of Crimea, Taman and Kuban … shall be left and handed over to the Tatars; and secondly Yenikale and the port of Kerch, with their districts and territories, shall be made over to Russia; and at most the Sublime State may build a fortress on an imaginary island, whose name and body do not exist, between Yenikale and Taman.

Daily lifeCrimea1770s

Muslim Tatars and the sultan as caliph

Although the Tatars were declared independent of the Ottomans, the memoir keeps a religious tie. Because 'the Tatar nation is of the religion of Islam', and two caliphs cannot coexist under Islamic law, Russia agrees that the Tatars acknowledge the Ottoman sultan, 'caliph and head of all Muslim rulers and protector of the Two Holy Cities', as holding supreme spiritual authority over them. It shows the Crimean Tatars as a Muslim people whose faith linked them to Istanbul.

But since the Tatar nation is of the religion of Islam … the Russian state consents that the Tatars accept and acknowledge that supreme spiritual authority is vested in the noble person of His Majesty the compassionate and mighty Padishah of the House of Osman, as caliph and head of all Muslim rulers and protector of the Two Holy Cities.

The nameCrimea1770s

Only a descendant of Genghis may be khan

The memoir sets out what happens when a Crimean khan dies or the Tatar peoples agree to depose him. Because it is 'among impossible things' for anyone outside the line of Genghis to hold the rank of khan, they must elect and install another khan from Genghis's descendants, and then notify both Russia and the Ottoman sultan. Five centuries after the Mongol conquest, the Crimean Tatars' rulers still drew their legitimacy from Genghis Khan.

On the khan's death, or if the Tatar peoples by common agreement wish to depose him, since it is among impossible things for anyone other than the line of Genghis to attain this rank of khan, when they elect and install another khan from the descendants of Genghis
