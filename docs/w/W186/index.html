Zadonshchina: the Russian poem of the battle of Kulikovo

A 14th to 15th century Russian prose-poem celebrating Grand Prince Dmitry's victory over the Horde leader Mamai on Kulikovo field in 1380.

Catalogue title Zadonshchina (poetic tale of the battle of the Don, Kulikovo 1380), first publication 1858 from the Kirillo-Belozersk copy; with the 1959 Academy edition Povesti o Kulikovskoi bitve (Zadonshchina, chronicle tale, Skazanie o Mamaevom poboishche)

Задонщина (Слово о великом князе Дмитрии Ивановиче и о брате его князе Владимире Андреевиче)

Anonymous (attributed to Sofonii of Ryazan); ed. I. I. Sreznevsky 1858

The Zadonshchina ('the tale beyond the Don') is an Old Russian poetic lament and victory song about the battle of Kulikovo (8 September 1380), traditionally attributed to Sofony of Ryazan and closely modelled on the Lay of Igor's Campaign. This corpus copy is I. I. Sreznevsky's 1858 Academy edition, which combines the 15th century Kirillo-Belozersk manuscript with the later Undolsky copy, adds variant passages from the prose tales of the 'Mamai battle', and a long commentary. Sreznevsky argues the poem circulated orally and was written down from memory. The 1959 Academy edition listed on the card is not stored in this corpus.

How they knew
From informants
Who “Tartar” means here
The Tatars are the army of the Golden Horde under the emir Mamai, called 'pagan Tatars', 'Muslims' (busormane) and, in poetic language, 'Khinova'. Batu, the conqueror of 1237 to 1240, is remembered as the model of Horde power.
Why read it
The Russian literary memory of the Horde at the moment its hold began to break: Tatars as grey wolves, the loot of their camp, Mamai fleeing to Genoese Kaffa, and (in Sreznevsky's notes) the chronicle scene of Batu's siege of Kiev.
Watch out
This is poetry, not a battle report: numbers and speeches are rhetorical and hostile to the 'pagans'. Some quotes come from Sreznevsky's editorial matter and from later prose tales he prints alongside, and are labelled as such.

CitiesKyiv1240

Batu at Kiev: creaking carts, bellowing camels, walls battered day and night

In his introduction Sreznevsky quotes the Hypatian (Galician-Volhynian) Chronicle's famous account of Batu's siege of Kiev to show the old Russian epic style. The Tatar host was so large that nothing could be heard in the city for the creaking of carts, the bellowing of camels and the neighing of horses. Batu set up siege engines by the Polish Gate and battered the walls day and night until they fell. It shows the Horde as a besieger of cities, with engineers and machines.

Batu came to Kiev in heavy force... And Batu was at the city, and his warriors besieged the city, and nothing could be heard for the creaking of his carts, the bellowing of his many camels and the neighing of his herds of horses... Batu set up siege engines against the city by the Polish Gate … The engines battering without cease day and night broke down the walls.

CitiesFeodosia (Kaffa), Crimea1380

Mamai flees to Kaffa, and the Genoese mock him with Batu's memory

In the poem Mamai escapes from his army 'howling like a grey wolf' and runs to the city of Kaffa, the Genoese colony in Crimea. The Fryazi (Italians) jeer that he is no Batu: Batu had 400,000 armoured men and warred over the whole Russian land, while Mamai came with nine hordes and seventy princes and now flees with eight companions. Historically Mamai did flee to the Crimea and was killed near Kaffa.

You will never be like Tsar Batu: Tsar Batu had four hundred thousand armoured men, and he made war on the whole Russian land from east to west … And you came to the Russian land, Tsar Mamai, with great forces, with nine hordes and seventy princes, and now, pagan, you flee with eight companions to the sea-shore.

Daily lifeKulikovo field (Tula region)1380

The Tatars' lament: no more tribute, and their camels and sugar carried off

The poet gives the defeated Tatars a lament as they flee toward the sea: they will never again see their land or children, never again raid Rus or collect the 'vykhod', the tribute Russian princes paid the Horde. The Russians have plundered their armour, horses, oxen, camels, wine, sugar and treasures. The list shows what a Horde army carried with it: herds, camels and luxury goods from trade.

Now, brothers, we shall not be in our own land nor see our children, nor march in war on Rus, nor ask tribute of the Russian people. … for the sons of Rus have plundered the Tatar treasures, and armour, and horses, and oxen, and camels, and wine, and sugar, and costly finery.

The nameDon and Dnieper, Kulikovo field1380

Not grey wolves but the pagan Tatars: the 'Khinova' comes to Rus

The poet builds the Horde's approach from omens: creaking carts between the Don and the Dnieper, the 'Khinova' marching on the Russian land, grey wolves howling by the river Mecha. Then he turns the image: those were not grey wolves but the pagan Tatars, come to take the whole Russian land. Sreznevsky's commentary suggests 'Khinova', an old poetic name for steppe enemies also used in the Lay of Igor, may hide the name of the Huns.

The Khinova is marching on the Russian land. Grey wolves are howling; by the river Mecha they mean to cross into the Russian land. Those were not grey wolves: the pagan Tatars have come, they mean to pass through making war and take the whole Russian land.

How we know

Card wording

retrospective literary account composed within decades of 1380 from oral and chronicle tradition; the Skazanie o Mamaevom poboishche is a 15th-16th century expanded narrative

Cautions
  • Zadonshchina imitates the Slovo o polku Igoreve (or the reverse, per sceptics); Skazanie has many redactions with legendary elements; no djvu OCR obtained.

Why it matters

The Russian literary memory of the Horde at Kulikovo; the Skazanie names Mamai, the Horde army composition and Genoese allies, texts central to disputes over the battle site and date.
