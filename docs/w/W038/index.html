Abu'l Ghazi's Genealogical History of the Tatars

A 17th-century khan of Khiva tells the Turkic and Mongol peoples' own story of their origins, from Adam and the Flood through Chinggis Khan and his heirs.

Catalogue title A General History of the Turks, Moguls, and Tatars: Genealogical History

Abu al-Ghazi Bahadur Khan

Abu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (1603-1663), Uzbek ruler of Khiva (Khwarazm), wrote this dynastic history in Chaghatai Turkic near the end of his life. He compiled it from earlier Persian histories (he names Rashid al-Din) and from oral tradition, adding his own Islamic framing. Volume I covers the descent of the Turks and Mongols from Noah's son Japheth, the rival lines of Tatar Khan and Mogul Khan, the tribes, the Irgana-kon legend, Chinggis Khan, and his sons' realms, including Batu's Kipchak (Golden Horde). This corpus copy is the anonymous English translation of 1730, made through the French edition, with a translator's preface and notes.

Who “Tartar” means here
For Abu'l Ghazi, the Tatars are one ancient Turkic tribe descended from a prince named Tatar Khan, rivals and later partners of the Mongols; he writes of Tatars and Mongols as distinct peoples. His English translator uses 'Tatar' in the broad European way for all the peoples of 'Tatary'.
Why read it
A rare chance to hear an insider's version: the Turkic and Mongol peoples' own origin legends as a Chinggisid khan told them, including the melting of the iron mountain and the Tatar and Mogul twins.
Watch out
The early chapters are legend and genealogy, not eyewitness history, and the English passed through several translations. The corpus OCR for this volume is unusable (English read as Cyrillic), so every quote here was transcribed from the page images and should be checked against them.

CitiesYenisei river (Ikar), Siberia13th century and earlier

A town of pied horses and silver plate on the Yenisei

Another branch of the Tatars, Abu'l Ghazi says, settled on the river Ikar (glossed in the margin as the Yenisei). Near its mouth stood a great town called Alachzin ('pied'), ruling smaller ones, whose people rode only piebald horses and, thanks to local silver mines, used nothing but silver plate. He adds that the Uzbeks had a tradition of such a nation but could not say where it was.

Near its Mouth there is a great Town, which commands several small ones thereabouts, call'd Alachzin, that is to say, pied; because the Inhabitants of that Town have none but pied Horses … There are rich silver Mines in the Neighbourhood of that Town; and 'tis for that reason that nothing is seen there but silver Plate.

Who A branch of the Tatar tribe

Legend or rumourAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

Keep in mind. Hearsay bordering on legend; Abu'l Ghazi himself says the Uzbeks know of it only 'by Tradition'. The page footnote compares a Russian tale of a 'pied people' in Siberia.

CitiesBorders of northern China and Manchurialegendary past

Tatar Khan's realm by the powerful town of Dsurdsut

When Oghuz Khan forced his neighbours to accept the true faith, a few refugees fled to Tatar Khan, who lived near 'Dsurdsut'. Abu'l Ghazi calls it a very powerful town ruling several smaller ones, near China's frontier. Dsurdsut is most likely the Jurchen, the people of Manchuria, so here the early Tatars are placed beside a town-dwelling kingdom.

a very few who retir'd into the Dominions of Tatar Chan, who resided in the Neighbourhood of Dsurdsut. The Town of Dsurdsut is very powerful, having several small Towns under its Jurisdiction: 'Tis situate towards the Frontiers of Kitay, being called Zinu in the Indian and Persian Language.

Who Tatars under the legendary Tatar Khan; the Jurchen (Dsurdsut)

Legend or rumourAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

Keep in mind. Part of the Oghuz Khan legend; identifying Dsurdsut with the Jurchen is a modern reading, not the text's.

CitiesDasht-i Kipchak (Golden Horde)1236-1255

Batu's campaign west and his capital, Coc-orda

Abu'l Ghazi tells how Ögedei gave Batu an army to conquer the Russians ('Uresses'), Circassians and Bulgars. After a forty-day feast Batu set out, 'filled all the West' with his exploits, returned to the Kipchak steppe and died in his capital, which Abu'l Ghazi calls Coc-orda (probably Kök Orda, the 'Blue Horde'). The name shows the royal camp, the orda, serving as the capital.

gave him a numerous Army to go and subdue the Uresses, the Zerkass, the Bulgars, and other neighbouring Nations … Batu-sagin Chan after he had filled all the West with the Glory of the great Exploits which he atchieved upon that Occasion, at length return'd happily into his own Country of Dasht Kipzak, where he died some time after in the Capital of the Country called Coc-orda.

Who Batu Khan and the Mongols of the Golden Horde

Keep in mind. Written four centuries after Batu; he does not describe the capital or mention Sarai here.

Daily lifeMongol Empirecustomary rite (reported 1660s)

The khan strikes the red-hot iron

To remember the escape from Irgana-kon, Abu'l Ghazi says, the Mongols held a yearly ceremony: a piece of iron was heated red-hot, the khan struck it first with a hammer, then the tribal chiefs and officers in order of rank, then the common people. It ties Mongol kingship to smithing and iron.

They kindle upon that Day a great Fire, wherein they put a Piece of Iron to make it red-hot; after which the Chan gives the first Stroke with a Hammer upon the Piece of Iron, and then all the Heads of the Tribes and other Officers of Distinction, each according to his Rank, give it a Stroke with a Hammer

Who Mongols

Read itAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

Keep in mind. He says the Mongols 'still' observe it, but gives no date or place for the rite.

Daily lifeDasht-i Kipchak (Golden Horde)c. 1257-1266

Berke, first Muslim khan of the Golden Horde

Batu's brother and successor Berke ('Burga'), Abu'l Ghazi says, met merchants from Bukhara on the road, was won over by their explanations of Islam, adopted it on the spot, and on his return ordered his subjects to follow. It is an early step in the Horde's move to Islam, the religion of the later Volga and Crimean Tatars.

he met upon the Road with several Merchants of Great Bucharia and falling into discourse with some of them, particularly upon the Articles of the Mahometan Faith, he was so pierced with the Explanations which these Men gave him of it, that he resolv'd off-hand to embrace that Religion

Keep in mind. A simplified story told four centuries later. The page ends 'but he died before...', so check the next page for how far the conversion got.

The namelegendary past (written 1660s)

Tatar and Mogul: twin brothers

In Abu'l Ghazi's genealogy, a king named Alanza Khan, descended from Japheth son of Noah, had twin sons, Tatar and Mogul, and split his realm between them. So the Tatars and the Mongols begin as two rival brother nations. A footnote in the printed edition insists that the Tatars are named after this prince, not after a river 'Tata'.

Alanza Chan had two Twin-Sons, the one called Tatar, and the other Mogull … 'Tis from this Prince the Tatars take their Name, and not from the River Tata, as the Mogulls from Mogull.

Who Tatars and Mongols (legendary ancestors)

The nameMongolia and the borders of northern Chinabefore the 13th century

The Tatars: one of the oldest Turkic tribes

In his chapter on the tribes, Abu'l Ghazi describes the Tatars as a specific tribe, once 70,000 families under one powerful khan, that split into branches and declined. The main branch lived on the frontier of 'Kitay' (northern China) and was subject to its emperor, rebelling often. This is the historical Tatar confederation that Chinggis Khan later crushed, not the later Volga or Crimean Tatars.

The Tribe of the Tatars is one of the most antient and famous of the Turkish Nations; it was heretofore above 70000 Families strong, and had only one Chan who was very potent: But being afterwards divided into several Branches, its Power by degrees began to decline.

Who Tatar tribe (a Turkic people in Abu'l Ghazi's scheme)

The nameEurope; northern Asia1730 (translator's preface)

'We are no other than a Colony of Tatars'

The anonymous English translator argues that Europeans should despise the Tatars less because Europeans descend from them: the Cimbrians, Celts, Gauls, Goths, Huns, Vandals and Franks were all 'swarms from the same hive' out of Tatary. On the next page he even toys with the idea that the Saxons were akin to the Uzbeks. This is 18th-century speculation about 'Scythian' origins, using 'Tatar' for any northern Asian people.

Our Contempt for the Tatars would still lessen perhaps, did we consider how nearly we stand related to them: that our Ancestors came originally from the North of Asia, and that our Customs, Laws, and way of Living were formerly the same with theirs: In short, that we are no other than a Colony of Tatars.

Who Europeans and 'Tatars' in the translator's broad sense

Read itThe anonymous English translator's preface (1730), not Abu'l Ghazi

Keep in mind. The translator's own theory, offered without evidence; nothing in Abu'l Ghazi's text says this.

The nameDasht-i Kipchak (Pontic-Caspian steppe)legendary past

Kipchak: the boy born in a hollow tree

The legendary conqueror Oghuz Khan's officer died in battle, and his pregnant widow gave birth inside a hollow tree. The khan raised the boy and named him Kipchak, said to mean 'hollow tree' in old Turkish. The Kipchaks gave their name to the Dasht-i Kipchak, the steppe that later became Batu's Golden Horde.

she could find no other Place to retire to when her Time was come but an old Hollow-Tree; into which having crept when her Labour came on, she there brought forth a Son. … and gave him the Name of Kipzak, which signifies in the antient Turkish, an Hollow-Tree.

Who Kipchaks (ancestors of the Golden Horde's Turkic subjects)

Legend or rumourAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

Keep in mind. A folk etymology from the Oghuz legend.

Strange talesIrgana-kon (a legendary enclosed valley)legendary past

Melting a mountain of iron with seventy bellows

After a crushing defeat by the Tatars, the last two Mongol princes fled with their wives and herds into a valley ringed by mountains, Irgana-kon, reached only by a goat track. Four hundred years later their crowded descendants wanted out. A blacksmith noticed the mountain was thin and made of iron ore, so they piled wood and charcoal against it and melted a road through with seventy leather bellows.

a Farrier, who thought he had observ'd that the Mountain was not over thick in a certain Place, and also that it entirely consisted of Iron-Mine, propos'd to apply Bellows to it … they so effectually blew up by the help of 70 Leathern Bellows, that the Mountain at last coming to melt in this Place, left a Road large enough for the Passage of a loaded Camel

Strange talesMongolialegendary past

A being of light enters through the tent's roof opening

The widowed princess Alancu refused to remarry. One morning, she said, a shining orange-coloured man came down through the opening at the top of her chamber (in a felt tent, the smoke-hole), lay with her and vanished. In Mongol tradition the sons born after this were the ancestors of Chinggis Khan. The English margin calls it a 'Fable of an Incubus'.

having slept all Night long, she saw something as she awak'd in the Morning, as bright as the Sun, fall into her Chamber thro' the Opening a-top, and approach her in the Form of a Man, of an Orange Colour, with Eyes of a singular Beauty

Who Alancu (Alan Qo'a), ancestress of Chinggis Khan

Legend or rumourAbu'l Ghazi Bahadur Khan (English translation, 1730)

Keep in mind. The Alan Qo'a legend, known from the Secret History of the Mongols; a sacred origin myth, not history.

Strange talesIrgana-kon; the Caucasus1730 (translator's preface)

The translator: Irgana-kon is borrowed from Gog and Magog

The English translator is openly sceptical: he calls the origin of the Tatars 'fabulous', Oghuz Khan 'a mere Legend', and the bellows story 'a whimsical Invention'. In a footnote he suggests the enclosed-valley tale grew out of Arabic legends of Gog and Magog (Yajuj and Majuj) shut up behind the mountains of the far north.

Their melting their way out of the Mountains with seventy Bellows, is a whimsical Invention. … In all likelihood this Fiction of the Moguls being inclosed in the Mountain of Irgana Kon, took its Rise from one of the like kind, which we find in Arabik Authors relating to the People of Yajuj and Majuj, or Gog and Magog
