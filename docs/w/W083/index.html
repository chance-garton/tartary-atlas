James Webster's ride through the Crimea in 1827

A young Scottish lawyer's travel journal of the Crimea in 1827, with Tatar villages, the Khans' palace at Bakhchysarai and a dervish ceremony, published after his death.

Full title Travels through the Crimea, Turkey and Egypt

James Webster

Read the 12 passagesOpen the original book

James Webster, a Scottish barrister of the Inner Temple, travelled with W. H. Newnham through Poland, southern Russia, the Crimea, Turkey and Egypt between 1825 and 1828. He died young, and an editor published his journals in two volumes in 1830 as the work of 'the late James Webster'. This corpus copy is Volume I; the Crimean chapters (pages 48 to 90) record late summer 1827: Tatar caravans at Perekop, the old Tatar quarter of Simferopol, village hospitality, Alushta, the cave dwellings of Inkerman, and a long visit to Bakhchysarai with its palace, mosque, tombs and a dervish ritual. Most of it is his own observation, told with youthful wit and some condescension.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Crimean Tatars: Muslim villagers, townspeople and carters of the peninsula under Russian rule since 1783, and the memory of their former Khans of the Giray dynasty.
Why read it
Clear descriptions of what Crimean Tatar towns and buildings actually looked like in 1827: walled courtyard houses, terraced flat-roofed villages, a wood-and-clay mosque, and the Khans' palace and domed tombs at Bakhchysarai.
Watch out
Webster often mocks what he does not understand (Tatar singing, the call to prayer, the dervish rite) and relies on guides and Russian hosts for history. The book was edited after his death.

CitiesSimferopol1827

Ak-Mechet: a Russian new town beside a walled Tatar town

Webster found Simferopol, 'called by the Tartars, Ak-Metchet', split in two: the part built by the Russians since the conquest, and what remained of the old Tatar town. In the Tatar quarter every house and yard was surrounded by a wall with no window facing the street, and women rarely appeared, always veiled.

Symferapol, called by the Tartars, Ak-Metchet … consists of two parts, that which has been erected by the Russians, since their possession of the Crimea, and that which remains from the ancient Tartar town. This latter part is dull, dirty, and disgusting: every Tartar house and yard being surrounded by a wall, and not having a single window looking into

James Webster

BuildingsAlushta1827

Alushta: terraced roofs and a mosque of wood and clay

Webster describes the Tatar village of Alushta built in terraces up the hillside, so that each house's flat roof served as the street or promenade for the one above. Its mosque, under huge walnut trees, was a 'rude structure of wood and clay' with a projecting roof. It is a precise picture of vernacular Tatar building materials.

Alushta is built on a sort of terrace, so that the flat roof of one house serves as a promenade to the people of the upper one. There are seven or eight rows of these houses, none having more than one flat, and that very low, with a portico in front … The mosque is embowered among old and immense walnut trees. It is a rude structure of wood and clay

James Webster

BuildingsInkerman1827

Cave homes at Inkerman, later reused by Tatars

Webster examined the rock-cut chambers and chapel at Inkerman, near Sevastopol. From the altar, carved cross and style he judged them medieval Christian dwellings, and he noticed rafter marks showing some were part built, with a few 'reinhabited, or reconstructed by the Tartars'. He credits the builders to Christians, not Tatars, and says the Tatars reused them.

Some seem to have been partly excavated and partly built, which is proved by the marks of the rafters in the rocks, as also by these being, in a few instances, reinhabited, or reconstructed by the Tartars … the inference is natural that these excavations were the dwellings of the Christians.

James Webster

BuildingsBakhchysarai1827

The golden chamber of the Khans' palace

Webster describes the palace of the Khans set in a great courtyard with the palace, mosque and tombs together. Past a marble fountain, a staircase led to the 'golden chamber' with a ceiling of gilded stars. On the next page he adds the crimson walls, stained glass and divans, faded but still showing 'the magnificence of the Tartar rulers', and notes Russian repairs under way.

The most interesting object is the palace of the Khans, which stands in a large area … We entered the court-yard, which brought into our view the palace, the mosque, and the tombs … In the entrance we passed a marble fountain, and ascended a spacious staircase, which brought us to a large room, called the golden chamber, the ceiling of which, divided into compartments, and studded with gilded stars

James Webster

BuildingsBakhchysarai1827

Low roofs, minaret chimneys and a wooden harem

Seen from the garden, the Khans' palace was a low building, in parts only one storey, with white roofs and chimneys shaped like little minarets. The harem was a square building of three lofty rooms with verandas and small wooden-grated windows, behind a high wall. The palace of the Crimean Khans, in other words, was modest in scale.

This harem is a square building, with verandas, having only three rooms: these are large and lofty … Each room has a double range of small windows, with wooden gratings … The appearance of the exterior of the palace, as seen from the garden, is that of a low building, in some parts only one story. Its roof is white, and the chimnies appear like so many minarets

James Webster

BuildingsBakhchysarai1827

Domed tombs of the Khans

Beside the palace mosque, the Khans lay in a small burial ground of Turkish-style tombs, some of marble, and in two domed octagonal mausoleums about thirty feet across. Inside were wooden coffins draped in black cloth and topped with the turbans of the dead. Webster also describes the town's one-storey houses and streets of shops grouped by trade.

There are two large octagons, with domes, of about thirty feet diameter, which contain the bodies and monuments of many Khans. In the first are ten or twelve wooden coffins, covered with black cloth, and having at the top the turbans of the deceased.

James Webster

BuildingsBetween Simferopol and Alushta, Crimea1827

Inside a spotless Tatar house

After rejecting a filthy Russian post-house, Webster's party lodged with a Tatar family and were 'much surprised' by the cleanliness: double carpets, a row of cushions along the wall to sleep on, an open hearth in a big chimney, cushions piled to the roof and pots ranged on the rafters, all whitewashed. On the next page the host hints he hopes to be paid, unlike some Russian officers who answered with blows.

On entering, we were much surprised at the perfect cleanliness of the house. The room was doubly carpeted, and along the wall was a row of cushions, of sufficient breadth to admit of a traveller's stretching himself with perfect comfort. The fire was on the ground, in a large open chimney.

James Webster

Daily lifeAlushta1827

Graves dug for pilgrims to Mecca

In Alushta Webster watched villagers dig two holes near the mosque for the two oldest men, about to leave on pilgrimage to Mecca. If they did not return, their upright gravestones topped with turbans would be set up there. The whole village was to share a feast paid for by voluntary gifts.

The two oldest, and for whom this ceremony was acting, were to set out, in two days, on a pilgrimage to Mecca, and, should they not return, their monuments, upright stones, surmounted by turbans, would be placed on the spot where the holes were dug.

James Webster

Daily lifePerekop1827

Four hundred ungreased wheels

At the entrance to the Crimea Webster met a Tatar caravan of about a hundred wagons, drawn by oxen and camels, whose ungreased wheels made an 'excruciating' noise. The Tatars in their black lambskin caps, the camels and the bustards on the plain struck him as a novel eastern scene.

These people never grease the wheels. The creaking of one is sufficiently offensive; but the noise of four hundred was absolutely excruciating. The Tartars, too, were singing, which, with them, is another term for howling … The Tartars, in their black lamb-skin caps and eastern dresses, the camels grazing on each side of the road

James Webster

Daily lifeBakhchysarai1827

Dervishes 'snorting' in the Khans' mosque

For a few rubles, Webster's party had thirteen men, five of them dervishes, perform their ritual (a zikr) in the Khans' mosque. He describes the rhythmic bowing and rasping breath in comic, hostile terms, yet on the next page concedes the rite was 'civilization' next to the flagellations he had seen in Rome.

They first snorted with great fury, then bellowed as furiously; the bellow consisting of three notes, like that of a calf, or a bull. Each time they bent forward with a violent motion. The rite altogether is absolutely terrific, and must be most fatiguing.

James Webster

About Crimean Tatar dervishes

Keep in mind

Daily lifeCrimea1827

The happiest peasantry possible

Webster ends his Crimean chapters defending Tatar 'laziness' against Russian complaints: the Tatar sits on his roof under his grandfather's walnut tree and wants nothing more. He honestly adds that, not speaking their language, a traveller may mistake the look of happiness for the real thing.

The Russians exclaim against the laziness of the Tartars: but wherefore should they work? They are the happiest peasantry possible without it … not understanding their language, we may mistake the appearance of happiness for its reality.

James Webster

About Crimean Tatars

Keep in mind

A romantic generalisation, which Webster himself qualifies.

Strange talesKyzyl-Tash, south coast of Crimea1827

The bride inside the Red Rock

Webster records a legend 'implicitly believed by the Tartars': a girl forced to marry against her will prayed to Allah to bury her alive inside the red rock of Keesil-tash, and at every full moon at midnight the rock opens and she walks out in her bridal clothes. The next page adds a Tatar's dry joke about why a Russian count failed to see her.

She besought Allah to hide her from the world, and to bury her alive in the centre of the Keesil-tash. This prayer was granted, and she was snatched away by an invisible hand. She is said to inhabit the rock, but at every full moon, at midnight, the rock, as it is declared, opens, and the lady walks forth in her

James Webster
