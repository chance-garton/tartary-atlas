Written source · Latin

Friar Riccoldo's pilgrimage book, with chapters on the Tartars of Persia and Baghdad

A Florentine Dominican who lived under Mongol rule in Tabriz and Baghdad around 1288 to 1300 describes the Tartars' looks, laws, women, funerals, legends and conquests.

Full title Book of Pilgrimage (Liber peregrinationis), with chapters on the Tartars of Persia and Baghdad, in Four Medieval Pilgrims

Riccoldo of Monte Croce; J. C. M. Laurent, editor

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, a Dominican friar from Florence, sailed to Acre around 1288, crossed Cilician Armenia and Anatolia into Ilkhanid Persia, preached for half a year at Tabriz and lived for years in Baghdad before returning to Florence and writing his Liber peregrinationis around 1300. Chapters 9 to 13 are a small treatise on the Tartars: their appearance and hatred of cities, their customs and funerals, the owl legend of how they left their mountains, Genghis Khan and the arrows, the sack of Baghdad in 1258, and their drift to Islam. This corpus holds J. C. M. Laurent's Latin edition (Peregrinatores medii aevi quatuor, Leipzig 1864 and 1873, with the pilgrim Burchard of Mount Sion in the same volume), a 1793 Florentine Italian version and his 1511 anti-Islamic tract.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongols of the Ilkhanate (Hulegu, Abaqa, Arghun and their armies and officials) whom Riccoldo met in Anatolia, Tabriz and Baghdad. He also treats 'Mogoli' as their own name and ties them to Gog and Magog.
Why read it
A rare Latin eyewitness from inside Mongol-ruled Baghdad: part sharp observation (armed women riding into towns, funeral horses on poles, the name left blank in letters), part hearsay and legend (the owl, Gog and Magog, the Lost Tribes).
Watch out
Riccoldo is a missionary who openly despises the Tartars and mixes what he saw with tales he heard; some stories (the owl, the caliph made to eat gold, the officer boiled and eaten) are legends or rumours. The Latin OCR is noisy, and the 1793 Italian text in this corpus is a shorter Tuscan version that stops before the Tartar chapters.

CitiesBaghdad1258

The caliph of Baghdad made to eat his gold

Riccoldo's version of the fall of Baghdad, told a generation later by a man living in the city. The Tartars found the caliph in a marvellous palace heaped with treasure, starved him for three days, set gold, silver and jewels before him, and when he said he had only kept what his ancestors gathered, poured molten gold into his mouth. They then killed the people for forty days.

When he fell silent they ordered gold to be melted and poured into his mouth, and said to him: 'You thirsted for gold, drink gold!' And they killed his people for forty days on end.

CitiesBaghdadc. 1290

Baghdad forty years on: mostly ruined, still crowded, ruled by Tartars

Riccoldo lived in Baghdad and describes it after the Mongol conquest: the city is 'for the most part destroyed' yet still full of people, with many thousands of Christians and Jews and over two hundred thousand Muslims, all under Tartar lordship. He also found its Muslim colleges and Sufi houses thriving and admired their scholars.

In that city, although it is for the most part destroyed, there is nevertheless a very great multitude of people. For besides Christians and Jews, who are many thousands there, there are more than two hundred thousand Saracens, as is plausibly believed; yet all are under the lordship of the Tartars.

BuildingsAnatoliac. 1288

A people who seem to hate every city and building

Riccoldo's first sight of the Tartars, inside 'Turchia' (Seljuk Anatolia under Mongol rule). He describes broad faces, narrow eyes and thin beards, and then claims they 'seem to hate every city and every dwelling building', destroying nearly all the towns, castles and houses they meet. For a source on who built what in 'Tartary', this is a blunt statement from a man who lived among them that the Tartars were destroyers, not builders.

but they seem to hate every city and every dwelling building. For they destroy almost all cities and castles and dwellings and buildings, and they harm most the one who humbles himself most before them

Daily lifeTabrizc. 1288 to 1300

Women who ride into town with bow and arrows

Riccoldo says Tartar women are held in great honour, run the realm or the household, buy and sell, and are fierce and warlike. He adds from his own eyes that they ride like men and that he often saw them entering cities armed with bows and arrows. Elsewhere on the same page he explains their tall crowns, topped with a foot to remind them they are still under their husbands.

and it is they who govern the kingdom or the household, and they buy and sell, and they are fierce and warlike. They ride like men, and I often saw them entering cities girded with bow and arrows like men.

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

A horse on a pole for the dead lord

Riccoldo describes Tartar funerals: cooked meat, clothes and money buried with the dead, and for a great lord a horse ridden to exhaustion, killed, stuffed with green grass and hung up on a stake, told to be ready whenever its master rises again. He adds that they once buried up to twenty living slaves with a lord until Christians shamed them out of it, and that Tartars even paid bishops to be buried in Christian cemeteries.

and the horse falls, and he guts it and empties everything from the horse's belly and fills it with green grass, and then drives a great stake in from behind and makes the stake come out at the mouth, and so leaves the horse impaled, and hangs it up and orders it to be ready whenever its lord wishes to rise

Working translation

Original wording

et equus cadit, et ipse exenterat eum et euacuat omnia de ventre equi, et implet herba viridi, et postea infigit palum magnum per posteriora, et facit palum exire vsque ad os, et ita dimittit equum impalatum, et suspendit eum et mandat ei, quod sit paratus, quandocumque wlt dominus surgere

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

Execution called 'sleep', and a blank space for the lord's name

Two sharp details of Mongol court manners. When writing to a superior, a Tartar leaves a gap where the lord's name belongs and writes the name out in the margin. When a man is condemned, the lord says 'cut him and make him sleep', because they do not call death death, and the condemned simply holds out his arms to be bound.

When they write to their superiors they keep such reverence that they do not write their superior's name in its place, but leave an empty space and afterwards write the name outside, in the margin, in another place.

Original wording

Cum scribunt suis maioribus, tantam reuerenciam habent et seruant, quod non scribant nomen sui maioris in suo loco, sed dimittunt spacium vacuum et postea scribunt nomen de foris in margine in alio loco.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1873)

Daily lifeIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1260 to 1300

Why the Tartars became Muslims

Riccoldo explains the Ilkhanate's conversion to Islam from a missionary's frustrated point of view: the Tartars had neither law nor prophet, found Islam an easy law, and Muslims gave them rich gifts to convert, so that now 'for the most part' they have become Saracens. He adds that many still like Christians but find Christianity too hard and are offered no presents.

they took up the law and became Saracens, above all because the Tartars had neither law nor prophet. So now for the greater part the Tartars have become Saracens. For the Saracens buy them and give them very great gifts so that they will become Saracens.

Working translation

Original wording

acceperunt legem, et facti sunt Sarraceni, maxime quia ipsi Tartari nec legem nec prophetam habebant. Vnde modo pro magna parte Tartari facti sunt Sarraceni. Nam et Sarraceni eos emunt et dant eis maxima dona, vt ipsi efficiantur Sarraceni.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

The nameCaspian Gates (Caucasus)c. 1300

'Mogoli' from 'Magogoli': the Tartars and Gog and Magog

Riccoldo weighs the theory that the Tartars were the Lost Ten Tribes of Israel shut behind the Caspian mountains by Alexander, notes that they hate Alexander's name and that their script looks like Chaldean, then argues against it. He reports that they themselves claim descent from Gog and Magog, which is why they are called Mogoli, as if a corruption of 'Magogoli'. It is a textbook example of how Europeans fitted the Mongols into biblical and Alexander legends.

They themselves say they are descended from Gog and Magog. Hence they are called Mogoli, as if from a corruption of the word Magogoli.

Ipsi vero dicunt se descendisse de Gog et Magog. Vnde ipsi dicuntur Mogoli, quasi corrupto vocabulo Magogoli.

The hare, the owl and the fortress of wind

Riccoldo's origin tale: the Tartars lived shut in by mountains whose only pass held an empty fortress that roared with the noise of horses, men and trumpets, a trick of the wind. A hunter chasing a hare followed it in, saw an owl singing on the gate, reasoned that no one lives where a hare runs and an owl sings, and led his people out. So, Riccoldo says, Tartars honour the hare and paint it on arms and tents, and nobles wear owl feathers and owl skins, which Western merchants now sell them for up to twenty pounds each.

'There is no dwelling of men where a hare flees and an owl sings.' And so, entering confidently, he found no one.

Original wording

Non est habitacio hominum, vbi lepus fugit et bubo cantat. Et sic fiducialiter intrans neminem inuenit.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Strange talesIlkhanate (Persia and Iraq)c. 1290

A shaman who seemed to fly

Riccoldo says the Tartars honour above all men certain 'boxitae' (bakhshis), priests of idols from India, wise and grave, who practise magic with the help of demons, show many marvels and foretell the future. One of their leaders was said to fly, but, Riccoldo reports, it was found that he only walked just above the ground without touching it and sat on nothing solid.

For one of the greater among them was said to fly, but according to the truth it was found that he did not fly, but walked close to the ground without touching it, and when he seemed to sit he was held up by nothing solid.

Original wording

Nam et vnus maior inter eos dicebatur volare, sed secundum veritatem repertum est, quod non volauit, sed ambulabat iuxta terram et eam non tangebat, et quando videbatur sedere, nulla re solida sustentabatur.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Strange talesSyriac. 1280

The officer boiled alive by the Tartar women

After Abaqa's army turned tail before the Mamluks in Syria, Riccoldo says, a great Tartar baron was caught plotting revolt. When the khan wanted to execute him, the Tartar ladies asked for him instead, boiled him alive and fed him in tiny pieces to the whole army as a warning.

the ladies and women of the Tartars opposed it, asking that he be given to them. When he was handed over they cooked him alive and gave him, cut into the smallest pieces, to the whole army to eat, as an example to others.

Working translation

Original wording

obstiterunt domine et mulieres Tartarorum, petentes eum sibi dari. Quo accepto coxerunt eum viuum, et dederunt eum comedere incisum per partes minimas toti exercitui in exemplum aliorum.

Riccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1873)

firsthand The author saw the places or people described.

firsthand observation: Dominican missionary who travelled through Anatolia, Armenia and Ilkhanid Persia to Baghdad c. 1288-1300 and describes Tartar customs, religion and rule at first hand

Research notes
  • Laurent's text is from the Berlin MS; modern edition Kappler 1997 (Latin/French). Ricoldo also wrote Contra legem Sarracenorum (1511 print bub_gb_DP9sEe62VNgC on IA) which is polemic, not travel.
  • The same volume contains Burchard of Mount Sion, Odoric of Pordenone (Latin) and Wilbrand of Oldenburg.

Why it matters

Rare Latin eyewitness ethnography of the Mongols in the Ilkhanate (chapters De Tartaris: origin legend, customs, religion, Genghis Khan story) by a friar who lived in Baghdad; independent of the Carpini/Rubruck line.
