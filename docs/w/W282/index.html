Georgi's catalogue of all the peoples of the Russian Empire

A German academician's 1770s encyclopedia of the empire's peoples, with a whole section on the 'Tatar nations'.

Catalogue title Description of All the Nations of the Russian Empire, their way of life, religion, customs, dwellings, clothing and other remarkable features, 4 parts with coloured plates (part 2: Tatar nations; part 4: Mongolian nations)

Johann Gottlieb Georgi

Johann Gottlieb Georgi, a German naturalist of the St Petersburg Academy, travelled in Siberia and the Urals in 1770 to 1774 and then compiled this survey of every nation in the Russian Empire (first published 1776 to 1780). He combined his own observations with the reports of Pallas, Falck, Lepekhin, Gmelin, Müller and others. The second part treats the 'Tatar nations' from Kazan to the Yenisei, with an overview of their names, history, ruins and legal status, followed by entries on each group; a later part covers the Mongol peoples (Kalmyks, Buryats). The copy read here is the 1783 reissue in two volumes held by the Bavarian State Library.

Who “Tartar” means here
The Turkic-speaking peoples of the Russian Empire (Kazan, Siberian, Tobolsk, Tomsk, Crimean and Nogai Tatars, Bashkirs, Kazakhs and others), which Georgi carefully separates from the Mongol peoples such as Kalmyks and Buryats.
Why read it
It shows an eighteenth-century scholar sorting the old catch-all 'Tartary' into distinct peoples, and it lists real Tatar ruins (Kazan, Kasimov, Bolgar, Sibir) with the stone and brick they were built of.
Watch out
A compilation: Georgi seldom says which colleague supplied which detail, and his history leans on Abulghazi and older European writers. The German is eighteenth-century Fraktur, so quotes are OCR-normalized.

CitiesTyumen13th century to 1580, and the 1770s

The 'town people' of the Tura and the old city of Tyumen

Georgi explains that one Tatar colony in western Siberia took to fixed dwellings and towns earlier than others, and so was called Turali, 'town people', from the Tatar word tura, a town. Their oldest seat was Chingi Tura, later called Tyumen, a name the Russians gave to the city they built in its place. He adds that when Yermak conquered them in 1580 their ruler lived in another town, Tura, rebuilt by the Russians in 1600 as Turinsk.

the Tatar colony … had more and earlier reason than other colonies to think of permanent, fixed dwellings or towns (Tatar tura), from which they were called Turali or Turalinzy, that is, town or settled people. … Their oldest fixed seat was Chingi Tura (the town of Chingi), which they later named Tyumen

A khan's tower, mosque and mausoleum at Kasimov

Georgi says that across the old Tatar lands one finds ditches, ramparts and ruined towns, which Russians call gorodishche. At Kasimov on the Oka he lists a tall round tower, a mosque, palace walls and a mausoleum, all of rubble stone and fired brick, with an Arabic inscription naming the local khan Shah Ali. On the same page he describes the 'splendid' ruins of Bolgar on the Volga (towers, mosques, houses and tombs of stone and brick) and the stone walls, towers and former khan's palace inside the Kazan fortress, which he calls a monument of the Tatar kingdom.

At Kasimov on the Oka, for example, the Tatar suburb (sloboda) seems to have been the court of a khan. Among the ruins there is a tall round tower (Tatar misgir [minaret]), a prayer house (Tatar mechet [mosque]), walls of a palace, and in a cemetery (Tatar mazar) a handsome mausoleum, all of rubble stone and fired brick.

Daily life1770s

Free men who elect their own leaders

Georgi describes the Tatars' legal position in the empire: they were free, never subject to serfdom, and kept their own customs. They chose their own headmen, usually from nobles and princes who traced their descent in a direct line from Chinggis Khan and Batu. He adds that their tribute was light and some groups served as Cossacks instead.

The Tatars are free people, not subject to serfdom at all, and they remain undisturbed in their ancestral constitutions. They elect their superiors from among themselves, usually from their nobility, which partly consists of princes who are accustomed to derive their descent in a straight line from Chinggis, Batu and other famous rulers.

Original wording

Die Tataren sind freye, der Leibeigenschaft gar nicht unterworfene Leute, und bleiben bey ihren väterlichen Verfassungen ungestört. The name1770s

Tatars, Turks and a name some found insulting

Georgi opens his section on the 'Tatar nations' by noting that most of these peoples call themselves Turks or Turkomans rather than Tatars. Following the Khivan khan and historian Abulghazi, he traces both names to an ancestor Turk and his grandson Tatar. He adds that many Tatars found the name itself offensive because their rulers used it with contempt.

The Tatar tribes found under such varied names inside and outside Russia call themselves sometimes Tatars, but mostly Turuks, Turks, also Turkomans. Their historians, especially Abulghazi Bahadur Khan, derive both names from their fruitful ancestor Turuk or Turk and one of his grandsons, Tatar … so many Tatars regard it [the name Tatar] as insulting.

Working translation

Original wording

Die unter so verschiedenen Namen in und ausser Rußland befindlichen Tatarischen Stämme nennen sich bald Tataren, bald und meistens Turuken, Turken, auch Turkomanen. Beyde Benennungen leiten ihre Geschichtschreiber, besonders Abulgasi Bajadur Chan … von ihrem fruchtbaren Stammvater Turuk, oder Turk und einem seiner Enkel Tatar … so halten ihn viele Tataren für kränkend.

Why it matters
