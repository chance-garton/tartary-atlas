The Qing gazetteer of Western Regions names

An imperial glossary of 1763 giving Central Asian place and personal names in six scripts.

Full title Imperially Commissioned Gazetteer of Unified Script for the Western Regions (multilingual place-name and personal-name glossary), 24 juan, 1763

欽定西域同文志

Fuheng and the Qianlong commission

Open the original book

Fuheng and a Qianlong commission compiled this 24 chapter glossary in 1763, after the Qing conquest of Dzungaria and the Tarim Basin. It lists places, mountains, rivers and rulers of Dzungaria, Kashgar, Yarkand, Khotan, Turfan, Qinghai, Tibet and Mongolia. Each name appears in Chinese with Manchu, Mongolian, Tibetan, Oirat and Turki forms and a Chinese gloss, drawn from administrative records and local informants. The card lists a Siku quanshu manuscript copy. The atlas holds a stored machine reading (OCR) of this scan, of fair to poor quality, but no passages were selected or read for this guide.

How they knew
Official record
Who “Tartar” means here
Not recorded. This is a Qing name list that names the Dzungars (Oirats), Kazakhs, Kirghiz and Turkic Muslims by their own names, not a European work using the label Tartary.
Why read it
It shows how the Qing court itself named the lands that European maps often labelled Tartary, with the native forms side by side.
Watch out
The non-Chinese scripts in the manuscript copy were hand copied and may hold copyist errors compared with the 1763 palace print. The card also notes a companion item that was not examined.

What it says

No quotes from this one yet. Its text is out of reach for now, or the scan could not be read.

How we know

official compilation from administrative records and local informants after the conquest of Dzungaria and Kashgaria (1755-1759)

Research notes
  • Siku copy is a manuscript transcription; the non-Chinese scripts are hand-copied and may contain copyist errors compared with the 1763 palace print. Companion user-upload IA item 'xiyutongwenzhi' (181 files, PDFs of a modern reprint) not examined in detail.

Why it matters

The central Qing name register for Central Asia: gives each place, mountain, river and ruler's name in six scripts with etymologies, letting the inventory map Qing Chinese forms onto Mongolian, Oirat, Turki and Tibetan forms.
