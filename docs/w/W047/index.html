Xuanzang's Records of the Western Regions

A Chinese Buddhist pilgrim's country-by-country description of Central Asia and India, 629 to 645, with the Western Turks as overlords of the Silk Road.

Catalogue title Si-yu-ki: Buddhist Records of the Western World

Xuanzang; Bianji, compiler

Xuanzang (about 602 to 664), a Chinese Buddhist monk, travelled from Chang'an across Central Asia to India and back between 629 and 645. At the emperor's request his disciple Bianji compiled this record of the countries he passed or heard of from his reports in 646: distances, cities, crops, customs and many local legends, some places visited and some only heard of. The corpus copy is volume 2 of Samuel Beal's English translation (1884), covering books 6 to 12, which end with the return journey through Afghanistan, the Pamirs and Khotan. The Chinese text (Siku quanshu copy, Kanseki Repository) is also in the corpus and adds book 1, the outward route through the lands of the Western Turks.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Xuanzang never says 'Tartar'. He writes of the Tujue (Beal's 'Tu-kiueh', the Turks), whose Western Turkic khaganate ruled the oasis states in his day, and of the ancient Xiongnu ('Hiung-nu'). Beal's index and notes use 'Tartar' for the Hu and for the Toba Wei, and 'High Tartary' for the Tarim Basin.
Why read it
A rare seventh-century view of Central Asian steppe rulers from the Chinese side: Turkish kings who own towns but live in tents, subject cities, captives, and the legends told along the road.
Watch out
Only volume 2 of Beal is in the corpus; the book 1 passages here are quoted from the Chinese with my own working translation. Many stories are local legend that Xuanzang reports as 'the common story'. Beal's 1884 glosses (such as 'Hu people (Uighurs)') are his own.

CitiesTaraz, Kazakhstan630

A walled town of Chinese captives taken by the Turks

A little south of the merchant city of Taraz stood a small walled town of some 300 households whose people were originally Chinese. They had been carried off by the Turks, then gathered their countrymen together and held this town. They had taken up Turkish dress and ways, though the next page adds that they still kept the speech and manners of their homeland. A glimpse of who actually lived in towns under steppe rule.

Ten li or so to the south is a small isolated town of more than 300 households. They were originally people of China, carried off long ago by the Turks. Later they gathered together their fellow countrymen and jointly hold this town, living inside it. In clothing and bearing they have become like the Turks.

CitiesKucha, Xinjiangearly 7th century (tradition)

A town emptied by Turkish soldiers near Kucha

On Kucha's eastern border lay a town with no wells, whose people drank from a dragon pool. The story went that dragons had fathered a race of strong, swift men there who defied the king, so the king called in the Turks, who killed everyone, young and old. The town was now waste, with no smoke of hearths. A dragon legend wraps what may be a real memory of a Turkish massacre.

There were no wells in the city; they drew water from the pool. The dragons turned into men and lay with the women, who bore sons brave and fierce, able to outrun a galloping horse. Gradually all the people became of dragon stock; trusting in their strength they defied the king's orders. So the king called in the Turks to slaughter the people of this city, young and old alike, leaving hardly a soul. The city is now a waste, with no smoke of hearths.

CitiesKunduz, Afghanistanabout 644

A Turkish king who will not stay in his own capital

Kunduz (Huo) had a walled chief town and farmland, but its king was a Turk who ruled all the small kingdoms south of the Iron Gates. He did not live in the town: he kept moving 'like a bird'. Chains of neighbouring states in the old Tukhara lands are each described as having a capital but 'no chief ruler', being dependent on the Turks.

The king is of the Turkish clan; he governs all the little kingdoms to the south of the Iron Gates. He constantly shifts his dwelling, like a bird (his bird-dwelling), not constantly occupying this town.

CitiesSouthern Tarim Basin, between Niya and Cherchen, Xinjiangabout 644

Empty cities of an old kingdom in the desert

Crossing the drifting sands east of Niya, travellers piled up bones to mark the way, and people heard singing or wailing that led them astray, which Xuanzang blames on demons. After 400 li he reached the 'old country of Tukhara', long empty, its cities all desolate. He credits the ruins to a vanished kingdom, not to any Turks or Tartars.

So those who come and go pile up the bones of the dead to mark the way. Water and grass are lacking and hot winds are frequent... At times one hears singing and whistling, or wailing and crying, and between what is seen and heard one is bewildered and does not know where one is going; many die this way, which is the work of demons and goblins. Going 400 li or so one reaches the old country of Tukhara. The country has long been empty and its cities are all in ruins.

BuildingsHimatala, Badakhshan, Afghanistanabout 644

Dozens of fortified towns, yet the people live in felt tents

Because the land bordered the Turks, its people took up Turkish ways and were raided by them. They scattered, held dozens of strong walled towns each under its own chief, and lived in domed felt tents, moving about. Towns and tents side by side, in the same breath. Beal's English of this passage runs across pages 290 to 291.

[They took on] their [the Turks'] customs, and were also raided by them, and so defended their own borders. Therefore the people of this country are scattered in different districts, in several tens of strong walled towns, each with its own chief. They live in domed felt tents and move about from place to place.

BuildingsCentral Asian steppe646

The Horse Lord's people live in felt domes

The preface divides the known world among four 'lords': elephants in the south, treasure in the west, men (China) in the east, and horses in the cold north. The northern horse peoples are fierce, live in felt tents and domed yurts, and move 'like birds' after their herds. It is a learned Buddhist scheme rather than an eyewitness scene, but it is a compact Chinese picture of steppe housing.

In the north is the Horse Lord, whose land is bitterly cold and suited to horses... The ways of the Horse Lord's people: fierce by nature, hardened to killing; they live in felt tents and domed yurts, dwelling like birds and following their herds.

Daily lifeThousand Springs (Mingbulak), near Taraz, Kazakhstan630

The Turkish khan's tame deer at the Thousand Springs

The meadows of the Thousand Springs were where the khan of the Western Turks came every summer to escape the heat. Herds of deer there wore bells and rings and were so tame they barely ran from people, because the khan loved them and decreed death without pardon for anyone who killed one. Xuanzang passed this way on his outward journey.

The khan of the Turks comes here every year to escape the summer heat. There are herds of deer, many decorated with bells and rings, so tame with people that they hardly startle and run. The khan loves them and has ordered his followers that anyone who dares to kill one will be executed without pardon, so these deer live out their natural lives.

Daily lifeHimatala, Badakhshan, Afghanistanabout 644

Dressed like the Turks, crowned with a wooden horn

In Himatala, near Badakhshan, people behaved and dressed like the Turks, in wool, skin and felt. Married women wore a wooden horn about three feet long on the head, with two branches standing for the husband's father and mother; when one parent-in-law died a branch was removed, and when both were dead the horn was given up.

In respect of their modes of behaviour and forms of etiquette, their clothes of wool, and skin, and felt, they are like the Turks. Their wives wear upon their head-dress a wooden horn about three feet or so in length. It has two branches (a double branch) in front, which signify father and mother of the husband.

The nameTashkurgan, Xinjiangabout 644

'Hu': the Chinese word the translator turns into Uighurs or Tartars

The royal family of Tashkurgan in the Pamirs looked Chinese but dressed like the 'Hu'. Hu was a broad Chinese word for foreigners of the west and north. Beal adds '(Uighurs)' here, and in his index he lists 'Tartar (Hu) pilgrims', so the English 'Tartar' in this book is often the translator's choice for a Chinese catch-all word, not Xuanzang's.

The members of the royal family in appearance resemble the people of the Middle Country (China). They wear on their heads a square cap, and their clothes are like those of the Hu people (Uighurs).

Strange talesBalkh, Afghanistanearly 7th century

A Turkish prince speared in a dream for raiding a monastery

The New Monastery at Balkh was so rich that rulers coveted it. A son of the Turkish Yabghu khan (named on the previous page) led his whole horde to plunder it and camped nearby. That night he dreamed that the guardian god Vaisravana drove a long halberd through his chest; he woke in pain, sent for the monks to beg forgiveness, and died before the messenger returned.

[He gathered] his tribes and led his troops to fall on the monastery by surprise, meaning to seize its treasures. He camped in the open not far from here. That night he dreamed he saw the god Vaisravana, who said: 'What power have you, that you dare to destroy this monastery?' and drove a long halberd through him from chest to back. The khan woke in terror, with a pain in his heart.

Strange talesKhotan, Xinjiangancient (legend)

Sacred rats that gnawed a Xiongnu army's harness

West of Khotan stood mounds made by rats 'as big as hedgehogs', with gold and silver fur. Long ago, the story went, a Xiongnu army camped there; the king of Khotan prayed to the rats, and in the morning the raiders found every strap, bowstring and fastening gnawed through. Khotan built the rats a temple, and travellers still got down from their carts to bow as they passed.

The Hiung-nu, hearing their approach, were overcome by fear. They hastened to harness their horses and equip their chariots, but they found that the leather of their armour, and their horses' gear, and their bow strings, and all the fastenings of their clothes, had been gnawed by the rats.

Who Xiongnu (Hiung-nu), ancient steppe raiders

Legend or rumourXuanzang and Bianji (translated by Samuel Beal, 1884)

Keep in mind. Xuanzang introduces it as 'the common story' he heard. Beal notes a parallel in Herodotus (mice gnawing an Assyrian army's bowstrings).

Strange talesKhotan oasis, Xinjiangancient (legend)

A city buried overnight by a rain of sand and earth

East of Khotan Xuanzang heard of Ho-lo-lo-kia, a rich city that mistreated a holy man and was buried when 'it rained sand and earth' for a night. One man escaped by a tunnel. The town was now a great sand mound; kings who came to dig for its treasure were driven off by sudden winds and darkness. Beal's note points to other sand-buried towns reported by nineteenth-century travellers in the region.

The town of Ho-lo-lo-kia is now a great sand mound. The kings of the neighbouring countries and persons in power from distant spots have many times wished to excavate the mound and take away the precious things buried there; but as soon as they have arrived at the borders of the place, a furious wind has sprung up
