Edifying and curious letters: Jesuit reports from China and Tartary

Letters from French Jesuits in Qing China, 1690s to 1770s, reporting on Manchu and Mongol 'Tartary', in Querbeuf's Paris edition of 1780 to 1783.

Full title Edifying and curious letters written from the foreign missions, new edition (26 vols, Paris 1780-1783): Memoirs of China volumes containing letters on Tartary by Gerbillon, Jartoux (ginseng in Tartary, 1711), Parrenin, Regis, Gaubil, Amiot and others

Jesuit missionaries; Y. M. M. de Querbeuf, editor

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

The Lettres édifiantes et curieuses were letters sent home by Jesuit missionaries and printed in Paris to inform and inspire readers. The China volumes include men who lived at the Qing court and travelled in Manchuria and Mongolia with the Kangxi and Qianlong emperors or on the imperial map survey: Parrenin, Jartoux, Fontaney, Noël and Amiot. They mix firsthand observation with court news and pious stories. The letters were cut and rewritten by Paris editors. This corpus has Querbeuf's new edition (Paris, Mérigot, 1780 to 1783, vols 17 to 25) and a Toulouse reprint of vol. 18 (T0654; IA dates it 1707, but it prints letters of 1711 in nineteenth-century spelling, so it is evidently the Toulouse edition of about 1810).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mostly the Manchus ('Tartares Mantcheoux', 'Tartares orientaux') who ruled China, and the Mongols ('Tartares occidentaux', 'Mongous', Khalkhas), with the Dzungars (Eleuths) on the far frontier; the writers also speak of 'Chinese made Tartar' in the Manchu banners.
Why read it
Direct reports from Europeans who rode with the Qing emperors into Tartary: a palisade across Manchuria, ten thousand ginseng hunters, a Tartar city inside Peking, and the Manchu script debated with a prince.
Watch out
Letters were chosen, cut and polished in Paris to edify readers; missionary aims colour what is reported. Volume numbers differ between editions, and the OCR of the 1780 set is fair but uneven.

CitiesManchuria and Mongoliac. 1703

New towns in eastern Tartary, none in the west

In a report on the China missions presented in Rome, François Noël says Eastern Tartary (Manchuria) is filling up: the emperor is building towns there and villages are well peopled. Western Tartary has neither towns nor villages except toward the Uzbeks and the Caspian; its peoples, recently subjected to the emperor, own nothing but herds, and move on every three months or so when the grass is eaten.

Eastern Tartary is filling with people every day. The Emperor is having towns built there, and very populous villages can be seen. As for Western Tartary, there are neither towns nor villages except toward the Uzbeks and the Caspian Sea … All the wealth of these peoples consists only of numerous herds, with which they wander here and there.

Working translation

Original wording

La Tartarie orientale se peuple tous les jours. L'Empereur y fait bâtir des villes, & l'on y voit des villages fort peuplés. Pour la Tartarie occidentale, il n'y a ni villes ni villages que du côté des Yousbecks, & de la mer Caspienne … Toutes les richesses de ces Peuples ne consistent qu'en de nombreux troupeaux, avec lesquels ils errent de côté & d'autre.

François Noël, Mémoire on the state of the China missions (presented in Latin at Rome, translated into French)

CitiesBeijing1688 (recalled in a letter of 1703)

Peking: a Tartar city and a Chinese city

Jean de Fontaney, one of the French Jesuits sent by Louis XIV, describes Peking as two joined cities, each four leagues round: the 'Tartar city', with the emperor's palace in the middle, and the 'Chinese city'. The streets were so crowded one could hardly walk. On the same page he describes the great bell of Peking set on a base of brick and stone.

Peking is made up of two cities: the first, in the middle of which is the Emperor's palace, is called the city of the Tartars; and the second, the city of the Chinese. They are joined to each other, and each is four leagues round.

Working translation

Original wording

Peking est composée de deux Villes: la premiere, au milieu de laquelle est le Palais de l'Empereur, s'appelle la ville des Tartares; & la seconde, la ville des Chinois. Elles sont jointes l'une à l'autre, & ont chacune quatre lieues de tour.

Jean de Fontaney, letter to Père de La Chaise (Zhoushan, 15 February 1703)

CitiesChengde (Jehol)1754

Jehol: the emperor's hunting palaces in 'Chinese Tartary'

Amiot explains why the painter-brother Attiret was summoned north: some ten thousand Dzungars had fled their ruler's usurper with families and baggage to submit to the emperor, who settled them in 'Chinese Tartary' and ordered their leaders to Gé-hol (Jehol, modern Chengde), where he hunted each year and had palaces nearly as fine as those around Peking. Attiret, it was guessed, was wanted to paint the portraits of the leading newcomers at the ceremony.

The Emperor ordered them to go to Gé-hol, a place in Chinese Tartary where he goes every year for the pleasure of hunting, and where he has palaces almost as beautiful as those around Peking.

Working translation

Original wording

L'Empereur leur ordonna de se rendre à Gé-hol, lieu de la Tartarie Chinoise où il va chaque année pour prendre le divertissement de la chasse, & où il a des palais presque aussi beaux que ceux qui sont aux environs de Peking.

Joseph-Marie Amiot, letter to Père de La Tour (Peking, 17 October 1754)

BuildingsAmur River17th century (written 1703)

Russian forts and towns built across 'Great Tartary'

Fontaney explains how the Muscovites had pushed from Moscow through Siberia along the Irtysh, Ob, Yenisei and Angara to the Selenga and the Amur ('Sangalien-oula' to the Tartars, the Black Dragon river to the Chinese), building forts and towns as they went. The nearest to China were Selenginsk, Nerchinsk and Albazin. In this corner of 'Tartary' the builders were Russians.

The Muscovites were not content with making these discoveries; they built forts and towns at intervals on all these rivers to secure possession of them. The nearest to China were Selinga, Nipchou and Yacsa.

Working translation

Original wording

Les Moscovites ne se contenterent pas de faire ces découvertes; ils bâtirent de distance en distance des forts & des villes sur toutes ces rivieres, pour s'en assurer la possession. Les plus proches de la Chine étoient Selinga, Nipchou & Yacsa.

Jean de Fontaney, letter to Père de La Chaise (1703)

BuildingsLiaodong and Manchuria1709-1711

A palisade of wooden stakes around Liaodong

On the imperial map survey of Tartary, Pierre Jartoux found the ginseng country walled off from the province of Liaodong by a barrier of wooden stakes (the Willow Palisade), patrolled by guards to keep Chinese from slipping out to dig the precious root. He adds that two or three thousand still sneaked through, risking their freedom.

The places where ginseng grows are entirely separated from the province of 'Canton', called Liaodong on our old maps, by a barrier of wooden stakes that encloses the whole province, around which guards patrol constantly to prevent the Chinese from leaving it and going to look for this root.

Working translation

Original wording

Les endroits où croît le gin-seng, sont tout-à-fait séparés de la province de Canton, appelée Leaotong dans nos anciennes cartes, par une barrière de pieux de bois qui renferme toute cette Province, et aux environs de laquelle des Gardes rôdent continuellement pour empêcher les Chinois d'en sortir, et d'aller chercher cette racine.

Pierre Jartoux, letter from Peking, 12 April 1711

About Manchus (Qing state); Chinese ginseng poachers

Keep in mind

'Canton' is an error in this reprint (the province meant is Guandong, i.e. Liaodong, not Canton in the south). The volume is a later Toulouse reprint.

Daily lifeBeijing1720s

Why a Manchu prince could not write 'prendre'

Parrenin reports a friendly debate with a Manchu prince who praised the Manchu alphabet over European letters. Parrenin challenged him to write the French words prendre, platine, griffon and friand; he could not, because Manchu cannot put two consonants together, so he had to write perendre, pelatine, feriand. Parrenin adds that the script lacks initial B and D, writing P and T instead.

'Try, for example, whether you can write these words: prendre, platine, griffon, friand.' He could not, because in the Tartar language two consonants cannot be joined in a row; a vowel must be placed between them, and one writes perendre, pelatine, feriand, gerifon, etc.

Working translation

Original wording

Essayez, par exemple, si vous pourrez écrire ces mots: prendre, platine, griffon, friand. Il ne le put, parce que dans la langue Tartare on ne peut joindre deux consonnes de suite; il faut placer entre deux une voyelle, & écrire perendre, pelatine, feriand, gerifon, &c.

Dominique Parrenin, letter to the Académie des Sciences

Daily lifeManchuria (near the Korean border)1709

Ten thousand Tartars sent to hunt ginseng

Jartoux writes that the emperor, wanting Tartars rather than Chinese to profit from ginseng, ordered ten thousand Tartars in 1709 to gather it, each owing him two ounces of the best, with the rest bought at its weight in fine silver. His survey party met these herb-hunters in the wilderness; on the same page he describes their troops of a hundred men advancing in a line through the forest. The letter opens with a Tartar villager near the Korean border bringing him four whole plants in a basket.

The Emperor, wishing the Tartars rather than the Chinese to profit from this trade, had ordered ten thousand Tartars that same year, 1709, to go and gather for themselves all the ginseng they could, on condition that each of them would give His Majesty two ounces of the best, and that the rest would be paid for at the weight of fine silver.

Working translation

Original wording

L'Empereur souhaitant que les Tartares profitassent de ce gain, préférablement aux Chinois, avait donné ordre, cette même année 1709, à dix mille Tartares, d'aller ramasser eux-mêmes tout ce qu'ils pourraient de gin-seng, à condition que chacun d'eux en donnerait à Sa Majesté deux onces du meilleur, et que le reste serait payé au poids d'argent fin.

Pierre Jartoux, letter from Peking, 12 April 1711

Daily lifeBeijing1727

The emperor: 'We Manchus have our own rites'

Parrenin translates the Yongzheng emperor's words to his ministers about a Manchu prince who had become Christian: every nation honours Heaven in its own way; the Manchu rite is the Tiaochin, when all burn incense and paper on New Year's day; Mongols, Chinese, Muscovites and Europeans each have their own. The prince, as a Manchu, should follow the Manchu rite. A footnote says the Tiaochin had no fixed form and each family did it its own way.

The rite of the Manchu Tartars is the Tiaochin: there is no one who does not burn incense and paper on the first day of the year, and that is to honour Heaven. We Manchus have our own particular rites; the Mongols, the Chinese, the Muscovites, the Europeans and so on also each have the Tien [Heaven] proper to them.

Working translation

Original wording

Le rite des Tartares Mantcheoux est le Tiaochin, il n'y a personne qui le premier jour de l'an ne brûle des odeurs & du papier, & cela pour honorer le Ciel. Nous autres Mantcheoux, nous avons nos rites particuliers; les Mongous, les Chinois, les Moscovites, les Européens, &c. ont aussi chacun le Tien qui lui est propre.

The Yongzheng emperor, as translated by Dominique Parrenin (letter of 26 September 1727)

About Manchus; the Yongzheng emperor and a Christian Manchu prince

Keep in mind

Parrenin's translation of an edict hostile to Christianity; 'Tiaochin' is his transcription of a Manchu household rite.

The nameBeijing1720s

Manchus are 'Eastern Tartars', Mongols 'Western Tartars'

Parrenin, sending the Académie des Sciences his Manchu translation of a French anatomy commissioned by the Kangxi emperor, stops to explain what 'Tartar language' means: the language of the Manchus, who have reigned in China for eighty years and are called Eastern Tartars, not that of the Mongols, called Western Tartars and almost all tributaries of the emperor. The two languages, he says, are very different.

I mean the language of the Manchus, who have reigned in China for eighty years and are called Eastern Tartars. I do not at all mean the language of the Mongols, who are called Western Tartars and are almost all tributaries of the Emperor. These two languages are very different from each other.

Working translation

Original wording

je parle de la langue des Mantcheoux qui régnent à la Chine depuis quatre-vingts ans, & qui s'appellent Tartares Orientaux. Je ne parle nullement de la langue des Mongols, qu'on nomme Tartares Occidentaux, & qui sont presque tous tributaires de l'Empereur. Ces deux langues sont très-différentes entr'elles.

Dominique Parrenin, letter to the Académie des Sciences (Peking, 1 May 1723)

The nameShenyang (Mukden)c. 1703

Mukden, capital of Eastern Tartary, where Chinese count as Tartars

Noël reports a plan to open a mission at Chin-yam (Shenyang, the Manchu Mukden), capital of Liaodong and all Eastern Tartary, where the emperor had set four supreme tribunals for the Tartars' affairs. He adds that Liaodong now counts as part of Tartary and its people are regarded 'not as Chinese, but as true Tartars'. The label followed political allegiance, not ancestry.

Chin-yam, capital of Liaodong and of all Eastern Tartary. This city is considerable, and the Emperor has set up four supreme tribunals there to judge in the last resort all the affairs of the Tartars; for Liaodong now passes for part of Tartary, and its inhabitants are no longer regarded as Chinese, but as true Tartars.

Working translation

Original wording

Chin-yam capitale de Leaoton, & de toute la Tartarie orientale. Cette ville est considérable, & l'Empereur y a établi quatre Tribunaux souverains pour y juger en dernier ressort toutes les affaires des Tartares; car le Leaoton passe aujourd'hui pour être de la Tartarie, & on n'en regarde plus les habitans comme Chinois, mais comme de véritables Tartares.

François Noël, Mémoire on the state of the China missions

The nameBeijing1720s-1730s

Eight banners each of Manchu, Mongol and 'Tartarized Chinese'

A footnote to Parrenin's letters on the persecuted Christian princes of the Sunu family explains the Qing banner system: eight banners of Manchu Tartars, eight of Mongol Tartars and eight of 'Tartarized Chinese', each with its own coloured flag, its own quarter in the Tartar city of Peking and its own courts, apart from those of the ordinary people. 'Tartar' here is a legal status as much as an ancestry.

There are eight banners of Manchu Tartars, eight more of Mongol Tartars, and eight of Tartarized Chinese. … Each banner has its own quarter in the Tartar city, and its own justice, independent of that of the common people.

Working translation

Original wording

Il y a huit bannières de Tartares Mantcheoux, huit autres de Tartares Mongous, & huit de Chinois tartarisés. … Chaque bannière a son quartier dans la ville Tartare, & sa justice particulière indépendante de celle du peuple.

Footnote in Parrenin's letter to Du Halde (Peking, 22 October, mid-1730s; probably added by the editors)

The fine print

How we know

mixed Part firsthand observation, part drawn from other sources.

mixed: firsthand letters from Jesuits who travelled in Manchuria and Mongolia (Jartoux on the 1709-1711 survey, Parrenin at Kangxi's court, Gerbillon) alongside hearsay and edifying narrative; heavily edited in Paris

Research notes
  • Letters were selected, cut and rewritten by the Paris editors; the 1780-83 edition regroups them by mission and drops some. Which volume holds a given letter differs between the original Recueils and later editions: cite by edition and volume.
  • Jartoux's ginseng letter (1711) and Gerbillon's journeys are the Tartary items most cited; Gerbillon's full journals are in Du Halde vol. 4 (recorded separately), not in the Lettres.
  • Further copies: archive.org/details/cihm_41297

Why it matters

The running Jesuit news-service on Qing Tartary for eighteenth-century France: letters describe the Manchu homeland, Mongol princes at court, the Kangxi survey of Tartary and the ginseng country. Much of what Du Halde, Grosier and Deguignes say about Tartary is footnoted to these letters.
