J. G. Kohl's Russia, from St Petersburg to the Crimean Tatar villages

A German travel writer's lively picture of the Russian Empire in the late 1830s, including Tatars in Moscow and St Petersburg and a horseback tour of Tatar Crimea, in an 1844 English abridgment.

Full title Russia … the Steppes, the Crimea, and the Interior of the Empire

Johann Georg Kohl

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

Johann Georg Kohl (1808-1878), a German geographer and travel writer, spent several years in the Baltic provinces and Russia in the 1830s and published four works in nine German volumes. This 1844 London volume (Chapman and Hall) is an English abridgment of all of them by an unnamed editor, strongest on St Petersburg. The Tartar material is scattered: Tatar coachmen, traders and a small mosque in Moscow; Tatar leatherwork and 'dressing-gown Tartars' in the capital; kurgans plundered in the south; and an eight-day ride through the Crimea with village stays, Tatar songs, and a visit to Bakhchysarai and the Khans' tombs. Most Crimean passages are eyewitness.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mostly the Crimean Tatars of the mountain villages and Bakhchysarai; also Muslim Tatars (largely from Kazan and the Volga) working as coachmen and traders in Moscow and St Petersburg; historically, the Mongol-Tatar armies that burned Moscow and the Crimean Khanate as 'the last fragment of the great Mongolian empire in Europe'.
Why read it
Kohl notices how Tatar buildings and towns worked: flat roofs you could ride over, windowless houses with fold-down wooden fronts, a Tatar-only Bakhchysarai, a mosque in Moscow paid for by a Tatar merchant, and tombs that overturned his idea of 'barbarian' Khans.
Watch out
This is an abridged translation, so wording is the English editor's as much as Kohl's. Kohl has a German nationalist streak and sometimes romanticises; the stories behind the Khans' tomb inscriptions were presumably explained to him by guides.

CitiesBakhchysarailate 1830s

A town where only Tatars may live

Kohl reports that the Russian government had made the old capital a Tatar reserve: apart from officials, only Tatars were allowed to live there. The government had also preserved the Khans' palace. He found the town crowded and noisy with music, and a complete contrast to the new Russian cities of Sevastopol and Simferopol.

Baktshiserai has been declared their exclusive possession, and, with the exception of official personages, no other than a Tartar is allowed to reside in it. The government has also had the good taste to bestow much pains on the preservation of the palace of the ancient Khans, once the dreaded enemies of Muscovy.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

CitiesMoscow13th to 16th centuries, written late 1830s

Moscow 'singed' by the Tartars twelve times

Kohl says no European city except Constantinople had burned so often as Moscow, and that the Tartars alone had set it alight more than twelve times. He adds that about two-thirds of its 12,000 houses were still wooden, the rest built of soft local limestone and brick.

Constantinople excepted, there is, perhaps, no city in Europe that has been so often burnt to the ground. By the Tartars alone it was well singed more than twelve times.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

BuildingsDerekoi, near Yalta, Crimealate 1830s

A Tatar village you could ride across by the rooftops

Guided by a young villager named Ismael, Kohl climbed to the Tatar village of Derekoi near Yalta. Its houses leaned into the hillside so their big flat roofs, made of packed earth and pebbles rolled smooth and resting on thin tree trunks, served as courtyards; there was no window glass, only wooden flaps. On the next page he watches village life going on across the rooftops.

The dwellings lie thickly scattered about among the trees, and leaning on one side against the mountain, so that one may ride over their flat roofs. These are large, as they supply the place of a courtyard covered with earth and small stones, laid smooth with a sort of roller, and supported on thin trunks of trees. Glass windows there are not, but in their place wooden flaps

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

BuildingsBakhchysarailate 1830s

Houses with fold-down wooden fronts

In Bakhchysarai's single long, narrow street the houses had no windows: the whole front was a set of wooden flaps that could be raised or lowered and used as counters. So every trade was on show, from a baker at his oven to a brewer of 'boussa' (boza, a fermented millet drink) and a tailor with his journeymen.

The houses have no windows, but the entire front wall consists only of wooden flaps, which can be let down or drawn up at pleasure, and which sometimes serve as tables or boards for goods. In one house you may see a baker at work … In the next you may overlook all the manipulations required in the preparation of the Turkish beverage entitled “boussa,”

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

BuildingsBakhchysarailate 1830s

The Khans' graves upset a traveller's ideas

Kohl had been taught to think of the Khans as destroyers who burned Moscow and terrorised Vilna and Warsaw. In their burial ground he found neat white marble monuments among vines, shrubs and flowers, with thoughtful inscriptions, and he concluded that 'the records of history' were imperfect.

In the burial-place of the Khans I found many memorials strikingly at variance with our notions of these barbarians; so many traces of humanity and piety, that I felt more strongly than ever, how imperfect are the records of history.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

BuildingsMoscowlate 1830s

Moscow's mosque, rebuilt by a Tatar merchant

Kohl visited Moscow's small mosque in the Tatar quarter. Its predecessor burned in the fire of 1812, and the poor congregation of about 120 Tatars could not rebuild until a wealthy Tatar paid for new walls. When Kohl saw it, it still had only a temporary roof and bare walls, and Russians had stolen one of its prized carpets.

The little building erected here to the honour of Allah … had a predecessor which in 1812 met with the same fate from the French fire, in which so many Russian churches shared. The flock were long unable to get together the necessary funds for a new temple, till about twelve years ago, when a wealthy Tartar erected the walls that are now standing.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

BuildingsSouthern Russia and Kerchlate 1830s

Kurgans built by others, dug up by everyone

Describing the Crimean gold in the Hermitage, Kohl credits the southern burial mounds to the Greeks of Taurida and older peoples of the Caucasus and Siberia. The Tartars appear not as builders but as one of many later peoples, with Goths, Huns and Cossacks, who dug into the kurgans and melted down the treasure.

the countless graves of the Greeks of Taurus, and of the original inhabitants of the Caucasus and Siberia, have been objects of zealous research … the Tartars and the Cossacks of the present day, have plundered them, and melted down the treasures found therein. The greater part of the kurgans and mohilos of South Russia, have been long since burrowed like rabbit-warrens

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

Daily lifeYaltalate 1830s

Shop songs about Khans and the fall of Kazan

Hearing singing from a grocer's shop in Yalta, Kohl went in and sat on an apple chest until late at night while two Tatars, an Armenian and a Circassian sang and explained their songs. Besides love songs, some were historical: about Khan Krym Giray, Khan Devlet Giray, the Russian conquest of Crimea and the taking of Kazan. Tatar history lived on in popular song.

Love formed, as usual, the principal theme, but some, to my surprise, were on historical subjects. One treated of Krim-Gerei, the last distinguished Khan of the Crimea, the friend of the well known French ambassador, Baron de Tott, another of Khan Devlet-Gerei, a third of the conquest of the Crimea by the Russians, a fourth of the taking of Kasan

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

Daily lifeBaidar Valley, Crimealate 1830s

'No Tartar has more than one wife'

Sheltering from rain with a Tatar named Ali Mustapha Oglu, Kohl asked a guest how many wives he had. The answer was firm: one, as for every decent Tatar. The same man then told Kohl about his village's lawsuit against a Russian landowner who had turned a grant of 700 acres into 20,000 by enclosing Tatar woods and meadows.

We inquired of him how many wives he had, and his answer was, “Of course only one! No Tartar has more than one wife, except such as are rascals;” so that it appears Mahomet's indulgent permission to have four is made but little use of.

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844), quoting a Tatar villager

Daily lifeAlupkalate 1830s

A stone for a man who lifted her veil

At Alupka, beside Count Vorontsov's new palace, Kohl saw the Tatar village with a handsome new mosque the count had built 'for his Tartars'. When one of Kohl's companions lifted a passing Tatar woman's veil, she threw a great stone at him, and Kohl says they all took the lesson.

On the top of one stands the handsome new mosque, built by Count W. for his Tartars. One of our party had the temerity to lift the corner of the veil of a Tartar woman who was passing, to get a peep at her face … for the lady took up a great stone and hurled it at him

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

Daily lifeSt Petersburglate 1830s

Leather masters and 'dressing-gown Tartars'

Among the street cries of St Petersburg Kohl heard 'Kazan boots' and 'Bukhara dressing-gowns'. He credits Mongols and Tatars with founding almost every branch of Russian leatherwork, from Moscow's embroidered caps and belts to Kazan's morocco boots, and says Tatar sellers of silk robes were nicknamed 'dressing-gown Tartars'.

The Mongolians and Tartars are distinguished for their skill in the preparation of leather, and almost every branch of manufacture connected with this article in Russia has had its origin from them … Dressing-gowns are almost the only things that do not come through Russian hands, but from those of the Tartar makers … where they are often called “dressing-gown Tartars.”

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

About Tatars (from Kazan and Central Asia) trading in the capital

Keep in mind

The claim that Russian leatherwork originated with the Tatars is Kohl's generalisation.

The nameBakhchysarailate 1830s

Bakhchysarai, 'seraglio of gardens'

Kohl explains the town's name from the Tatar word for garden and places the Crimean Khanate in history as the last surviving piece of the Mongol empire in Europe. It shows how a nineteenth century writer linked the Crimean Tatars back to the Mongols.

The word “Baktsha,” signifies in the Tartar language “garden” and the name of Baktshiserai would, therefore, signify “seraglio of gardens.” This city was for centuries the capital of the remarkable state which formed the last fragment of the great Mongolian empire in Europe

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)

The nameMoscow1812 remark, reported late 1830s

Moscow as a 'Tartar Rome'

Kohl opens his chapter on Moscow's churches with Madame de Staël's famous exclamation on first seeing the city: 'Voilà Rome Tatare'. It shows how Western Europeans used 'Tartar' as shorthand for Moscow's un-European look.

'Voilà Rome Tatare' means 'There is a Tartar Rome'.

Strange talesBakhchysarailate 1830s

A roofless tomb, a walled-up tomb, a vine for a sinner

Kohl retells the stories behind several Khans' tombs: one left roofless so the dead ruler could look up at heaven, one walled up because its occupant felt unworthy of God's sunlight, and one with a vine planted over the head so that a Khan who bore little fruit in life might do better in death. Another Khan asked to be buried under the mosque eaves so rain could wash away his sins.

without a roof, because “he considered the Heavens so beautiful and sublime, that even from his grave he would wish to look towards the firmament, the abode of God.” Another had had his tomb entirely walled up, because, as the inscription ran, “he did not feel himself worthy to be shone on by the least ray of God's sun.”

J. G. Kohl (English abridged translation, 1844)
