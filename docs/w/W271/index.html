Carpini and Rubruck in the original Latin

The Paris Geographical Society's 1839 volume printing the Latin reports of the friars John of Plano Carpini (1245-1247) and William of Rubruck (1253-1255), with Jordanus and learned notes.

Catalogue title Collection of voyages and memoirs published by the Geographical Society, vol. 4 (1839): the first critical Latin editions of Carpini’s Historia Mongalorum and Rubruck’s Itinerarium, with Jordanus’ Mirabilia and other medieval texts

Societe de Geographie; d'Avezac, Michel and Wright, editors

Volume 4 of the Recueil de voyages et de memoires (Paris, 1839) gives the first critical Latin texts of the two great Franciscan eyewitness reports on the Mongols: John of Plano Carpini's History of the Mongols (his mission to Guyuk's court, 1245-1247), edited by Armand d'Avezac, and William of Rubruck's Itinerary (to Mongke Khan at Karakorum, 1253-1255), edited by Francisque Michel and Thomas Wright from manuscripts in Paris, Leiden, Cambridge and London. It adds the Mirabilia of the missionary bishop Jordanus (c. 1330) and d'Avezac's long study of the missions. The corpus holds two scans of volume 4; volume 1 (1824, a Latin Marco Polo) is cited once.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
For Carpini and Rubruck 'Tartari' are the Mongols of the Chinggisid empire (Batu's Golden Horde and Mongke's court). Rubruck records that the Mongols themselves refused the name, saying the Tartars had been another people. Jordanus's 'Great Tartar' is the Mongol emperor in China.
Why read it
These are the two best 13th-century eyewitness accounts of the Mongols, in the friars' own Latin: houses on carts, kumiss, Karakorum's mud walls and quarters, a silver fountain-tree made by a Parisian goldsmith, and the friars' doubts about monsters and Prester John.
Watch out
Manuscript variants crowd the pages and the OCR mixes notes with text; d'Avezac's Carpini text was later superseded (Wyngaert, 1929). Carpini's monster stories are hearsay he flags himself, and some editorial notes quote hostile medieval propaganda.

CitiesCrimea (north of Sudak)June 1253

'No abiding city': entering another world

Three days after leaving the Crimean port of Soldaia (Sudak), Rubruck met the first Tartars and felt he was entering another world. His opening line about them turns a verse from the Letter to the Hebrews ('here we have no abiding city, but we seek one to come'): the Tartars have no abiding city anywhere, and know nothing of the city to come.

So on the third day after we left Soldaia we came upon the Tartars, and when I went in among them it truly seemed to me that I was entering another world. I will describe their life and customs to you as best I can. Nowhere do they have an abiding city, and of the city to come they know nothing.

CitiesKarakorum1254

Karakorum: smaller than the town of Saint-Denis

Rubruck's verdict on the Mongol capital, where he spent the spring of 1254: apart from the khan's palace it was not as good as the little town of Saint-Denis outside Paris, and the abbey of Saint-Denis was worth ten times more than that palace.

As for the city of Karakorum, you should know that, leaving aside the Khan's own palace, it is not as good as the town of Saint-Denis, and the monastery of Saint-Denis is worth ten times more than that palace.

CitiesDerbentlate 1254

Derbent: the Iron Gate, its towers cut down by the Tartars

Returning south along the Caspian, Rubruck passed Derbent, the 'Iron Gate'. The town ran more than a mile between mountain and sea, with a strong castle on top, very strong walls without ditches and towers of large polished stones; but the Tartars had cut down the tops of the towers and the battlements to the height of the walls. The land within, he says, used to be like a paradise.

It has very strong walls without ditches, and towers of large polished stones; but the Tartars destroyed the tops of the towers and the battlements of the walls, levelling the towers with the wall. Within that city the land used to be like a paradise.

CitiesMongolia1246

Carpini: no towns except Karakorum, which he never saw

Describing the Mongol homeland, Carpini says there is little wood, so everyone from the emperor down cooks on fires of cattle and horse dung, and hardly a hundredth of the land is fertile. With few streams and very rare rivers there are no villages and no towns, except one said to be fairly good, called Karakorum. The friars did not see it, though they were half a day's journey away at the Sira Ordo, the imperial camp.

But waters and streams there are few, and rivers very rare: so there are no villages there, nor any cities, except one which is said to be quite good, called Karakorum; we did not see it, but we were about half a day's journey from it when we were at the Sira Ordo

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Felt houses thirty feet wide, pulled by 22 oxen

Rubruck describes the Mongol felt houses built on a frame of rods and carried whole on carts. He measured one cart's wheel track at 20 feet, with the house overhanging by at least 5 feet each side, and counted 22 oxen pulling one house, in two rows of eleven, with an axle as big as a ship's mast and a driver standing in the doorway.

And they make these houses so large that they are sometimes thirty feet across. I once measured the width between the wheel tracks of one cart at 20 feet, and when the house was on the cart it overhung the wheels on each side by at least 5 feet. I counted 22 oxen drawing one house on one cart, eleven in a row across the width of the cart and another eleven in front of them.

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Cuman mounds, stone statues and brick towers

Among the burial customs he saw on the steppe, Rubruck notes that the Cumans raised a great mound over the dead and set up a statue facing east holding a cup at the navel. For the rich they built small pointed 'pyramids', and in places he saw tall towers of baked brick and even stone houses, though no stone was found there. He also saw a newly dead man with the hides of sixteen horses hung on tall poles around him.

The Cumans raise a great mound over the dead man and set up a statue to him, its face turned to the east, holding a cup in its hand in front of the navel. For the rich they also build pyramids, that is, small pointed houses, and in some places I saw great towers of baked tiles, in others stone houses, although stones are not found there. I saw one man newly dead for whom they had hung the hides of sixteen horses, four towards each quarter of the world, between tall poles

BuildingsKarakorum1254

Karakorum's quarters, temples and mud wall

Rubruck describes Karakorum as two quarters: one of Muslims (Saracens) with the markets, where merchants gathered because of the court, and one of 'Cathayans' (northern Chinese), who were all craftsmen. Outside them stood the large palaces of the court secretaries. There were twelve temples of various idolaters, two mosques and one church; a mud wall with four gates enclosed the town, each gate with its own market for grain, sheep and goats, oxen and carts, or horses.

[one quarter] of the Saracens, where the markets are, and many merchants gather there because of the court … the other is the quarter of the Cathayans, who are all craftsmen. Outside these quarters are large palaces belonging to the court secretaries. There are twelve idol temples of different nations, two mosques in which the law of Mahomet is proclaimed, and one Christian church at the far end of the town. The town is enclosed by a mud wall and has four gates.

BuildingsKarakorum1254

The silver tree made by a Paris goldsmith

At Karakorum Mongke had a large walled court beside the town wall with a great palace where he held drinking feasts twice a year. Because it was unseemly to carry skins of milk and drink into the hall, Master William of Paris, a goldsmith captured in Hungary and working for the khan, made at the entrance a great silver tree with four silver lions at its roots, from which mare's milk flowed through a pipe.

Mongke himself has at Karakorum a great court next to the town walls … There is a great palace in which he holds his drinking feast twice a year … At the entrance of this great palace, since it was unseemly to bring in skins of milk and other drinks, Master William of Paris made him a great silver tree, at whose roots are four silver lions with a pipe, all spouting white mare's milk.

BuildingsCaucasus (south of Derbent)late 1254

Ruined walls from mountain to mountain: 'Alexander's barriers'

A day beyond Derbent Rubruck crossed a valley where the foundations of walls ran from one mountain to the next. He identifies them with the barriers of Alexander meant to hold back 'wild peoples', and explains these rationally as the shepherds of the wilderness kept out of the farmland and cities. He adds that he could learn nothing certain about the other barriers where Jews were said to be enclosed.

The next day we crossed a valley in which the foundations of walls could be seen running from one mountain to another, and over the mountain tops there was no road. These used to be the barriers of Alexander, holding back the wild peoples, that is, the shepherds of the wilderness, so that they could not come into the cultivated lands and the cities.

Working translation

Original wording

In crastino transivimus per vallem quamdam, in qua apparebant fundamenta murorum de uno monte ad alium, et per summitates montium nulla erat via. Ista solebant esse claustra Alexandri, feras gentes chohibentia, hoc est, pastores de solitudine, ut non possent ingredi super terras cultas et civitates.

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppeJune 1253

First taste of kumiss, and a moving city of carts

Rubruck says that in two months between Soldaia and Sartaq's camp they never slept under a roof and saw no village, nor any trace of a building, except great numbers of Cuman tombs. One evening his guide gave him kumiss; he broke into a sweat from horror and novelty, but found it very tasty. Next morning the carts of the commander Scatatai, loaded with houses, looked to him like a great city coming towards him.

That evening the lad who was guiding us gave us kumiss to drink, and at the first draught I sweated all over from horror and novelty, for I had never drunk it before. Yet it seemed to me very tasty, as indeed it is. In the morning we met Scatatai's carts loaded with houses, and it seemed to me that a great city was coming to meet me.

Original wording

Illo sero dedit nobis garcio, qui ducebat nos, bibere cosmos, ad cujus haustum totus sudavi propter horrorem et novitatem, quia nunquam biberam de eo. Valde tamen sapidum videbatur michi, sicut vere est. Mane ergo obviavimus bigis Scatatay honustis domibus, et videbatur michi quod obviaret michi civitas magna.

Daily lifeBatu's camp on the Volga1246

Prince Michael refuses to bow to Chinggis Khan's image

Carpini tells how Michael of Chernigov, a great Russian prince, came to submit to Batu. He was first made to pass between two fires, then told to bow southwards to the image of Chinggis Khan. He answered that he would gladly bow to Batu and his servants, but not to the image of a dead man, since Christians may not; after repeated refusals he was kicked in the chest until he died.

So it recently happened that Michael, one of the great dukes of Russia, when he went to submit to Batu, was first made to pass between two fires; after this they told him to bow to the south to Chinggis Khan. He answered that he would willingly bow to Batu and even to his servants, but he would not bow to the image of a dead man, because Christians may not do so

Daily lifeMongolialate 13th century

Natigay, the felt household god with a wife and children

In the Latin Marco Polo of the Society's first volume, the chapter on Tartar customs describes their god Natigay (here 'Naagay'), lord of the earth who guards children, herds and crops. Every household made him of felt and cloth, with a felt wife and children beside him. At meals they smeared fat on the idols' mouths and threw broth out of the door for them before eating. The same page describes kumiss, called 'chenus'.

Their law is this: they have their god called Naagay, and they say he is an earthly god, or god of the earth, who guards their children, their beasts and their crops … They make this god of felt and cloth, and believe this god of theirs has a wife and children … When they eat, they take fat meat and smear the mouth of their idol with it, and of the idol's wife and children

The namePontic-Caspian steppe (Sartaq's camp)1253

'Do not call our lord a Christian, nor us Tartars'

As Rubruck left Sartaq's camp, the secretary Coiac and other court scribes warned him not to say their lord was a Christian: he was a Moal. Rubruck explains that the Mongols treat 'Christian' as the name of a nation and are so proud that they want their own name, Moal, above every other, and will not be called Tartars either, because the Tartars had been a different people.

'Do not say that our lord is a Christian. He is not a Christian but a Moal'; for the name of Christianity seems to them the name of some nation. They are raised to such pride that, though they may perhaps believe something about Christ, they will not be called Christians, wishing to exalt their own name, that is Moal, above every name; nor will they be called Tartars. For the Tartars were another people, about whom I learned as follows.

Original wording

« Nolite dicere quod dominus noster sit christianus. Non est christianus, sed Moal »; quia enim nomen christianitatis videtur eis nomen cujusdam gentis. In tantam superbiam sunt erecti, quod quamvis forte aliquid credant de Christo, tamen nolunt dici christiani, volentes nomen suum, hoc est Moal, exaltare super omne nomen, nec volunt vocari Tartari. The nameMongolia1839 (on Carpini, 1247)

The 'water Mongols' who called themselves Tartars

In his commentary d'Avezac summarises Carpini's account of the four peoples who once lived in Mongolia: the Yeka Mongal ('great Mongols'), the Su-Mongal ('water Mongols') who called themselves Tartars after a river named Tatar in their land, the Merkit and the Mecrit. He confirms that Yeka and Su do mean 'great' and 'water'.

In that country there were formerly four peoples, namely: the Yeka Mongal or great Mongols; the Su-Mongal or water Mongols, who gave themselves the name Tartars after a river called Tatar that flowed through their country; the Merkit; and lastly the Mecrit.

Original wording

En ce pays étaient autrefois quatre peuples, savoir: les Yeka Mongal ou grands Mongals; les Su-Mongals ou Mongals aquatiques, lesquels se donnaient à eux-mêmes le nom Tartares, d'après un fleuve appelé Tatar qui coulait en leur pays; les Merkit; et enfin les Mécrit.

Strange talesCentral Asia (Naiman country)1253-1254 (story set c. 1100)

Prester John: 'ten times more than the truth'

Rubruck traces the legend of Prester John to a Nestorian shepherd-lord of the Naimans who made himself king after the death of the Kara-Khitai ruler. The Nestorians called him King John and said ten times more about him than was true, as they make great rumours out of nothing. Rubruck crossed his pastures and found that nobody knew anything of him except a few Nestorians.

When Con Khan died, that Nestorian raised himself up as king, and the Nestorians called him King John and said ten times more about him than the truth. For that is what Nestorians coming from those parts do: they make great rumours out of nothing … So the great fame of this King John went out; and when I crossed his pastures, nobody knew anything about him except a few Nestorians.

Mortuo Con cham, elevavit se ille nestorinus in regem et vocabant eum nestorini regem Johannem, et plus dicebant de ipso in decuplo quam veritas esset. Strange talesSiberia (north of Mongolia)1254

Bone skates, but no monsters

From informants at Mongke's court Rubruck learned of northern peoples: the Orengai, who tied polished bones under their feet and sped over snow and ice fast enough to catch birds and beasts. He also asked about the monstrous races described by Isidore and Solinus; his informants said they had never seen any such thing, which, he writes, 'we much wonder whether it is true'.

There are also the Orengai, who tie polished bones under their feet and push themselves over the frozen snow and over the ice so fast that they catch birds and beasts. … I asked about the monsters, or monstrous men, that Isidore and Solinus tell of. They told me they had never seen such things, at which we much wonder whether it is true.

Ox-footed men with dogs' faces

Telling of Mongol campaigns in the far north, Carpini says the army passed the Samoyeds, who lived by hunting in tents of hide, and came to a land by the Ocean where they found monsters, 'as we were firmly told', with human bodies but ox feet and dogs' faces, who spoke two words like men and barked at the third.

Going on further they came to a land on the Ocean, where they found certain monsters, as we were firmly told, which had human form in every way except that their feet ended in the feet of oxen, and they had human heads but faces like dogs; they spoke two words in the human way and at the third barked like a dog

Strange talesChina (Yuan empire)c. 1330

Jordanus on the 'Great Tartar' of China

The Dominican bishop Jordanus, who never went to China, repeats what 'trustworthy people' told him of the Great Tartar: very rich, just and generous, with paper money and monasteries like Europe's. Some subjects keep their dead unburied at home for up to ten years, serving them food as if alive, and great lords are buried with a horse and one or two favourite servants alive.

Of the Great Tartar I tell what I heard from trustworthy people … There they do not burn their dead; nor do they bury them, sometimes for ten years … but they keep them in their houses and serve them food as if they were alive. Great lords, when they die, are buried with one horse and with one or two of their favourite servants alive.

Strange talesEurope (Hungary and Poland)13th century poem, quoted 1839

A 13th-century poem calls the Tartars cannibals

In his notes d'Avezac prints lines, copied by Thomas Wright from a Cotton manuscript, of John of Garland's poem On the Triumphs of the Church. It paints the Tartars as Satan's household and the Church's plague who eat human flesh: no lion eats a lion and no wolf tears a wolf, but these men roast human limbs on spits. D'Avezac traces the poet's 'reliable' information to atrocity letters from priests in central Europe.

A people exceedingly savage, of Satan's household, a plague to the Church, at odds with the faith, in love with slaughter. … Hungry, man gnaws man; no lion fills its slack belly with lion's flesh; no wolf tears a wolf with its claw; this people turns limbs spitted on hazel skewers over the fire.

Original wording

Gens est sæva nimis, Sathanæque domestica, pestis Ecclesiæ, fidei dissona, cædis amans. … Esuriens hominem corrodit homo; leo nullus Carne leonina viscera laxa replet; Non lupus ungue lupum lacerat; gens ista colurnis In veribus vertit membra veruta foco.

  • Other volumes of the Recueil (1824-1866) include Idrisi (Jaubert translation, vols 5-6, 1836-1840) and Marco Polo (vol. 1, 1824, Roux edition): both relevant to Tartary geography and located on IA (recueildevoyages51soci, recueildevoyages62soci, india.history.resource.111340).

Why it matters

The scholarly Latin base for Carpini and Rubruck before Beazley and Wyngaert; d'Avezac's introduction is the first full study of the Mongol mission texts and their manuscripts, so this volume is the transmission check for every later translation of Carpini and Rubruck (W043 in English, Bergeron in French).
