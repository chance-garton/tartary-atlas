Gaspar da Cruz's treatise on China

The first European book devoted to China, by a Portuguese friar who was in Canton in 1556, with a chapter on China's Tartar neighbours and the Wall.

Catalogue title Treatise in which the things of China are related at length, with its particularities, and likewise of the kingdom of Hormuz (first European book devoted to China)

Gaspar da Cruz, Dominican

Gaspar da Cruz (died 1570) was a Portuguese Dominican who worked in India, Malacca and Cambodia and spent some weeks in Canton (Guangzhou) late in 1556. Back in Portugal he printed this treatise at Évora in 1569, shortly before he died of plague. It mixes what he saw in Canton with the written account of Galeote Pereira and reports from Portuguese prisoners held in China around 1549 to 1553. Chapters 2 and 3 discuss the lands bordering China: the Tartars beyond the Wall, the Mughals ('Mogores') of Samarkand, and even Germany and Russia. This corpus copy is a Google scan of the original 1569 blackletter print.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mostly the Mongols beyond China's northern Wall, with whom the Ming were at war (he reports the great raid of 1550), plus Tartar prisoners serving as soldiers in Canton. He also ties Tartary to the Mughals ('Mogores') of Samarkand and to the Scythians and Magog of classical and biblical learning.
Why read it
A rare mid-sixteenth-century view of the Tartars from the Chinese side of the Wall, from a man who actually saw Tartar captives in Canton. It shows how a learned friar stitched Chinese hearsay, prisoners' stories and Bible-based geography into one picture.
Watch out
Most Tartar material is second hand (Chinese informants and Portuguese captives), and his geography of Tartary, Russia and 'Germany' is speculation. The corpus OCR of this blackletter print is nearly unreadable, so all quotes here were read from the page images.

CitiesNorthern China1550

The Tartar raid of 1550 and a city retaken

Portuguese prisoners in China were told that after some years of truce, the Tartars made a great invasion in 1550 and took a very important city. The Chinese besieged it but could not storm it; on the advice of a low-born man they found a way to kill the Tartars and their horses and recovered the city. Cruz does not name the city or the method. The date matches the great Mongol raid on the Beijing region in 1550.

It was affirmed to the Portuguese who were captives … that for some years there had been truces between the Chinese and the Tartars; and in the year fifty the Tartars made a great incursion into China, in which they took from them a very important city … on the advice of a man of low rank they found a means by which they killed them and their horses, and became masters of the city once more.

CitiesNanjingc. 1569 (describing the early 1400s)

The court moved to Beijing to face the Tartars

Describing the province of Nanjing, Cruz says the king once lived there because the land is fertile and pleasant, but moved his court to Beijing ('Paquim') to be nearer the wars against the Tartars. It is an early European statement of why Beijing became the capital.

This province has sixteen cities. The king anciently resided in this city, because it is a very fertile, fresh and pleasant land, and moved the court to Paquim [Beijing] in order to attend more closely to the wars of the Tartars.

BuildingsGreat Wall of China1556 to 1569

A hundred leagues of wall, with mountains in the gaps

Cruz reports that the Chinese have a wall of a hundred leagues (some say more) between themselves and the Tartars, always garrisoned against Tartar raids. He reasons that it cannot be continuous but must use mountains to fill the gaps, because a Persian lord told him Persia had similar walls broken by hills. The Wall is credited plainly to the Chinese, built against the Tartars.

as I have said, the Chinese have a hundred leagues of wall (others say it is more) between themselves and them, where there are always garrisons to defend against the incursions of the Tartars. It may be believed that this wall is not continuous, but that some mountains or ranges come in between, because a lord of Persia assured me that there were similar works in some parts of Persia, with hills or ranges coming in between.

Daily lifeNorthern frontier of China1550s (as reported to Cruz)

Raw meat, blood and a smell carried on the wind

Cruz describes the Tartars as reddish, naked from the waist up, eating raw meat and smearing their bodies with its blood, so that they stink. An old Chinese man told him that when Tartar raiders approached with the wind behind them, the Chinese could smell them coming. In war, he adds, they carry raw meat under themselves to eat. This is a Chinese stereotype passed to a Portuguese friar, not something Cruz saw.

[they go naked] from the waist up, eat raw meat, and anoint their bodies with its blood, for which reason they commonly stink and have a bad smell. An old Chinese man assured me that sometimes, when they came against the lands of China, if the wind blew from where they were coming, they were detected by the smell; and when they go to war they carry the raw meat underneath them to eat.

The nameSamarkandc. 1569

Samarkand, 'the head of Tartary' on the maps

Describing the Mughal rulers of Sind and Delhi, Cruz says the head of their kingdom is 'the great Samarkand', which the maps of his day label 'the head of Tartary'. He then uses Josephus to make these people Scythians descended from Japheth, son of Noah, through Magog. This is book learning, not observation: it shows 'Tartary' already working as a map label in the 1560s.

The head of their kingdom is called the great Samarkand, which on the maps is called the head of Tartary. These are counted among the Scythians, as Josephus testifies in the first book of the Antiquities, who according to him descend from Japheth son of Noah through Magog.

The nameGuangzhou1556

Tartar prisoners in Canton, called 'Tatos'

Here Cruz speaks as an eyewitness: in Canton he saw many Tartar captives whose only 'captivity' was serving as soldiers far from home. They wore red caps as a badge but otherwise dressed like the Chinese, and received a fixed royal pay. The Chinese, he says, call them 'Tatos' because they cannot pronounce the letter R. Chinese Dada (Tatar) is real; the missing-R explanation is his.

In the city of Canton I saw many Tartar captives, whose only captivity is to serve as men-at-arms in other parts far from Tartary. They wear red caps as a badge; otherwise they wear the dress of the Chinese among whom they live … The Chinese call these people Tatos, because they cannot pronounce this letter r.

The nameCentral Asiac. 1569

Mogores, named after Magog

Cruz says these peoples trade as far as Hormuz, border the Caspian and have the Tartars at their side. The Greeks called them Scythians, but across Asia they are called Mogores, a name he believes they kept from their ancestor Magog. It is a learned folk etymology linking the Mughals (from 'Mongol') to the biblical Magog.

Many of them come with goods to Hormuz; they border on the Caspian Sea, and at their side are the Tartars. And although the Greeks call these people Scythians, throughout Asia the greater and the lesser they are called Mogores, keeping the first name they took from their father Magog, from whom they descend and take their name.

Strange talesGreat Wall of China1550s (as reported)

Bearded 'Alimenes' guarding the Tartar passes

Cruz reports that the king of China keeps salaried soldiers to guard the weak passes and walls facing the Tartars: big, bearded men in cut breeches and caps, with curved swords. A Portuguese captive heard the Chinese call them 'Alimenes', which Cruz takes to mean Germans (Alemanes), concluding that China must border Upper Germany. He tells the same story on an earlier page (n25). The identification is his own leap.

the Chinese say that the King of China has many salaried men-at-arms who guard for him the weak passes and the walls on the side of the Tartars; they say they are big, heavily bearded men who wear cut breeches, caps and curved swords; and a Portuguese who had been taken captive inland told me that he had heard the Chinese call these people Alimenes.

Strange talesCaspian Seac. 1569

Scythians who will march for the Antichrist

Right after the Magog passage, Cruz cites a treatise on the Antichrist by one Pedraza, who says these Scythians live beyond the Caspian, are a huge multitude, and will come to help the Antichrist at the end of time. Cruz offers it as support for his own view. It is the old Gog and Magog end-of-the-world legend, repeated from a book.

Of these Pedraza says, in the treatise he made on the Antichrist, that they are Scythians and live beyond the Caspian Sea, and that they are a great multitude, which is to come to the aid of the Antichrist; I mention this to show that it agrees with what I have said.

Strange talesGuangzhou1556

Painted giants who guard the passes of Tartary

Describing officials' houses in Canton, Cruz notes huge gates painted with two giants holding staves. He saw four such figures in a temple, said to be portraits from life of men the king keeps to guard the weakest passes of Tartary, big-limbed and twelve or thirteen palms tall. What he saw were painted door gods; the frontier story is what he was told.

on the front they have some very large doors, like the gates of a city, with two giants painted on them holding staves in their hands. I saw four in a pagoda (which is a temple of idols), portrayed from life of some whom they say the King keeps to guard for him the passes [that are weakest in Tartary; they are very big-limbed, perhaps twelve or thirteen palms tall].

How we know

firsthand The author saw the places or people described.

Card wording

firsthand observation (Cruz spent some months in Canton in 1556) combined with the earlier Portuguese captive Galeote Pereira's account and Chinese informants; includes a chapter on the Tartars and the Wall and on the Mongol (Tartar) rule over China

Cautions
  • Incorporates Galeote Pereira's manuscript; printed in Évora during a plague year and little circulated; English translation Boxer 1953 (South China in the Sixteenth Century, Hakluyt, lending only).
  • Galeote Pereira's own text ('Algumas cousas sabidas da China') was not located in an open Portuguese or Italian (Nuovi avisi 1565) copy this pass.

Why it matters

Earliest printed Portuguese eyewitness account of China, with a specific chapter on China's Tartar neighbours and the Great Wall; the source silently used by Mendoza 1585 and hence by all of Europe.
