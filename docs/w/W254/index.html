Gaubil's history of Genghis Khan from the Chinese annals

A Jesuit in Peking retells the rise and rule of the Mongols from Chinese histories, Paris 1739.

Catalogue title History of Genghis Khan and of the whole dynasty of the Mongols his successors, conquerors of China, drawn from Chinese history

Antoine Gaubil

Antoine Gaubil, a French Jesuit who lived in Peking from 1723 to 1759, translated and condensed the Chinese annals of the Mongol (Yuan) dynasty into a French history of 'Gentchiscan' and his successors, with his own footnotes on geography and names. He never saw Mongolia; his knowledge comes from Chinese books and informants in Peking. The book follows Chinggis Khan's wars, the fall of the Jin and Song, the building of Mongol capitals and the reign of Khubilai. This corpus copy is the 1739 Paris edition (Briasson and Piget), Wellcome Library scan.

Who “Tartar” means here
Gaubil uses 'Tartarie' for the whole steppe north of China and 'Tartares' for its peoples in general, but he separates the 'Horde des Tatars' (one tribe) from the 'Mongous', and calls the Jin (Jurchen) emperors 'Eastern Tartars'.
Why read it
The Chinese side of the Mongol story: who walled Karakorum, who designed Khubilai's summer capital, and how Chinese historians judged Mongol rule.
Watch out
It is a condensed translation of official Chinese histories, which have their own dynastic bias; Chinese transcriptions of names are hard to match with Persian and European forms, and the OCR mangles some names.

Ögedei puts walls round the camp at Karakorum, 1235

The Chinese annals record that in spring 1235 Ögedei ordered walls built around the encampment of Holin (Karakorum) and a palace called Wan'an five li round. Gaubil elsewhere in the book says Holin is the Caracoram of other writers. It is a precise date for when the Mongol camp-capital became a walled town.

In the spring of the year 1235 the Emperor Ögedei ordered the camp of Holin to be enclosed with walls, and the palace called Ouangan to be made five li in circumference.

BuildingsShangdu (Xanadu)1256

A Chinese monk lays out the Mongols' new capital (Shangdu)

Finding Holin (Karakorum) inconvenient, Möngke had a new capital chosen in Tartary. The task went to a learned Chinese Buddhist monk, Lieoupingtchong (Liu Bingzhong), who picked a site east of Huanzhou; a city, imperial palace, temples, lords' palaces and courts were built inside high thick walls, and it soon filled with Chinese and Tartars. Gaubil notes it was later called Changtou (Shangdu, the Xanadu of legend).

He ordered a place in Tartary to be chosen that would henceforth be the capital of his states. This task was given to a Chinese monk named Lieoupingtchong. … A great city was built there, a palace for the Emperor, temples, palaces for the lords, and courts of justice. It was enclosed with high, thick walls.

The Tatar horde revolts, and the Jin are 'Eastern Tartars'

In the story of young Temüjin, Gaubil writes that 'the horde of the Tatars' rebelled against the Jin emperor, keeping the Tatars as one tribe distinct from the Mongols. His footnote explains that the Jin were 'Eastern Tartars' who ruled the steppe north and north-east of Liaodong and much of northern China, and to whom the petty princes of both Tartaries paid tribute.

The horde of the Tatars revolted against the Emperor of the Kin. … The Emperor of the Kin spoken of here is the Emperor of the Eastern Tartars, who were then masters of the part of Tartary lying north and north-east of Liaodong

Strange talesShaoxing, Zhejiangc. 1280s

A Tibetan lama loots the Song tombs and builds a pyramid of bones

Under Khubilai a Tibetan lama in favour with the Mongols dug up the tombs of the Song emperors near Shaoxing, reportedly finding much gold and jewels, and piled the bones with those of oxen and horses into a pyramid. The outraged local officials jailed him, but Mongol lords and court lamas got Khubilai to free him and return most of his treasure, which, Gaubil says, the Chinese never forgave.

His passion for gold and silver drove him to dig up the dead bodies of the Emperors and great men whose tombs were near Shaoxing. He found there, it is said, much gold, silver and jewels. From these bones, mixed with the bones of oxen and horses, he made a pyramid.

Why it matters

First European history of the Mongols built on Chinese rather than Persian sources; Gaubil consistently calls the steppe "la Tartarie" while distinguishing Mongous from other Tartars, so the book documents how a Sinologist in Peking mapped Chinese ethnonyms onto the European term.
