The Novgorod First Chronicle: the Tatars arrive

Year-by-year records kept in Novgorod, with the closest Russian testimony to the Mongol arrival in 1223 and 1237 to 1238.

Catalogue title Novgorod First Chronicle, Older and Younger Redactions (1950 Academy edition)

Новгородская первая летопись старшего и младшего изводов

Anonymous Novgorod chroniclers; ed. A. N. Nasonov

Monks and clergy in Novgorod kept these annals year by year. The oldest copy, the Synodal manuscript, dates from the 13th and 14th centuries, and the younger version runs to 1447. They record the first appearance of the 'Tatars' at the Kalka in 1223, Batu's campaign of 1237 to 1238, which stopped about 100 versts short of Novgorod, the Mongol census of 1257 to 1259 and later dealings with the Horde. The corpus copy is the 1841 Archaeographic Commission volume (PSRL vol. 3), which prints the Synodal text with variants from the younger copies and adds the Second and Third Novgorod chronicles.

How they knew
Official record
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongol armies and later the Golden Horde, its khans, envoys and tax officials. The chroniclers also call them by biblical names (Ishmaelites, sons of Hagar) and once, at Kulikovo, by the older steppe name Polovtsy. The 16th-century entries use 'Tatars' for Kazan Tatars and Crimean raiders.
Why read it
Here the Tatars appear as the Rus saw them: at first unknown strangers, then besiegers with palisades and siege engines, and then census takers riding through Novgorod's streets. The concrete details of how cities fell are close to the events.
Watch out
The annalists were churchmen who read the Tatars as God's punishment for Rus sins and used hostile religious language. Some later entries were written up long after the events, and the 1841 edition mixes several copies. The OCR is damaged, so quotes have been normalized against a second scan of the same printing.

CitiesVladimirFebruary 1238

The fall of Vladimir: palisade, siege engines, a burning church

The chronicle describes the Tatar method at Vladimir. They surrounded the city with a palisade (tyn), set up siege engines (poroki), took the city and set it on fire. The prince's family and the bishop, who had taken monastic vows as the end approached, shut themselves in the gallery of the cathedral. The Tatars broke down the doors, piled up timber and burned the church, suffocating everyone inside.

And the lawless Ishmaelites drew near the city, and surrounded the city in force, and fenced it all round with a palisade … having set up siege engines they took the city and set it on fire … they ran into the church of the Holy Mother of God and shut themselves in the gallery; and the pagans, breaking down the doors, set fire to the church, having piled up timber, and suffocated them all.

CitiesTorzhokMarch 1238

Torzhok's two-week siege and the road to Novgorod

After Moscow, Pereyaslavl, Yuryev, Dmitrov, Volok and Tver, the Tatars besieged Torzhok. As at other towns, they fenced it with a palisade and battered it with siege engines for two weeks while Novgorod sent no help. They took it on 5 March and chased the fugitives along the Seliger road 'cutting down people like grass' as far as Ignach Krest, 100 versts from Novgorod. There they turned back, which the chronicler credits to God and St Sophia.

the lawless ones came and surrounded Torzhok, on the Sunday of Orthodoxy, and fenced it all round with a palisade, as they took other cities, and the accursed ones battered it there with siege engines for two weeks … Then the accursed godless ones pursued from Torzhok along the Seliger road as far as Ignach Krest, cutting down all the people like grass, to within 100 versts of Novgorod

Daily lifeDnieper River1223

The Tatar envoys: 'we have not taken your towns'

Before the Kalka battle the Tatars sent envoys to the Rus princes, who had allied with the Polovtsy (Cumans). They said they had not touched Rus land, towns or villages and had come against 'our slaves and grooms, the pagan Polovtsy'. The princes killed the envoys. The chronicle records the Mongol claim that the steppe nomads were their runaway subjects.

we hear that you are coming against us, having listened to the Polovtsy; but we have not taken your land, nor your towns, nor your villages, nor have we come against you; but we have come, sent by God, against our slaves and grooms, the pagan Polovtsy.

Daily lifeRyazanwinter 1237 (chronicle year 6746)

A sorceress envoy demands a tenth of everything

The Tatars came to Ryazan land 'without number, like locusts'. From their camp they sent envoys to the Ryazan princes, a woman sorceress and two men, demanding a tenth of everything: of people, of princes and of horses. The editor's footnote on this page adds that another copy lists the horses by colour, ten of each. The princes answered that when none of them were left, all would be theirs.

foreigners called Tatars came upon the Ryazan land, a multitude without number, like locusts … and from there they sent their envoys, a woman sorceress and two men with her, to the Ryazan princes, asking of them a tenth of everything: of people, and of princes, and of horses, a tenth of everything.

Daily lifeNovgorod1259

The census: Tatars ride the streets writing down houses

Novgorod split over the Mongol census. The Tatars threatened to leave ('give us the number, or we go') while the common people swore to die for St Sophia rather than be counted. In the end the boyars gave way, and the census takers 'began to ride through the streets writing down the Christian houses'. The chronicler complains that the boyars made it easy for themselves and hard for the poor.

and the Tatars said: give us the number, or we will run away; and the common people would not give the number, but said: let us die honourably for St Sophia and for the houses of the angels … and the accursed ones began to ride through the streets, writing down the Christian houses

Daily lifeNovgorodJanuary 1557

Tatar prisoners in Novgorod: baptism or the river

The Second Novgorod Chronicle, a terse 16th-century town record, notes that in 1552 sixty Kazan Tatars were brought from Moscow and three new prisons were built for them. On this page, under the winter of 7065 (1556 to 1557), it records that clerks handed the Tatars held in the prisons who wished to be baptised over to the monasteries, while 'those who did not wish to be baptised, they threw into the water'.

the clerks gave out to the monasteries the Tatars who sat in the prisons and wished to be baptised; those who did not wish to be baptised, they threw into the water.

Daily lifeTver1327

Tver kills the Horde's envoy, and a Tatar army lays the land waste

In 1327 Prince Alexander of Tver killed many Tatars in Tver and other towns, after the mighty envoy Shevkal had come from the Horde with a large Tatar retinue. That winter a huge Tatar army took Tver, Kashin and the Torzhok district and 'laid the whole Russian land waste'. Novgorod escaped by paying the Tatar envoys 2000 in silver and sending gifts to the commanders.

Prince Alexander Mikhailovich killed many Tatars in Tver and in other towns … for a mighty envoy had come from the Horde, named Shevkal, with a multitude of Tatars. … That same winter a Tatar army came, a very great multitude, and took Tver, and Kashin, and the Torzhok district, and, to put it simply, laid the whole Russian land waste

The namePontic steppe1223 (chronicle year 6732)

'Unknown peoples': nobody knew who the Tatars were

The chronicler's first notice of the Tatars is an admission of ignorance. Nobody knew who they were, where they came from, what language they spoke or what faith they held. People called them Tatars, Taurmens or Pechenegs, and learned men linked them to the prophecy of Pseudo-Methodius about peoples locked away in the desert who would come out at the end of time.

there came unknown peoples, whom no one clearly knows: who they are and whence they came, and what their language is, and of what tribe they are, and what their faith is; and they call them Tatars, and others say Taurmens, and others Pechenegs, and others say that these are the ones of whom Methodius, bishop of Patara, bears witness: that they came out of the Etrian desert

Working translation

Original wording

придоша языци незнаеми, ихъ же добрѣ никто же не вѣсть, кто суть и отколѣ изъидоша, и что языкъ ихъ, и котораго племене суть, и что вѣра ихъ; а зовуть я Татары, а иніи глаголють Таурмены, а друзіи Печенѣзи, иніи же глаголють, яко се суть, о нихъ же Меѳодій Паторомьскыи епископъ съвѣдѣтельствуеть: яко си суть изъшли изъ пустыня Етріевьскыя

The nameNovgorod1257 to 1259

The 'raw-eaters' come with their wives

In 1257 'evil news' reached Novgorod that the Tatars wanted the tamga (a trade tax) and a tithe. In the winter of 1259 the Tatar officials Berkai and Kasachik arrived with their wives and many others. The chronicler calls them 'accursed Tatar raw-eaters' (syroyadtsy), a standard insult for steppe nomads.

Evil news came from Rus that the Tatars wanted the tamga and the tithe from Novgorod, and the people were in turmoil all through the summer. … That same winter the accursed Tatar raw-eaters arrived, Berkai and Kasachik, with their wives, and many others

The nameKulikovo Field (Tula region)1380

Kulikovo: 'Ishmaelites' ruled by Mamai behind a puppet khan

The younger Novgorod text on the battle of Kulikovo explains the politics of the Horde: a weak khan reigned while Mamai did everything. It calls Mamai's army the pagan 'Ishmaelite race' and 'sons of Hagar', and when Dmitry arrays his troops it is 'against the pagan Polovtsy'. Old names for steppe enemies were simply reused for the Tatars.

the pagan Ishmaelite race rises against the Christians: for a certain feeble man reigns among them, and Prince Mamai does everything among them … and Grand Prince Dmitry, with his brother Vladimir, arrayed his regiments against the pagan Polovtsy

Working translation

Original wording

въздвизается на крестьяны Измаилтескии родъ поганыи: нѣкоему убо у нихъ худу царствующу, а все дѣющу у нихъ князю Мамаю … великии же князь Дмитрии, съ братомъ своимъ съ Володимеромъ, изрядивъ полкы противу поганыхъ Половець

Strange talesDnieper River1223

A Tatar commander buried alive in a Polovtsian kurgan

When Prince Mstislav crossed the Dnieper and beat the Tatar vanguard, the survivors fled with their commander Gemyabeg into a Polovtsian kurgan (burial mound). Unable to hold out, they buried him alive in the earth to save his life. He was found, and the Polovtsy begged him from Mstislav and killed him. The steppe was already dotted with mounds that the chronicler credits to the Polovtsy.

and the rest of them fled with their commander Gemyabeg into a Polovtsian kurgan, and there they could not hold out, and they buried their commander Gemyabeg alive in the earth, wishing to save his life; and there they found him, and the Polovtsy, having begged him from Mstislav, killed him.

Working translation

Original wording

а прокъ ихъ въбѣже съ воеводою своимъ Гемябегомъ въ курганъ Половьчьскыи, и ту имъ не бы мочи, и погребоша воеводу своего Гемябега жива въ земли, хотяще животъ его ублюсти, и ту й налѣзоша, испросивъше Половьци у Мьстислава, и убиша й.

Strange talesKalka River (near Donetsk)31 May 1223

A victory feast on top of the captured princes

After the Kalka, the Tatars crushed the captured Rus princes under boards and sat on top to feast, 'and so ended their lives'. Then they turned back from the Dnieper and vanished, and the chronicler confesses he does not know where they came from or where they went: 'God knows whence He brought them upon us, for our sins.'

and the princes they seized and crushed, putting them under boards, and they themselves sat on top to dine, and so they ended their lives. … And the Tatars turned back from the Dnieper river, and we do not know whence they came nor where they have gone again; God knows whence He brought them upon us, for our sins.

Working translation

Original wording

а князи имъше издавиша, подъкладъше подъ дъскы, а сами верху сѣдоша обѣдати, и тако животъ ихъ концяша. … Татари же възвратишася отъ рѣкы Днѣпря, и не съвѣдаемъ откуду суть пришли, и кдѣ ся дѣша опять; Богъ вѣсть, отколѣ приде на насъ, за грѣхы наша.
