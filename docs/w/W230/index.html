Letters of 1241 on the Mongol invasion, printed in the Golden Chronicle of Hohenschwangau

Latin letters written in 1241 by kings, dukes, bishops and a Dominican friar as the Tartars overran Poland and Hungary, printed by Joseph von Hormayr in 1842.

Catalogue title The Golden Chronicle of Hohenschwangau, appendix on the Mongols: Latin letters of 1241 from a Bavarian manuscript

Joseph von Hormayr, editor; anonymous and named correspondents of 1241

Joseph von Hormayr's local history of Hohenschwangau castle in Bavaria (Munich, 1842) includes an appendix, 'The Great Mongol Flood', where he tells the story of the 1241 invasion in romantic German and then prints Latin documents supplied by J. F. Boehmer: letters from King Bela IV of Hungary and King Wenceslas of Bohemia to Conrad IV, from Duke Frederick of Austria, Duke Otto of Bavaria and the bishops of Freising and Augsburg, and, notably, a text of the Hungarian Dominican Julian's report to the papal legate on the Tartars' origin and way of war. The corpus holds two scans of the book; the letters sit on scan pages n346 to n359 of T0566 (n364 to n372 of T0565).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongol armies of Batu and his generals that invaded Rus', Poland, Hungary and Moravia in 1240 to 1242; the letters call them 'Tartari', and Hormayr's German calls them Mongolen or Tataren.
Why read it
You hear the invasion in the voices of people living through it: a king writing from Vienna after losing his kingdom, dukes counting the enemy dead at their borders, and a friar relaying what he learned on the Russian frontier, including the khan's threatening letter to the king of Hungary.
Watch out
The Latin letters are contemporary, but Hormayr's German narrative is a nineteenth-century patriotic retelling full of local legends. The OCR of the Latin is very poor in places, so quotes are cleaned heavily. The card doubted that Julian's letter was here; it is (letter no. 5), though in a garbled text.

What it says: 11 passages

Citiesc. 1238

A khan's palace with golden doors in the city of 'Homah'

Julian reports that the son of the first Tartar duke, 'Chayn', rules from a great city called Homah, in a palace so large that a thousand horsemen ride in through one door and out again without dismounting, with golden doors and a high golden bed on golden columns. Anyone who touches the threshold with his foot is struck with a sword. It is a vivid second-hand picture of a steppe court, filtered through frontier rumour.

he resides in the great city of Homah … he has a palace so large that a thousand horsemen enter by one door and, bowing to him, go out again still mounted.

Working translation

Original wording

residet in civitate magna Homah … palatium habet ita magnum, quod mille equites intrant per unum hostium et eidem inclinantes equites exeunt nichilominus insidentes.

Heard itFriar Julian of Hungary, O.P., report to the bishop of Perugia (c. 1238), in the text printed by Hormayr (1842)

About Tartars (a khan Julian calls 'Chayn')

Keep in mind

Hearsay; 'Homah' is not identified (Julian also mentions a 'sultan of Homach'), and the golden palace may blend tales of the khan's great tent with rumours of Near Eastern cities. The threshold taboo matches what Carpini and Rubruck saw.

CitiesHungary1241

Bela warns that the Tartars are building forts in Hungary

In the same letter Bela urges the German princes to march with crossbowmen and supplies, warning that a Tartar hold on Hungary would be a heavy threat to the Empire 'because they are raising many fortifications there'. He also says he knows their strength from personal experience of battle, and that their wealth from forty conquered kingdoms could enrich twenty kingdoms if they were destroyed.

since their being received there, that is of the Tartars, would be most burdensome to the Empire, because they are raising very many fortifications there

Working translation

Original wording

cum eorum ibidem receptio, Tartarorum videlicet, esset imperio plurimum honerosa, eo quod munitiones ibidem plurimas erigant

Daily lifeRussian frontierc. 1238

Conquered men made to fight, and the rest 'called Tartars'

Julian explains how the Tartar army grows: they kill the rulers of conquered lands at once, drive the strong men into battle in front of them, leave the weaker men to farm, give each of them ten or more women of the dead, and order that 'from now on they be called Tartars'. Soldiers who retreat are killed by the Tartars, so they prefer to die fighting. Instead of storming strong castles they strip the land and force the local people to attack the castle.

assigning ten or more to each, and they order them that from now on they be called Tartars. … They do not storm fortified castles, but first lay waste the land and plunder the people, and compel the gathered people of that same land to fight to take the castle.

Original wording

cuilibet decem vel plures assignantes et imponunt eisdem, ut de cetero Tartari nuncupentur. … Castra munita non expugnant, sed prius devastant terram et populum depredantur et ejusdem terre populum congregatum compellunt ad pugnam ad expugnandum ipsum castrum.

Daily lifeHungaryc. 1237 to 1238

The khan's letter: 'You live in houses, how will you escape me?'

Julian carried to King Bela a letter the Tartar ruler had sent, written in 'pagan letters and the Tartar tongue', which a pagan he met in Cumania translated. The khan, calling himself the messenger of the heavenly king, demands Bela's surrender and the return of the Cumans who fled to Hungary, and draws a sharp contrast: the tent-dwelling Cumans might escape him, but a king who lives in houses, castles and cities cannot.

For it is easier for the Cumans to escape than for you, since they, going about without houses, with tents, may perhaps escape. But you, living in houses, have castles and cities: how will you flee my hands?

Working translation

Original wording

Facilius enim est Cumanis evadere quam tibi, quia illi sine domibus cum tentoriis ambulantes possunt forsitan evadere. Tu autem in domibus habitans habes castra et civitates, quomodo effugies manus meas?

Daily lifeRussian frontierc. 1238

They seem to rain arrows rather than shoot them

Julian's report on how the Tartars fight: they shoot farther than other nations, and in the first clash 'do not seem to shoot but to rain arrows', while swords and lances are said to be less suited to them. Their army is organised so that one Tartar commands ten men and a centurion commands a hundred, so that spies cannot hide among them.

it is said that they shoot farther than other nations are accustomed to. In the first clash of battle, it is said, they do not shoot, but seem as it were to rain arrows.

Original wording

dicitur, quod longius jaciunt, quam alie consueverint naciones. In prima congressione belli, ut dicitur, non sagittant, sed quasi pluere sagittas videntur.

The nameHungary1241

'Barbarian nations that call themselves Tartars', like locusts

King Bela IV of Hungary, writing to the German king Conrad IV after the disaster at Mohi, describes the invaders as barbarian nations 'which call themselves Tartars', coming out of the East like locusts from the desert. He lists Greater Hungary, Bulgaria, Cumania, Rus', Poland and Moravia as ravaged, with only some castles and strongholds still holding out, and his own kingdom beyond the Danube occupied.

barbarian nations which call themselves Tartars, coming out of the eastern region like locusts from the desert, having laid waste Greater Hungary, Bulgaria, Cumania, Rus' and also Poland and Moravia, except for certain castles and fortifications which still defend themselves to this day

The nameRussian frontier (Suzdal)1237 to 1238

Friar Julian: the Tartars' first land was 'Gothia'

Friar Julian, a Hungarian Dominican who had travelled to the edge of Rus' looking for the pagan 'Greater Hungarians', tells the papal legate what he learned 'from many': that the Tartars once lived where the Cumans now live, are 'in truth' called Ishmaelites but wish to be called Tartars, and came out of a land called 'Gothia'. He then tells a tangled origin story of a duke named Gurgutha whose vengeance war launched their conquests.

For it is reported by many that the Tartars, who formerly lived in the land the Cumans now inhabit, are in truth called Ishmaelites, but now wish to be called Tartars. The land from which they first came out is called Gothia

Working translation

Original wording

Relatum est enim a pluribus, quod Tartari, qui prius inhabitabant terram quam Cumani nunc inhabitant, dicuntur in veritate illi Ysmahelite, volunt nunc Tartari appellari. Terra autem de qua prius sunt egressi Gothia vocatur

Heard itFriar Julian of Hungary, O.P., report to the bishop of Perugia (c. 1238), in the text printed by Hormayr (1842)

About Tartars (Mongols)

Keep in mind

Hearsay gathered on the Russian frontier; the 'Ishmaelite' label is biblical classification, and the Gurgutha story has no basis in Mongol history. The OCR is badly garbled and the wording is reconstructed from it.

Strange talesStraubingApril 1241

A people from 'unknown lands' whose reason for coming no one knows

Duke Otto of Bavaria, writing from Straubing on 11 April 1241, two days after Liegnitz, warns the bishop of Augsburg of a barbarous people 'of infinite multitude, coming from distant and unknown parts', whose reason for coming nobody knows except that they persecute and kill Christians. He reports Cracow's land destroyed, the duke of Poland defeated, and the Tartars at the borders of Bohemia in three armies, and fears for all Germany.

a certain barbarous people of the Tartars, exceedingly cruel, of infinite multitude, coming from distant and unknown parts, the cause of whose coming we do not know, except that they wickedly pursue and kill all Christians.

Original wording

Strange talesHungary1242

Hungary after the Tartars: church towers the only signposts

Hormayr's own nineteenth-century retelling of what King Bela found on his return: church towers the only guides, grass growing in the streets, whole days' journeys without a living soul, thousands of unburied corpses, wolves snatching children, and human flesh sold openly. It is vivid but it is Hormayr's rhetoric, built on chronicles like Master Roger's, not an eyewitness text.

church towers were the only signposts, grass and brush on roads and lanes, whole days' journeys without a human soul, but many thousands of unburied, half-rotted, horribly mutilated corpses

Working translation

Original wording

waren Kirchthürme die einzigen Wegweiser, Gras und Gesträuch auf Strassen und Gassen, ganze Tagereisen weit keine menschliche Seele, aber viele tausende, unbegrabene, halb verwesete, grässlich verstümmelte Leichen

Strange talesStramberk1241 (legends recorded 1842)

Moravian legends: bakers' 'hands, feet and ears' and Tartar arrows in the fields

Hormayr collects Moravian memories of the invasion: Stramberk, which alone held out, where bakers every Pentecost bake pastry 'hands, feet and ears' because the Mongols cut these from the dead and sent them to their khan in sacks; a field where tiny horseshoes, swords and very long foreign arrows are dug up; and the legend that Jaroslav of Sternberg killed a Mongol prince at Olomouc. These are local traditions, not medieval evidence.

Stramberk in the Prerov district is the place that alone fended off the Mongols; in its eternal memory the baker every year at Pentecost, the day of deliverance, bakes hands, feet and ears, because the Mongols were wont to cut these off the slain, and probably many of the living

Working translation

Original wording

Stramberg im Preraner-Kreise ist der Ort, der sich der Mongolen allein erwehrte, zu dessen ewigem Gedächtnisse der Bäcker alljährlich um Pfingsten, dem Tage der Erlösung, Hände, Füsse und Ohren bäckt, weil die Mongolen diese den Erschlagenen und wohl auch vielen Lebenden abzuschneiden

Legend or rumourJoseph von Hormayr, editor's narrative (1842)

About Tartars (Mongols)

Strange talesGermany1241

Rumour: the emperor himself called in the Mongols

Hormayr notes that German enthusiasm for the crusade against the Mongols was weakened by a slander spread by papal partisans: that Emperor Frederick II, who liked the company of Arabs, Jews and heretics, had himself summoned the Mongols. He also repeats the chronicle story of an old Englishman, expelled from the crusader army, captured serving the Tartars as interpreter and guide.

the slander spread everywhere by the papal party: that the wicked Emperor Frederick himself, who liked best to keep company with Arabs, Jews and heretics, had called in the Mongols.

Working translation

Original wording

die von den Päpstlichen überall ausgestreute Verläumdung: der mit Arabern, Juden und Ketzern am liebsten verkehrende, ruchlose Kaiser Friedrich selber, habe die Mongolen herbeigerufen.

Legend or rumourJoseph von Hormayr, editor's narrative (1842)

Keep in mind

Cautions

Why it matters

Contemporary Latin correspondence documenting the 1241 Mongol invasion as it was perceived in Central Europe; the collection is one of the vehicles for the Hungarian Dominican Julian's letter on the Tartars (not verified in this sample) and the Ottobeuren letters.
