Wisdom of Royal Glory: a Turkic mirror for princes from Kashgar

A long Turkic poem of advice to rulers written at the Qarakhanid court in 1069 to 1070, in Vambery's 1870 edition.

Catalogue title Wisdom of Royal Glory: Qarakhanid Turkic mirror for princes, 1069-1070; Vambery’s 1870 partial edition of the Vienna (Herat 1439) Uyghur-script manuscript with German translation, and a facsimile of the Fergana (Namangan) manuscript

قوتادغو بیلیگ

Yusuf Khass Hajib (fl. 1069-1070)

Yusuf Khass Hajib wrote the Kutadgu Bilig ('Wisdom that brings good fortune') in Balasaghun and Kashgar in 1069 to 1070 and presented it to the Qarakhanid ruler, a Muslim Turkic dynasty. It is a verse dialogue about how a king should rule and how he should treat each class of subjects: scholars, merchants, farmers, herders. The corpus copies are Hermann Vambery's partial edition of the Herat manuscript (written in Uighur script in 1439), with German translation, glossary and introduction (Innsbruck 1870). It is literature, not a travel account.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The poem itself does not use 'Tatar'; its people are 'Turks'. Vambery, writing in 1870, uses 'Chinese Tartary' as an alternative name for East Turkestan and 'Tatar' for the Mongol conquerors.
Why read it
It shows a settled, literate, Muslim Turkic court in Kashgar two centuries before the Mongols, with its own views of nomads, herders, merchants and palaces.
Watch out
Vambery's text and translation are only a selection and his readings are superseded (Radloff 1891 to 1910, Arat 1947). Several quotes come from his glossary and introduction, not from a continuous text.

BuildingsKashgar1069 to 1070

'The wanderer builds no farmsteads and no palaces'

A line of the poem, quoted in Vambery's glossary under the word for 'nomad', says that the person who moves from place to place does not build farmsteads or palaces. The poet is contrasting nomad life with the settled court life of Kashgar, where palaces (saray) were built. It is an eleventh-century Turkic writer's own view that nomads and palace-builders were different ways of living.

köchükli kishi etmes urtu saray: he who moves from one place to another builds no farmsteads and no palaces. (chapter 87)

Daily lifeKashgar1069 to 1070

Herdsmen give kumis, milk, wool and cheese

The poem's chapter on how a ruler should treat the keepers of the horse herds lists what they provide: food, drink and horses to ride, and kumis (fermented mare's milk), milk, wool, butter, sour milk and cheese, food and clothing and coarse wool for ropes. The poet calls them useful and honest but 'without knowledge', people from whom a prince should not expect law or manners. It is a courtier's view of the herding life that fed the court.

3 Food, drink, horses to ride … stallions come from these (people), and also … 4 Kumis, milk, wool, butter, sour milk and cheese, food and clothing, as well as coarse wool for ropes.

Daily lifeKashgar1069 to 1070

Merchants and 'the caravan from Cathay'

In the chapter on merchants the poet advises the prince to welcome them: they travel from east to west, bring the finest clothes and 'wonders', and without them nobody could dress well in winter or see strings of pearls. When 'the caravan of Cathay' (China) sets out, thousands of gifts follow. It places the Turkic court of Kashgar on the great trade road between China and the west.

7 If there were no merchant who travels the world, how could you clothe yourself in the middle of harsh winter? 8 When Cathay's caravan sets out, it is a caravan from which thousandfold gifts will come.

The nameKashgar1069 to 1070 (prose preface added later)

One book, many names: 'the Turkish Shahnameh'

The prose preface of the Herat manuscript says the book was finished 'in the land of Kashgar' and presented to Bughra Khan, who made the author his chamberlain, hence his title Khass Hajib. It boasts that the Chinese called it 'The Manners of Kings', the Iranians 'the Turkish Shahnameh', and the 'Turanians' Kutadgu Bilig. The Turks here are a named people with their own literary language, 'the Turkish tongue' of the eastern lands.

The people of Chin called it Adab al-Muluk, 'the manners of princes'; the scholars of the prince of Machin called it Ayn al-Mamlaka, 'the eye of the realm'; the easterners called it Zinat al-Umara, 'the ornament of princes'. The Iranians called it the Turkish Shahnameh.

Original wording

The nameEast Turkestan (Xinjiang)1870

Vambery: East Turkestan, 'also called Chinese Tartary'

In his introduction Vambery places the Uighur homeland in 'today's East Turkestan or Chinese Tartary, as it is also called'. For a nineteenth-century scholar, 'Tartary' is just an older map name for the region where the Kutadgu Bilig was written, next to the modern term. He also claims Samarkand and Karshi as Uighur foundations from the meaning of their names, an etymological guess.

If we therefore describe the homeland of the Uighurs as present-day East Turkestan, or Chinese Tartary as it is also called, it must be noted that some colonies lay outside these borders.

Strange talesCentral Asia13th century (as Vambery tells it)

Lost books blamed on 'Tatar lust for war'

Vambery laments that Uighur manuscripts on history and religion, such as those Rashid al-Din used for his great history, are lost for ever, destroyed by time and 'the even more devastating fire of Tatar lust for war'. Here 'Tatar' means the Mongol conquerors. It is a nineteenth-century scholar's explanation for missing sources, not evidence of a hidden civilisation.

Much has been destroyed by the iron tooth of time and the even more devastating fire of Tatar lust for war. Precious, irreplaceable jewels of the history of those distant parts of Asia, such as those Rashid al-Din still used when compiling his Taj al-Tawarikh, are lost to us for ever.

Why it matters

Together with Kashgari, the Qarakhanid literary evidence that a Turkic Islamic state existed in the Tarim-Semirechye region in the 1070s, the region European maps later filled with 'Tartary'.
