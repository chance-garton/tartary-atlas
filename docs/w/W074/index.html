The memoirs of Babur, Timurid prince and founder of Mughal India

Babur's own Turki memoir of his wars in Fergana, Samarkand, Kabul and India, in the 1826 Leyden and Erskine translation.

Catalogue title Memoirs of Zehir-ed-din Muhammed Baber (Baburnama)

Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur

Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur (1483-1530), descended from Timur on his father's side and from Genghis Khan on his mother's, wrote the Baburnama in Chaghatai Turki. It follows his boyhood as king of Fergana, his repeated capture and loss of Samarkand to the Uzbeks, his years in Kabul, and his conquest of north India in 1526. This corpus copy is the 1826 English translation begun by John Leyden and completed by William Erskine from Persian and Turki manuscripts, with Erskine's long introduction on the 'Tartar tribes' and the geography of Central Asia, and a supplement covering gaps in the text.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Babur himself speaks of Turks, Moghuls (Mongols) and Uzbeks, not 'Tartars'. The word comes from Erskine, the editor, who calls Babur 'of Tartar race' and devotes pages to explaining that 'Tartar' was a European label for Turks and Mongols alike, and that the peoples themselves did not use it.
Why read it
A ruler's first-hand descriptions of Samarkand's stone mosques, palaces, observatory and bazaars, of felt tents and steppe customs, and an 1826 scholar's careful discussion of where the name 'Tartar' came from, including the Tartarus pun.
Watch out
Parts of the memoir are lost and the translators worked from differing manuscripts, often noting where copies disagree. Erskine's introduction is his own synthesis of earlier European and Oriental authors, and carries the racial assumptions of his day.

CitiesTransoxiana (Uzbekistan)1826 (about earlier centuries)

Towns for Persians, open country for Turks

Erskine explains why the Turki language absorbed so many Persian words. The cities north of the Oxus were mostly lived in and farmed by the older Persian-speaking people, the Sarts or Tajiks, because the Turks long disliked town life and farming, mocking wheat as 'a weed'. The Turkish rulers held court in these cities, but the townspeople were largely not Turks.

The cities of Samarkand, Bokhara, Ahsi, Andejan, and Tashkend, as well as the other towns to the north of the Oxus and Jaxartes, were chiefly inhabited by Persians, the Turks long retaining their aversion to the life of a town, and refusing to submit to the drudgery of agriculture for the sake of supporting themselves on the top of a weed, as they call wheat in derision.

Andijan: a stone-faced moat and three gates

Babur begins his memoirs with his own kingdom of Fergana. Its capital Andijan was, he says, the largest fortress in Transoxiana after Samarkand and Kesh, with three gates, a citadel on the south, a stone-faced moat circled by a pebbled road, and suburbs all round. Its people were all Turks and spoke the same Turki that poets wrote in.

In Maweralnaher, after the fortresses of Samarkand and Kesh, none is equal in size to Andejan. It has three gates. The citadel is situated on the south of the city. … Around the fortress, on the edge of the stone-faced moat, is a broad highway covered with pebbles. All round the fort are the suburbs

CitiesSamarkandc.1500

Samarkand: every trade in its own bazaar

Babur calls Samarkand 'a wonderfully elegant city'. Its markets were arranged so each trade had its own bazaar, its bakers and cooks were excellent, and it made the best paper in the world and a crimson velvet exported everywhere. He also measured the city walls himself at 10,600 paces round.

Samarkand is a wonderfully elegant city. One of its distinguishing peculiarities is, that each trade has its own bazar ; so that different trades are not mixed together in the same place. The established customs and regulations are good. The bakers' shops are excellent, and the cooks are skilful. The best paper in the world comes from Samarkand.

BuildingsSamarkandc.1497 to 1500 (describing buildings of c.1400)

Timur's stone mosque, built by stonecutters from India

Babur, who took Samarkand more than once, describes the buildings of his ancestor Timur: a four-storey palace, the Gok-serai, in the citadel, and a grand mosque of stone near the Iron Gate, whose Qur'an inscription could be read almost a kos away. He notes that the stonecutters were brought from Hindustan. Erskine's footnote adds that the historian Sharaf al-Din counted 200 masons from Azerbaijan, Fars and India.

There are many other magnificent edifices. One of these is the grand mosque, which is situated near the Iron-gate, within the walls of the city, and is built of stone. A number of stone-cutters were brought from Hindustan to work on it.

BuildingsSamarkandc.1500 (built 1420s)

Ulugh Beg's three-storey observatory

Among the works of Timur's grandson Ulugh Beg, Babur singles out the observatory on the hill of Kohik: three storeys high, fitted with instruments, where the astronomical tables still in use in his day were made. He places it among the seven or eight observatories ever built, alongside Hulagu's at Maragha.

Another remarkable edifice is the observatory, erected on the skirts of the hill of Kohik, which is provided with an astronomical apparatus, and is three stories in height. By means of this observatory, Ulugh Beg Mirza composed the Zich-Korkani (or Korkani Astronomical Tables), which are followed at the present time, scarcely any other being used.

BuildingsSamarkandc.1500

Forty stone pillars and a house of Chinese porcelain

In the Garden of the Plain, Babur describes the Chehel Sutun (Forty Pillars), a two-storey hall built entirely on carved stone columns, twisted and fluted, and a throne cut from a single huge stone brought from far away. Nearby stood a pavilion whose walls were covered in Chinese porcelain, for which, he was told, someone had been sent to China.

In this garden, there is another state pavilion, the walls of which are overlaid with porcelain of China, whence it is called the Chinese House. It is said that a person was sent to Khita, for the purpose of bringing it.

BuildingsHisar (Tajikistan)1504

A prince in exile sleeps in a felt tent of cross-poles

Driven out of his homeland, Babur set off for Khorasan with two or three hundred followers, most on foot. They had only two proper tents; Babur gave his to his mother and slept each night in a small felt tent on flexible poles. Erskine's note explains this alachuq was a felt-covered frame that folded up easily.

Such was our distress, that among us all we had only two tents. My own tent was pitched for my mother, and they erected for me at each stage a felt-tent of cross-poles, in which I used to take up my quarters.

BuildingsAgra1526 to 1528

Fifteen hundred stonecutters on Babur's buildings in India

Babur was scornful of Indian architecture but impressed by India's supply of craftsmen. He compares Timur's famous stone mosque in Samarkand, built with 200 masons from several countries, to his own works: 680 stonecutters at Agra alone, and 1,491 across his building sites. The buildings of this Timurid ruler were raised by local Indian hands.

In Agra alone, and of stone-cutters belonging to that place only, I every day employed on my palaces six hundred and eighty persons; and in Agra, Sikri, Biana, Dhulpur, Gualiar, and Koel, there were every day employed on my works one thousand four hundred and ninety-one stone-cutters.

Daily lifeSar-i Pul, near Samarkand1501

The Uzbek turning charge

Describing how he lost the battle, Babur explains the Uzbek method: they always tried to turn the enemy's flank (the tulghama), and charged front and rear together, shooting arrows at full gallop, chiefs and common men mixed together, retreating just as fast if driven back. Babur escaped by swimming his armoured horse across the Kohik river.

In battle, the great reliance of the Uzbeks is on the Tulghmeh (or turning the enemy's flank). They never engage without using the Tulghmeh. Another of their practices is to advance and charge in front and rear, discharging their arrows at full gallop, pell-mell, chiefs and common soldiers, and, if repulsed, they in like manner retire full gallop.

Daily lifeMoghulistanMid-1400s

A khan raised up on a white felt

Babur tells how his Mongol grandfather Yunis Khan was made khan: a leading beg seated him and his new wife on a white felt and proclaimed him, according to the ancient Mongol rules (tore). Erskine's footnote links it to the felt on which Genghis Khan was said to have been proclaimed.

Shir Haji Beg having seated the Khan and Ais-doulet Begum on a white felt, according to the Tureh, or ancient Institutions of the Moghuls, they proclaimed him Khan.

Daily lifeSamarkandc.1488

The veiled bride and the child who unveils her

Visiting his uncle in Samarkand at the age of five, Babur found the new bride still veiled 'according to the custom of the Turks', and was told to take the veil off. The translators' note explains the custom: a Turki bride stayed veiled for a time after marriage until a child of the family pulled the veil off and ran, which was thought to bring the child luck in marriage.

she still kept her face covered with a veil, according to the custom of the Turks. He directed me, and I took off her veil.

The nameMongolia1826 (discussing the 1240s and 1250s)

A name the conquerors themselves rejected

In his introduction Erskine explains that 'Tartar' was first used by thirteenth-century European travellers and that Genghis Khan's family disliked it. He cites Rubruck, who was warned at the court of Sartaq (actually Batu's son, not Genghis's grandson) to call him Moal, a Mongol, because the Tatars were a different, conquered tribe. The European name for the whole empire was thus the name of one of its subject peoples.

This name of Tartar, however, by which we are accustomed to designate Chengiz Khan and his successors as well as their empire, these princes themselves rejected with disdain. Rubriquis, who visited the court of Sartakh, Chengiz Khan's grandson, about the year 1254, was cautioned, therefore, to call him Moal (that is Moghul), and not Tartar

Who Mongols and the Tatar tribe

Read itWilliam Erskine, editor's introduction (1826), citing William of Rubruck

Keep in mind. Editorial synthesis, not Babur. Sartaq was Batu's son, a great-grandson of Genghis Khan, and in Rubruck's own account the warning was not to call Sartaq a Christian; the remark that the Mongols disliked being called Tartars is Rubruck's general comment.

The name1826

Tartar and Tartarus: why the name stuck in Europe

Erskine reports that the Mongols (Kalmyks) regarded 'Tatar' as an insult meaning something like 'robber', and asks why a name its bearers rejected became so widespread. His answer is the old pun: 'Tartar' sounded like Tartarus, the classical underworld, and fitted the terror the invaders caused in Europe.

It is singular that a name thus rejected among the nations to whom it is applied, should have had so much currency. The resemblance between Tartar and the infernal Tartarus, joined with the dread and horror in which the Tartar invaders were held, while they scattered dismay over Europe, probably, as has been well conjectured, preserved the name in the west.

The nameSar-i Pul, near Samarkand1501

'Were the name Moghul written in gold'

After his defeat by the Uzbek Shaybani Khan, Babur complains bitterly that the Mongol troops sent to help him did not fight but turned on his own men, unhorsing and stripping them, and he says this was their usual practice. He sums up with a couplet that shows how sharply a Timurid Turk could separate himself from the Mongols, though he was one by descent.

If the Moghul race were a race of angels, it is a bad race ; And were the name Moghul written in gold, it would be odious.

The nameFergana Valley1826 (about Babur, born 1483)

Babur: a Turk by his father, a Mongol by his mother

Erskine explains that Babur belonged to 'both races of Tartars'. Through his father he descended from Timur, and so counted himself a Turk; through his mother he descended from Genghis Khan. Erskine adds that Babur's sympathies were entirely with the Turks and that he often speaks of the Mongols with hatred and contempt.

Baber had a close connexion with both races of Tartars, He traced up his descent on the father's side in a direct line to the great Taimur Beg, whence he always speaks of himself as being a Turk ; while by the mother's side he was sprung from Chengiz Khan, being the grandson of Yunis Khan, a celebrated prince of the Moghuls.

Strange talesGanges near AllahabadMarch 1529

A Chaghatai prince makes wind and rain

In India, Babur notes calmly that the Chaghatai prince Tokhta Bugha Sultan 'performed some of his enchantments' in his boat, and a high wind rose and it began to rain. This is the steppe weather magic with a rain-stone (yada) that Erskine's introduction calls wholly of Tartar origin and traces to a legendary stone given by Noah to Japhet.

I sent for the Sultans into the boat ; Tokhteh Bugha Sultan performed some of his enchantments. A high wind having risen, it began to rain.
