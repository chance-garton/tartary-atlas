Martin Sauer's account of the Billings expedition

The secretary of Russia's Billings expedition describes the road across Siberia and the Yakuts, 1785 to 1794.

Catalogue title An Account of a Geographical and Astronomical Expedition to the Northern Parts of Russia

Martin Sauer

Martin Sauer, an Englishman, was secretary to Joseph Billings's Russian naval expedition to the Kolyma, the Chukchi coast and the Aleutians (1785 to 1794). He travelled overland from St Petersburg through Kazan, Tobolsk and Irkutsk to Yakutsk and Okhotsk, and spent winters among the Yakuts gathering their history and customs 'from personal inquiry and research'. The book was published in London in 1802; this corpus copy is that edition. The Tartars appear as the Muslim Tatars of Kazan and Tobolsk and, more often, as the Yakuts, whom Sauer and the Russians of his day still called Tartars.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Two different peoples: the Muslim Volga and Siberian Tatars around Kazan, Tobolsk and Tara, and the Turkic-speaking Yakuts (Sakha) of the Lena, whom he calls 'wandering tribes of Tartars' and 'Tartars tributary to Russia'.
Why read it
The Yakut chapter is one of the fullest eighteenth-century accounts of a Siberian 'Tartar' people: their own migration legend, kumis festival, shamans, oaths and houses, plus honest notes on how Russian rule was impoverishing them.
Watch out
Sauer's Yakut material is gathered from informants and Russian officials, not only his own eyes. He shares the anti-shaman scepticism of his time. The Sarychev volume (W009) covers the same expedition, so the two are not fully independent.

CitiesYakutsk1620-1632

How the Russians built their fort among the Yakuts

Sauer gives a short history of the conquest: Cossacks first heard of the Yakuts in 1620; a chief named Millach went over to the Russians and gave them forty archers to defeat his rival Tygin. Tribute was collected from 1630, and in 1632 the first ostrog (fort) was built on a hill, later moved to where Yakutsk stands. The Russian town grew out of a local feud.

Millach is the first chief reported to have gone over to the Russians. … He supplied the Russians with food, and gave them 40 archers to subdue Tygin and his tribe, which was accomplished on Tygin's falling in the field. Tribute was collected in 1630, and in 1632 the first ostrog was built among them on the mountain Tshebedal, but afterwards removed to the place where the town now stands.

BuildingsYakutsk region1786-1788

Birch-bark cone for the kumis festival

Each June, Yakut chiefs held a feast to bless their herds. Mare's milk was fermented into kumis in large leather bags, and a summer hut was raised on a meadow: a cone of thin poles covered with birch bark, decorated inside and out with birch branches, with a hearth in the middle. Guests of any nation were welcome; the shamans took the head seats. The shaman then poured kumis on the fire to each of the gods.

A summer hut is built of thin poles of a conical form, covered with the inner bark of birch, on some extensive meadow. It is ornamented inside and out with branches of the birch-tree, and a hearth is made in the centre. Relations and acquaintances are invited to the banquet; but all guests are welcome of every nation indiscriminately.

BuildingsYakutsk region1786-1788

Doors to the east, men south, women north

Inside a Yakut winter hut the door always faced east, the fireplace stood near the middle with room to walk around it, and benches and small sleeping cabins lined the walls. Men sat on the south side and women on the north. A hut where someone had died was abandoned to ruin as a home of demons.

They always take care that the doors of their huts shall face the east. The fire-place is nearly in the middle; the back of the chimney towards the door, and a free passage quite round it. The sides of the hut are furnished with benches and small cabins, which serve for sleeping places. … The men keep the south side, and the women the north.

Daily lifeYakutsk region1786-1788

An oath sworn over the shaman's drum

A Yakut accused of stealing cattle could clear himself by oath. He stood before the fire facing the sun, called down the loss of family, cattle, sunlight and life if he were guilty, stepped over the shaman's drum and robe, and breathed smoke from butter thrown on the coals. Some tribes also made him bite a bear's head, believing a bear would kill the perjurer. Many innocent men preferred to pay rather than swear.

A magician places his tambour and dress before the fire, the embers of which are burning. The accused stands before it, facing the sun, and says: "May I lose during my life all that man holds dear and desirable, father, mother, wives, children, relations; all my possessions and cattle; the light of the sun, and then my own life

Daily lifeTatarstan (east of Kazan)1786

Kazan's Tatars: clean, honest, better farmers

Sauer found Kazan a well-built trading city of Russians, Tatars and Armenians, and at the governor's dinner on 6 January 1786 the Muslim chief priest sat at table with the Orthodox bishop and a Lutheran pastor. Riding east from the city, he praises the Tatar villagers: Muslims, clean, honest and hard-working, whose land yielded nearly twice what Russian farmers got from the same ground.

The Tartars are Mahometans, and very clean in their persons and habitations. The women are, in general, very handsome, and dress extremely neat. They are industrious, honest, and peaceable; and, under their management, a piece of ground of a given extent will produce nearly twice as much as the Russians obtain from an equal quantity. All the villages are built in vallies on the borders of rivers

Daily lifeTara, Omsk Oblast1786 and 1794

Tatar women spinning thread from nettles

On the road to Tobolsk in 1786, Sauer noted Tatar farmers who also made and sold neat worsted carpets. Coming back in 1794, he saw Tatar women near Tara spinning thread from nettle fibres, making linen as good as flax. The Siberian settlers also told him that trade with the Mongols was not as open as before.

The Tartar women about Tara were preparing nettles, and spinning thread from the fibres; the linen made of which was fine and good, apparently equal to that made of flax. I have already mentioned the neat carpets then made by these industrious people.

Daily lifeYakutsk region1786-1788

Shamans no better than an English conjurer

Sauer explains that shamans could be men or, more rarely, women, trained by an old master in lonely woods. He heard wonderful stories of their power even from Russians, but after watching them many times he was not impressed. He then describes a healing ritual in detail, with a horsehair switch, a sacrificed mare or cow, and a drum.

I have heard most wonderful relations of their power, even from the Russians; but, notwithstanding I have seen their enchantments or incantations many times, I never could discover any of their feats equal to that of a common conjurer in England.

Daily lifeAmur River1787

Fleeing to China: 6,000 Yakuts in one year

Sauer reports that Yakuts and Tungus unhappy under Russian rule were slipping away to the upper Amur valleys under Chinese protection, where they had reportedly built strong places. Russian officials told him more than 6,000 Yakuts moved to China in 1787 with all their goods, and his footnote remarks that such migrations reduced 'the number of Tartars tributary to Russia'. Elsewhere he blames heavy exactions by Russian officials for the Yakuts' decline.

In the year 1787, there migrated to China, from the districts of Olekma, Yakutsk, and the Vilui, more than 6000 Yakuti, with all their possessions

Daily lifeYakutsk to Okhotsk roadlate 1780s

A priest flogs his Tartar guides

In a footnote Sauer tells why he calls the expedition's priest 'more than savage'. On the road to Okhotsk the priest lost some food, assumed his two Tartar guides had stolen it, and had them tied to trees and flogged so badly that one died. Runaway exiles turned out to be the thieves. The priest said no harm was done because the guides were not Christians.

On a mere supposition that his two Tartar guides had taken it, he tied each of them up by an arm to a tree, and had them flogged to such a degree, that one of them died, and the other never recovered the use of his arm: it was afterwards known, that some runaway exiles hid in the woods were the thieves.

The nameYakutskLegend set about 300 years before 1787

The Yakuts' own story: fleeing from Lake Baikal

The Yakuts told Sauer they call themselves Sakha and came from the south. Their chief Omogai Bey led his tribe and cattle from the plains west of Lake Baikal to escape a stronger horde, fought the Buryats, and followed the Lena north to the meadows around today's Yakutsk. A related people near Krasnoyarsk, which Sauer calls Mongols, also called themselves Sakha and spoke the same language.

The nation known among the Russians by the name of Yakuti call themselves Socha, and say that they came originally from the south. … The Toyon (i.e. Chief) Omogai Bey, with all his tribe and cattle, left the fertile plains situated to the west of the lake Baical, or Baighal, to make way for a more powerful horde

The nameAldan River, Sakha Republic1786

Yakut horse-breeders: 'very opulent Tartars'

Sauer and the Russians around him still called the Yakuts and their neighbours Tartars. Describing the Lena valley, he says the little Russian posts existed to collect fur tribute from 'the wandering tribes of Tartars' nearby. Here, crossing the Aldan on horseback with Yakut guides, he describes the plains as home to 'very opulent Tartars' with huge herds of horses and cattle: the cattle- and horse-breeding Yakuts.

The woods abound in wild beasts and game; and the plains are inhabited by very opulent Tartars, who possess immense numbers of horses

Strange talesYakutskLegend

Aley the shaman, father of twelve sons

The Yakut founding story continues with Aley, a runaway from the Buryats who became Omogai's overseer and was thought to have supernatural powers. He declared himself a shaman, prophesied that Omogai's daughter would bear no children, and chose the chief's adopted girl instead. After Omogai's death most of the tribe followed Aley, who is said to have had twelve sons; the eldest founded a named tribe.

He observed, that all his tribe esteemed Aley to adoration; for they supposed him to possess supernatural powers, and attributed his continual good fortune and success to the immediate influence of spirits. … Aley now avowed himself a Shaman, and assumed the powers of divination.
