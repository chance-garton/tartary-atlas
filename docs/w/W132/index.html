Fine Talk from the Jade Hall: two Chinese journeys through the Mongol world

Wang Yun's notebook preserving a Chinese scholar's 1247 to 1248 journey to Khubilai's camp in Mongolia and an envoy's 1259 ride west to Hulegu in Persia.

Full title Fine Talk from the Jade Hall: Yuan miscellany preserving the Record of an Embassy to the West (Xi shi ji, 1263), the Record of an Embassy to the North (Bei shi ji) and the Journey to Lingbei (Lingbei jixing)

玉堂嘉話 (秋澗集 卷九十三至一百)

Wang Yun (1227-1304), Hanlin academician; preserving the travel records of Liu Yu (1259 embassy of Changde to Hülegü in Persia), Wugusun Zhongduan (1220-1221 Jin embassy to Chinggis Khan) and Zhang Dehui (1247-1248 journey to Möngke's court)

Read the 14 passagesOpen the original book

Wang Yun (1227 to 1304), a Yuan Hanlin academician, filled his notebook Yutang jiahua (also printed in his collected works, the Qiujian ji) with things he heard and copied. Two pieces matter here. Zhang Dehui's Record of a Journey tells how he was summoned in 1247 from Zhending to the camp of the prince (Khubilai) and lived ten months at his court in the Orkhon and Tamir country, dated summer 1248. Liu Yu's Record of an Embassy to the West, written in 1263, retells what the envoy Chang De saw and heard riding from Karakorum to Hulegu's army in Iran in 1259. The corpus holds Kanseki Repository texts of the Siku Yutang jiahua and the Sibu congkan Qiujian ji, poor scans, and Bretschneider's Mediaeval Researches vol. 2.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Neither traveller uses the word Tatar: they speak of Mongol clans (蒙古部族), 'the northern tongue', the prince and the imperial army. The Bretschneider volume held with this record shows the Chinese term Ta-ta (Tatars, Dada) in later Ming geography, as in the 'Water Tatars' of Manchuria.
Why read it
Zhang Dehui is the closest thing to a Chinese Rubruck: he describes felt tents and felt carts, earth-roofed farm huts on the Kerulen, walls and towns built by earlier dynasties, and the round of the Mongol year. Chang De's report shows the cities of the Mongol West as full of Uighur and Chinese settlers, and adds legends such as the Assassins' paradise and the lamb that grows from the ground.
Watch out
Zhang saw what he describes; Chang De's account comes second-hand through Liu Yu and mixes observation with hearsay about distant lands. The Siku copy re-spells many names (for example 博囉 for 孛羅), so the older Sibu congkan text is quoted where possible. Translations are working translations.

What it says: 14 passages

CitiesKarakorum (Orkhon valley)1247

The Karakorum valley: Khitan ruins and irrigated fields

From the south of the lake a branch road leads west into the city of Helin (Karakorum), about a hundred li away. Due west of the lake there is a small old walled town, also built by the Khitans... In the middle is the Helin plain. Most of the inhabitants farm, bringing water to all their fields... It was then late in the first month of autumn and the hemp and wheat had all withered; when I asked the farmers, they said there had already been three frosts.

Working translation

Original wording

自泊之南而西分道入和林城相去約百余里泊之正西有小故城亦契丹所築也…中即和林川也居人多事耕稼悉引水灌之…時孟秋下旬麻麥皆槁問之田者云巳三霜矣

Editorial context

Near a large clear lake (Ugii Nuur) Zhang notes the branch road to Helin, the Chinese name for Karakorum, about a hundred li away, and a small old walled town built by the Khitans. The Orkhon plain itself was farmed: people irrigated their fields (and, the text adds, kept some vegetable gardens), though in early autumn the crops were already killed by three frosts.

Zhang Dehui, Record of a Journey (Qiujian ji juan 100 = Yutang jiahua juan 8; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)

CitiesAlmaliq (near Huocheng, Xinjiang)1259

Almaliq: running water, fruit, Uighurs and Chinese settlers

Leaving the pass he reached the city of Alimali (Almaliq). Its streets and markets are all crossed by running water. There are all kinds of fruit; the melons, grapes and pomegranates are the best. The Huihe (Muslim Turks) live mixed with the Han Chinese, and their customs have gradually been coloured until they are rather like China's. Further south is the city of Chimur, whose inhabitants are mostly people from Bing and Fen (Shanxi).

Working translation

Original wording

出関至阿里麻里城市井皆流水交貫有諸菓唯瓜蒲萄石榴最佳囬紇與漢民雜居其俗漸染頗似中國又南有赤木児城居民多并汾人

Editorial context

Beyond the Iron Pass, guarded by Chinese soldiers, Chang De reached Almaliq, a Mongol-ruled city of the Chagatai realm. Its streets were crossed by running water and its melons, grapes and pomegranates were excellent. Muslim Turks lived mixed with Chinese and had picked up Chinese ways, and a town further south was full of settlers from Shanxi.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)

CitiesBaghdad1258 (text: dingsi year)

The fall of Baghdad

In the dingsi year they took the country of Baoda (Baghdad), two thousand li from north to south. Its ruler was called the Helifa (caliph). The city has an east city and a west city, with a great river between. The west city has no ramparts; the east city is fortified with brick, painted very splendidly on top. When the imperial army reached the city, in a single battle it broke picked troops more than 400,000 strong; the west city fell and all its people were slaughtered.

Working translation

Original wording

丁巳歳取報逹國南北二千里其主曰合里法其城有東西城中有大河西城無壁壘東城固之以甓繪其上甚盛王師至城不一交戰破勝兵四十餘萬西城䧟皆尽屠其民

Editorial context

Chang De's report describes Baghdad as two cities, east and west, divided by a great river, the western one without walls and the eastern one fortified with painted brick. The Mongol army broke the caliph's troops in one battle and slaughtered the people of the western city; the eastern city fell after a six-day siege (the page goes on). It is one of the very few Chinese accounts of the sack.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 094-7a)

About Mongols of Hulegu; the Abbasid caliph

Keep in mind

The dingsi year is 1257; Baghdad fell in February 1258. This text reads 城不 where the Siku copy has 城下 ('below the city'); the translation follows the Siku reading. The troop figure is an exaggeration.

BuildingsZhangjiakou, Hebei (Yehuling pass)1247

Beyond the pass: felt tents and felt carts

From here northward, all the post stations are held as shares by Mongol clans, and each station is named after its holder. Going up from the ridge, we travelled northeast and for the first time saw felt tents and felt carts; [people] follow water and grass and herd their animals, and that is all. This is no longer the land and custom of the Central Plain.

Working translation

Original wording

自是以北諸驛皆蒙古部族所分主也毎驛各以主者之名名之由嶺而上則東北行始見毳幕氊車逐水草畜牧而巳非復中原之風土也

Editorial context

Travelling north from Beijing, Zhang Dehui crossed the pass beyond Xuande and found that from here on every post station belonged to a Mongol clan and was named after its holder. Climbing the ridge he saw for the first time the felt tents and felt carts of herders who moved with water and grass. For him this line marked the end of the Chinese world.

Zhang Dehui, Record of a Journey (Qiujian ji juan 100 = Yutang jiahua juan 8; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)

BuildingsYu'er lakes, Inner Mongolia1247

A princess's walled palace by the lake

On the eastern shore of the lake is the outer wall of a princess's detached palace, more than one zhang high and some two li square. Inside stands a sleeping hall flanked by two chambers, with a 'turtle' gallery behind, two side wings along its flanks and a lookout tower rising in front; climbing it gives a fine view. East of the palace, commoners and artisans live together, forming something of a settlement.

Working translation

Original wording

泊之東涯有公主離宫之外垣高丈餘方廣二里許中建寝殿夾以二室背以龜軒旁列兩廡前峙眺樓登之頗快目力宫之東有民匠雜居稍成聚落

Editorial context

Past the sandy desert, at the Yu'er lakes, Zhang found a walled detached palace belonging to a princess, with a sleeping hall, side chambers, galleries and a lookout tower, and a small settlement of commoners and craftsmen beside it. It is an early glimpse of Mongol-era buildings on the steppe, and of the artisans who lived around them.

Zhang Dehui, Record of a Journey (Qiujian ji juan 100 = Yutang jiahua juan 8; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 100-6b)

About Mongol court; Chinese and other artisans

Keep in mind

The text does not name the princess or say who built the palace.

BuildingsKerulen River, Mongolia1247

Old walls of 'the former dynasty' and earth-roofed huts on the Kerulen

After four stages there is the ruined base of a long wall, stretching on endlessly as far as one can see; this too is an outer rampart built by the former dynasty... The people along the river are a mix of fan (steppe peoples) and Han Chinese. There are a few houses, all roofed with earth, and they also do some farming, of hemp and wheat, and that is all.

Working translation

Original wording

四驛有長城䫋址望之綿延不盡亦前朝所築之外堡也…瀕河之民雜以蕃漢稍有屋室皆以土冐之亦頗有種藝麻麥而巳

Editorial context

Zhang passed the ruined base of a long wall stretching out of sight, which he credits to 'the former dynasty', meaning the Jurchen Jin, as their outer rampart. On the Kerulen he found a mixed population of 'barbarians' and Chinese living in a few houses roofed with earth and growing a little hemp and wheat.

Zhang Dehui, Record of a Journey (Qiujian ji juan 100 = Yutang jiahua juan 8; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 100-7a)

About Mongols and Chinese living together

Keep in mind

'Former dynasty' (前朝) here means the Jin, whose border walls ran across Inner Mongolia.

BuildingsChu valley, Kyrgyzstan (probably)1259

Ruined walls, 'the old home of the Khitans'

On the 24th of the second month he passed between the two Yidu mountains. The land is level and the people many, with irrigation channels winding everywhere. There are many old ramparts and ruined walls; when he asked about them, [he was told] they were the old home of the Khitans. He reckoned this land to be a little under 15,000 li from Helin (Karakorum).

Working translation

Original wording

二月二十四日過亦堵两山間土平民夥溝洫映帶多故壘壊垣問之盖契丹故居也計其地去和林萬五千里而近

Editorial context

Crossing a level, well-watered and crowded land between two mountain ranges, Chang De saw many old ramparts and ruined walls. Asking about them, he was told they were the former home of the Khitans, meaning the Qara Khitai empire that ruled here before the Mongols. He reckoned the place nearly 15,000 li from Karakorum.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)

BuildingsBolad (Bole, Xinjiang)1259

Houses of earth with windows of glass

Going further southwest he passed the city of Boluo (Bolad). What they sow is all wheat and rice. The hills have many cypresses, which cannot grow into proper trunks but grow twisting among the rocks. In the city, dwellings, shops and gardens are interspersed; the houses are of earth, and the windows and doors are all of glass.

Working translation

Original wording

又西南行過博囉城所種皆麥稻山多栢不能株絡石而長城居肆囿間錯土屋忩户皆琉璃

Editorial context

At the city of Bolad (here spelled in its Siku form) Chang De noted fields of wheat and rice, stunted cypresses on the rocky hills, and a town where houses, shops and gardens were interspersed. The houses were built of earth, and the windows and doors were of glass (liuli). Bretschneider rendered the same line 'the houses are built of clay, and the windows furnished with glass'.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Yutang jiahua juan 2, Siku quanshu copy, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 002-6b)

About Townspeople under Mongol rule

Keep in mind

The Siku editors re-spelled the name as 博囉; the older text has 孛羅. Liuli can mean glass or glazed material.

Daily lifeTamir River, Khangai, Mongolia1247 to 1248

Mare's milk for the gods at the great tent

On its sunny side stands the tent-palace; this is the summer retreat... On the Double Ninth the prince's following assembles at the great ordo-tent and sprinkles white mares' milk, performing the seasonal sacrifice. The vessels used are all of birch, not decorated with gold or silver: they value plainness.

Working translation

Original wording

其陽帳殿在焉乃避夏之所也…至重九日王師麾下㑹于大矛帳灑白馬湩脩時祀也其什噐皆用禾樺不以金銀為飾尚質也

Editorial context

The prince's summer camp stood on the sunny side of a ridge beyond the Tamir. Twice a year, on the ninth day of the ninth month and of the fourth month, his followers gathered at the great ordo-tent and sprinkled the milk of white mares as a seasonal sacrifice, using plain birchwood vessels rather than gold and silver.

Zhang Dehui, Record of a Journey (Qiujian ji juan 100 = Yutang jiahua juan 8; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 100-8a)

About Mongols of the prince's (Khubilai's) camp

Keep in mind

大矛帳 is a copyist's slip for 大牙帳 ('great tooth-banner tent', the ordo), as the Siku copy reads.

Daily lifeKhangai mountains, Mongolia1248

White furs for the New Year

On the last day of the year they always move the tents to a new site, as the place for celebrating the New Year. That day there is a great banquet for all his people in front of the tents, and everyone from the prince down wears pure white furs. Only after three days do they go to the great ordo-tent to offer their New Year greetings.

Working translation

Original wording

比歳除日輙迁帳易地以為賀正之所日大晏所部於帳前自王以下皆衣純白裘三日後方詣大牙帳致賀礼也

Editorial context

At each year's end the whole camp moved its tents to a fresh site to celebrate the New Year. There was a great feast in front of the tents at which everyone from the prince down wore pure white furs, and three days later they went to the great tent to offer New Year greetings. White was the lucky colour of the Mongol New Year (the White Month).

Zhang Dehui, Record of a Journey (Qiujian ji juan 100 = Yutang jiahua juan 8; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 100-8b)

Daily lifeBetween Bolad and the Chu valley, Central Asia (Ma'a)1259

Sledges from post to post, and Kyrgyz dog teams

On coming into Ma'a, they use horses to pull sledges from post station to post station, carrying heavy loads and going fast. Some say that the Qiliqisi (Kyrgyz) use dogs in place of horses.

Working translation

Original wording

至麻阿中以馬撁拖床逓鋪負重而行疾或曰乞里乞四易馬以犬

Editorial context

West of Bolad, Chang De found that coins were of gold, silver and copper with inscriptions but no square hole, and that in the land he calls Ma'a horses pulled sledges from one post station to the next, carrying heavy loads at speed. He adds, as a report, that the Kyrgyz used dogs in place of horses. Bretschneider doubted there were sledges on this route but noted that Marco Polo also mentions dog sledges in Siberia.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 094-5a)

About Peoples of the Chagatai realm; Kyrgyz (Qirqis)

Keep in mind

The dog-sledge detail is introduced with 'some say'.

The nameManchuria (Sungari and Amur region)Yuan period, recorded 1461

'Water Tatars' in Manchuria

The country was inhabited by the Nü-chi and the Shui Ta-ta (Water Tatars), and formed the circuits of Ho-lan-fu and Shui Ta-ta.

Editorial context

Bretschneider summarises the Ming imperial geography of 1461 on the Jurchen homeland: after the Mongols overthrew the Jin, the Yuan set up military posts there, and the country was inhabited by Jurchens and 'Water Tatars', who gave their name to an administrative circuit. His footnote compares Carpini's 'Su-Mongal' or Water Mongols, who Carpini said called themselves Tartars after a river.

E. Bretschneider, Mediaeval Researches vol. 2 (1888, reissued 1910), summarising the Ming Geography of 1461
See the original page (p. 175)

About Jurchens (Nu-chi) and 'Water Tatars' (Shui Ta-ta)

Keep in mind

A 19th-century summary in English of a Ming source, not the Yutang jiahua itself.

Strange talesAlamut region, Iran1256

The Assassins' paradise

The soldiers of that country are all assassins. Their custom is that when they see a brave, strong man, they lure him with gain and make him kill his own father or elder brother with his own hand; then he is enrolled as a soldier. They make him drunk, carry him into a cave chamber and entertain him with music and beautiful women, indulging his desires for several days, then put him back where he was. When he wakes, they ask what he saw and teach him that if he can become an assassin, at death he will enjoy bliss like that.

Working translation

Original wording

其國兵皆刺客俗見男子勇壯者以利誘之令手刄父兄然後充兵醉酒扶入窟室娱以音樂羙女縱其慾數日復置故處既醒問其所見教之能爲刺客死則享福如此

Editorial context

Describing the Ismaili state the Mongols destroyed in 1256, Chang De repeats the story that its soldiers were all assassins, trained by being drugged, shown music and women in a hidden chamber, and told they would enjoy such bliss after death. It is the Chinese version of the 'Old Man of the Mountain' legend that Marco Polo tells, and it is hearsay.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 094-6b)

About Ismailis (Mulahida), conquered by Hulegu's Mongols

Keep in mind

A hostile legend about the Ismailis, told by their conquerors; not an eyewitness account.

Strange tales'The Western Sea' (unspecified far west)1259, written 1263

The lamb that grows from the ground

The furrow-planted sheep comes from the Western Sea. A sheep's navel is planted in the soil and watered; when it hears thunder it comes to life, its navel still tied into the ground. When it has grown, they startle it with a piece of wood; the navel snaps and it grazes. By autumn it can be eaten, and inside its navel there is seed again.

Working translation

Original wording

壠種羊岀西海羊臍種土中溉以水聞雷而生臍系地中及長驚以木臍断嚙草至秋可食臍内復有種

Editorial context

Among the marvels Chang De heard of is the 'furrow-planted sheep': a sheep's navel sown in the ground and watered sprouts when it hears thunder, grows up still tied to the earth by its navel, and is freed by a scare so it can graze. This is the Chinese form of the 'vegetable lamb of Tartary' (barometz) that European books later placed on the Volga steppe.

Liu Yu, Record of an Embassy to the West, from Chang De's report (Qiujian ji juan 94 = Yutang jiahua juan 2; Sibu congkan text, Kanseki Repository transcription)
See the original page (p. 094-8b)

About Hearsay about peoples of the far west

Keep in mind

Liu Yu himself closes the list by saying such strange things 'cannot all be recorded'; it is hearsay.

Why it matters

Chinese originals of Bretschneider's Bei shi ji (W112) and of the Xi shi ji (the only Chinese eyewitness of Hülegü's Persian campaign) and Zhang Dehui's route description from Beijing to the Mongol court: earliest Chinese itineraries across Mongolia with place and tribe names.
