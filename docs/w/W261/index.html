Abel-Rémusat's Researches on the Tartar languages

A Paris professor of Chinese defines the 'Tartar' peoples by their languages (Manchu, Mongol, Uighur-Turkic, Tibetan) and reviews what Chinese and European writers said about them, 1820.

Catalogue title Researches on the Tartar languages, or Memoirs on various points of the grammar and literature of the Manchus, Mongols, Uighurs and Tibetans, vol. 1 (only volume published)

Jean-Pierre Abel-Remusat

Jean-Pierre Abel-Rémusat, first professor of Chinese at the Collège de France, published this first (and only) volume of research on the grammar and literature of the Manchus, Mongols, Uighurs and Tibetans in 1820. He never travelled; he worked from Chinese books, Jesuit manuscripts and printed grammars in Paris. His opening chapter asks who the 'Tartars' are and where the name came from, and he surveys Chinese descriptions of northern peoples and the Turkic peoples of Siberia. The corpus has four Google scans of the 1820 Imprimerie royale edition (T0660 to T0663) and his 1832 review of Schmidt's Mongol chronicle (T0664).

Who “Tartar” means here
For Rémusat 'Tartares' is a vulgar, collective European label for the peoples of high Asia, borrowed from the name of one tribe, the Tatars; he sorts them into four 'Tartar races' by language (Manchu-Tungus, Mongol, Turkic, Tibetan).
Why read it
The moment the word 'Tartar' was turned from a map label into a scholarly category, by a scholar who also cleared away older fanciful etymologies.
Watch out
A philologist's book, heavy on grammar; the ethnographic passages are Chinese texts in his translation, and his 'races' reflect early nineteenth-century classification. Google OCR lacks page mapping, so printed pages are read off the scans.

A Chinese portrait of the Tatars: wool tents, hot stones, tree burials

Rémusat translates a Chinese geography, the Kouang-iu-ki, on the Tha-tche (Tatars): they follow rivers and pastures with their herds, dress in hides, make tents of coarse wool, cure illness by burning the sick part with a heated stone, sing and dance at funerals, and some place the corpse in a tree and burn the bones three years later. Rémusat judges such Chinese portraits 'not lacking in accuracy and fidelity'.

The Tha-tche follow the rivers and the places rich in pasture … They make their tents of coarse woollen cloth. … When they are ill, they heat a stone and burn themselves (on the sick part). At funerals they accompany the dead singing and dancing. Some place the corpse in a tree, and three years later they gather the bones and burn them.

The name1820

'Tartar' is a European blanket name borrowed from one tribe

Rémusat opens by defining his subject. The peoples between India, China and Persia, the Sea of Japan, the Caspian and the Arctic are known by the 'vulgar and collective' name of Tartars, a word unknown to many of the peoples it is applied to. It seems to be a corruption of Tatar, the name of one tribe so powerful and famous that it was stretched to cover the others when all of Tartary united under Chinggis Khan's house.

are known by the vulgar and collective name of Tartars. This name, entirely unknown to several of the nations to which Europeans apply it, seems to be an alteration of Tatar, the particular name of a tribe which, by its power and fame, earned the extension of its name to the others.

The name1820

The 'river Tartar' is imaginary

Rémusat reviews earlier guesses about the name Tatar and dismisses them. Bergeron's river Tartar (taken from Matthew Paris) is 'entirely imaginary' in the light of better geography; his Syriac 'abandoned' and lost-tribes theory is 'more untenable still'; Fischer's claim that the Chinese called all neighbours Ta-ta is false; and Muslim writers' ancestor 'Tatar, brother of Mongol' is a genealogical invention modelled on the Bible.

Bergeron proposes, after Matthew Paris, the name of a river Tartar or Tatar, on whose banks the tribe in question lived; but more exact knowledge of the geography of these regions allows us to regard the river Tartar as entirely imaginary.

Russian labels: the Tatars of Tobolsk, Tomsk, the Ob and Krasnoyarsk

Rémusat explains the Siberian 'Tatars': southern Siberia is full of Turkic nations, partly descended from armies the Mongols brought in the thirteenth century and partly older inhabitants. The Russians who conquered them in the late sixteenth century call most of them simply Tatars, adding the name of the town or district where they live: Tatars of Tobolsk, of Tom, of the Ob, of Krasnoyarsk.

The Russians, who subdued them at the end of the sixteenth century, call most of them by the common name of Tatars, to which they add the name of the town or district where they find them settled. Thus they distinguish the Tatars of Tobolsk, of the Tom, of the Ob, of Krasnoyarsk, those of the Chulym

compilation of earlier sources and scholarly analysis: Remusat worked from Paris manuscripts and printed grammars, never travelled; defines "langues tartares" as Manchu, Mongol, Uighur and Tibetan and discusses the ethnonym Tartar

Cautions
  • Relies on Jesuit manuscripts (Amiot, Gerbillon) and on Klaproth; Remusat and Klaproth disputed priority on several points.
  • Remusat also published Observations sur l'Histoire des Mongols orientaux de Sanang-Setsen (1832, IA observationssurl0000rmus, 102 images): a French review of Schmidt's German edition of the Mongolian chronicle; recorded here as companion rather than separate record.

Why it matters

The book that gave "Tartar" its early nineteenth-century philological definition (a language family of Manchu, Mongol, Uighur-Turkic, with Tibetan) and reviewed every prior European statement about Tartar writing; essential for tracing how the word narrowed from geography to linguistics.
