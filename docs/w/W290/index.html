Bergmann's wanderings among the Kalmyks

A Livonian pastor's letters from a year spent living in the felt-tent camps of the Volga Kalmyks, 1802 to 1803, with their laws, legends and history.

Full title Benjamin Bergmann’s Nomadic Wanderings among the Kalmyks in the Years 1802 and 1803, 4 parts (Riga 1804-1805), including translations of the Kalmyk epic Dschangar, the Geser cycle, Kalmyk law and the history of the Torghut flight of 1771

Benjamin Bergmann

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

Benjamin Bergmann, a Baltic German scholar, lived among the Kalmyk hordes of the lower Volga steppe from 1802 to 1803, travelling with a Russian official's party from Sarepta and later with his own tent. His four volumes, written as letters (Riga, 1804 to 1805), describe Kalmyk tents, camps, food, drink, dress, Buddhist festivals and clergy, and translate Kalmyk laws and parts of the Dschangar and Geser epics. He also retells, from Kalmyk informants and written sources, the history of the Oirats and the great flight of the Torghuts from the Volga in 1771. The corpus holds the four German volumes (Bavarian State Library scans) and the 1825 French translation.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
In Bergmann's German, the Tatars are the Muslim Turkic neighbours of the Kalmyks (Astrakhan, Kuban and Crimean Tatars and Nogais), known for their carts, wine, sausages and trade; the Kalmyks themselves are a Mongol people. The 1825 French translation, by contrast, often calls the Kalmyks 'tartares'.
Why read it
It is the closest look any pre-1850 European gives at life inside a Mongol-speaking nomad camp: how the felt tents were built and moved, how the horde was laid out like a town, and how Kalmyks saw their own name and their descent from Chinggis Khan.
Watch out
Bergmann's Kalmyk was imperfect and much came through interpreters and informants; his theories (Huns as Mongols, the bird's-nest origin of the tent) are his own speculation. The 1771 history is retrospective, and the French copy blurs 'Kalmyk' and 'Tartar'.

CitiesKalmyk steppe west of the lower Volga (Kalmykia)May 1802

The horde: a town of 500 felt huts with a bazaar

A Kalmyk horde, Bergmann explains, was laid out like a moving town: the huts stood well apart to leave grazing, with three main quarters, the prince's, the priests' and the market, called bazar in Kalmyk as in Tatar and Russian, and the ordinary huts around them. His horde, of about 500 felt huts, was the chief one on the Volga because the head of all the Dalai Lama's followers in Russia lived there. He adds that since the vice-khan Ubashi led more than 70,000 Kalmyk households out of Russia in 1771, no new khan had been named until recently.

[the market,] which in Kalmyk, as in Tatar and Russian, is called bazar. … The horde in which I am is the foremost of all the Kalmyk hordes on the Volga, not so much because of the number of felt huts, which is about 500, as because the head of all the Kalmyk followers of the Dalai Lama living in Russia has set up his seat in it.

CitiesLower Volga (Astrakhan region)January 1771 (retold 1802 to 1803)

1771: why the Volga towns escaped the Kalmyk flight

Bergmann retells the great flight of the Torghut Kalmyks from the Volga back towards Zungharia in 1771. The common people were told the march would only go as far as the Emba, as a threat to win concessions from Russia, and were promised plunder of Russian property. Because the hordes on the western bank could not cross to join the rest, the Russian towns on that side were saved, and the fleeing Kalmyks contented themselves with plundering the stores of the Russian, Armenian and Tatar merchants in the horde's bazaar.

Whatever prevented the western hordes from crossing saved the Russian towns lying on that side of the Volga. Their destruction would have been unavoidable if the Kalmyks on this side and that had been able to join hands.

Working translation

Original wording

Was den Uebergang der westlichen Horden verhinderte, rettete die russischen Städte, die auf derselben Seite der Wolga lagen. Die Zerstörung derselben wäre unvermeidlich gewesen, wenn die Kalmüken auf dieser und jener Seite im Stande gewesen wären, sich einander die Hände zu bieten.

Benjamin Bergmann

BuildingsKalmyk steppe west of the lower Volga (Kalmykia)May 1802

Felt tents that beat army canvas

Bergmann explains the Kalmyk hut: a lattice wall, a wooden roof ring, poles between them, and felt covers tied on with strong bands of camel wool; when a fire is lit, the felt is simply pulled back from the roof ring to let the smoke out. He insists these huts keep out rain and storm, stay warmer in winter and cooler in summer than soldiers' canvas tents, and are ideal for nomads who rarely stay more than a week in one place, since they come apart easily and travel on camels.

Outside, such huts are covered with felt, and the felts are fastened with strong bands of camel wool. … They withstand rain and gale, are far warmer in winter and protect far better against the heat in summer than ordinary soldiers' tents of sailcloth. … which can be taken apart with little trouble and carried on by camel.

Working translation

Original wording

Auswendig sind dergleichen Hütten mit Filzdecken umzogen, und diese mit starken Bändern von Kameelwolle befestigt. … Sie widerstehen dem Regen und Sturmwinde, halten im Winter weit wärmer, und schützen im Sommer weit mehr gegen die Hitze, als die gewöhnlichen Soldatenzelte von Seegeltuch. … die sich mit leichter Mühe aus einander nehmen, und auf Kameelen weiter fortschaffen lassen.

BuildingsNear the source of the Manych, Kalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)1803

A tomb of cut shell-stone with 'Tatar' letters

Camped near the source of the Manych, Bergmann rode with a Kalmyk guide to some old graves on a ridge. The largest was a rectangle four paces by three, built of smooth blocks more than an arshin long, cut from larger masses, and the next page mentions 'Tatar script characters' on it. The stone was made of packed fossil sea snails, so crumbly it could be rubbed to powder; another grave bore a carved bull's head, and animal bones, some burnt, lay around. On the way back he looked again at rock masses that 'looked like natural walls', which his companion said ran for several versts; they were made of the same fossil shells, and Bergmann supposed the gravestones had been cut from them.

Graves with carved signs suggest that foreign peoples once lived [here]. … Its stones were more than an arshin long, cut smooth from larger masses and laid on top of one another in a rectangle. The rectangle was four paces long and three wide.

Working translation

Original wording

Gräber mit eingehauenen Zeichen, lassen uns den ehemaligen … fremder Völker vermuthen. … Steine davon waren mehr als Arschin lang, … glatt aus größeren Massen gehauen und im … Viereck über einander gelegt. Das Viereck war vier Schritt lang und drey breit.

Benjamin Bergmann

Daily lifeKalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)1803

'We get drunk every day': a mare's-milk spirit party

At a drinking bout with Kalmyk clergy, Bergmann watched a boy of ten or twelve drain five bowls and a sixty-year-old gelung twelve. He noticed for the first time that fermented mare's milk (tschigan, which the Tatars call kumiss) was intoxicating even before distilling: the drinkers began to speak Tatar and Russian and even scraps of German. When he asked whether such parties were frequent, the old monk answered flatly that they got drunk every day, and the others said it was a kind of medicine. Bergmann doubted it and blamed the drink for the eye troubles common among Kalmyks.

The Kalmyk drinkers were all so full of the fumes of this drink that they began to speak Tatar and Russian, and some even to throw about scraps of German. … I asked whether such tschigan parties were often held here. The old man answered me quite categorically: 'We get drunk every day.'

Working translation

Original wording

Die kalmükischen Trinker waren alle so voll von den Dünsten dieses Getränks, daß sie Tatarisch und Russisch zu sprechen, und einige sogar deutsche Brocken um sich zu werfen anfingen. … Ich fragte, ob man hier öfters dergleichen Tschigangelage anzustellen pflegte. Der Alte antwortete mir ganz kathegorisch: „Wir besaufen uns alle Tage.”

Benjamin Bergmann

Daily lifeKalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)Law code of the 17th century, translated 1802 to 1803

Fines counted in nines of cattle

Bergmann translates the old Kalmyk law code, the 'Ssadschin Bitschik' (the Mongol-Oirat code). Penalties are counted in head of livestock, usually in multiples of nine: whoever forgets himself towards father, mother or teacher pays three nines, two nines or one nine; a daughter-in-law who wrongs her parents-in-law pays three nines and, if she struck them, is flogged. Thieves lose cattle and are flogged and branded, and anyone who turns to sorcerers must give his best horse to the informer.

According to the Tsaadzhin Bichig, whoever forgets himself towards father, mother or teacher pays, according to the nature of the offence, 3 times 9, 2 times 9, or just 9 head of cattle. … Thieves shall be punished by loss of cattle, by lashes and by branding. Whoever takes refuge with sorcerers or sorceresses shall give his best horse to the informer.

Working translation

Original wording

Nach dem Ssadschist Bitschick zahlt derjenige, welcher sich gegen Vater, Mutter, Lehrer vergißt, nach Beschaffenheit des Vergehens, 3 mal 9, 2 mal 9, oder bloß 9 Stück Vieh. … Diebe sollen durch Verlust an Vieh, durch Geißelhiebe und Brandmal bestraft werden. Wer zu Zauberern und Zauberinnen seine Zuflucht nimmt, soll sein bestes Pferd dem Angeber hingeben.

Benjamin Bergmann, summarising the Kalmyk law code

The nameKalmyk steppe west of the lower Volga (Kalmykia)1802

'Kalmyk': a Tatar word for those who stayed behind, or turned away

Bergmann spells the name 'Kalmük' after the pronunciation of the Russians and Tatars, because the name itself comes from Tatar. The Kalmyks once called themselves Oelöt (Oirats). He rejects Strahlenberg's idea that the name comes from kalpak, a cap, and follows the Khivan historian Abulghazi: the Tatar word means either 'those left behind' or 'apostates'. On the next page he guesses that Tatars and Mongols once shared one religion, and that when the Mongols turned to Lamaist Buddhism the Muslim Tatars called them apostates.

Far more likely is the derivation from the word khalimak. Abulghazi himself seems to confirm this by giving this people the name Khalmak. According to this Tatar expression, the name Kalmyk means either apostates or those who remained behind.

Working translation

Original wording

Weit wahrscheinlicher ist die Herleitung von … Worte Chalimäk. Abulhasi selbst scheint das dadurch zu bestätigen, daß er diesem Volke den Namen Khalmak beilegt. Dem eben angeführten tatarischen Ausdrucke nach, bezeichnet der Name Kalmük entweder Abtrünnige oder Nachgebliebene.

Benjamin Bergmann

The name5th century (Priscus), argued 1802

Attila's court, seen as a Kalmyk khan's household

Bergmann argues that the Huns were a Mongol people. He compares Jordanes' description of Hunnic faces with the Kalmyks, and says that reading Priscus' account of his embassy to Attila feels like entering a Kalmyk khan's dwelling: the ruler on a raised seat facing the door, guests ranked on his right and left, and Attila eating from a wooden bowl while his guests used silver and gold. He goes on to explain Hunnic names from Mongol words.

The envoy sees him opposite the entrance on a raised couch, just as among the Kalmyks and all Mongol peoples. The envoys sit, as among the Kalmyks, on both sides of the prince, the most distinguished on the right, the others on the left. … but Attila follows the custom of his people when he eats the meat offered to him from a wooden bowl and drinks from a wooden cup.

Working translation

Original wording

Der Gesandte erblickt ihn dem Eingange gegenüber auf einem erhabenen Lager; ganz wie bey den Kalmüken und bey allen mongolischen Völkerschaften. Die Gesandten setzen sich, wie bey den Kalmüken, zu beiden Seiten des Fürsten, die vornehmsten rechts, die übrigen links. … aber Attila folgt der Sitte seines Volks, wenn er das dargebrachte Fleisch aus einer hölzernen Schaale speist, und sein Getränk aus einem hölzernen Becher trinkt.

Benjamin Bergmann, paraphrasing Priscus

The nameKalmyk steppe west of the lower Volga (Kalmykia)1802 to 1803

Proud heirs of Chinggis Khan

Bergmann reports that the Kalmyks counted themselves one people with the Mongols, remembered with pleasure the deeds of their forefathers under Chinggis and his successors, and that most of their princes traced their blood to him. No Kalmyk doubted that the heirs of that heroic stock could match those deeds again if there were more of them. He then records a Kalmyk asking him whether the Prussians had not been defeated by the Kalmyks, since some 4,000 had served in the Russian army against Prussia.

The Kalmyks count themselves as one [Mongol] people. They think with pleasure of the deeds of their ancestors under Chinggis and his successors. Most of the present Kalmyk princes honour in themselves the blood of that great hero from whom they derive their descent. No Kalmyk doubts that the descendants of the Mongol heroic line could equal the deeds of old, if greater numbers backed up their courage.

Original wording

Die Kalmüken rechnen sich zu einem … Volke. Sie denken mit Wohlgefallen an die Thaten ihrer Vorältern unter Tschingis und dessen Nachfolgern. Der größte Theil der jetzigen Kalmükenfürsten ehrt in sich selbst das Blut jenes großen Helden, von welchem sie ihre Abkunft herleiten. Kein Kalmük zweifelt daran daß die Nachkommen des mongolischen Heldenstamms, die ehemaligen Thaten erreichen könnten, wenn eine größere Anzahl ihren Muth unterstützte.

Strange talesKalmyk steppe west of the lower Volga (Kalmykia)May 1802

Was the yurt copied from a bird's nest?

Bergmann offers his own 'hypothesis' about how the Mongol peoples invented the felt tent. Living in treeless country, they could neither dig caves nor build huts of branches, so perhaps one of them copied the hanging, bag-shaped nest that the penduline tit weaves from plant wool. Because a house cannot hang from a branch, the Mongols set it on the ground and put the door at the bottom. He presents this frankly as a guess.

This remarkable bird makes from a kind of wool an oblong bag, which it fastens to twigs so skilfully that neither wind nor weather can damage its hanging home. This method, I fancy, the forefathers of the Kalmyks used, to make huts for themselves after the example of the remez [penduline tit], which they afterwards tried to perfect more and more and to support ever better with woodwork.

Working translation

Original wording

Dieser merkwürdige Vogel bereitet aus einer Art von Wolle, einen länglichen Sack, den er so geschickt an Zweige befestigt, daß weder Wind noch Wetter im Stande sind, seine schwebende Wohnung zu beschädigen. Diese Methode, dünkt mich, benutzten die Voreltern der Kalmüken, um sich nach dem Beispiele des Remes Hütten einzurichten, die sie in der Folge immer mehr zu vervollkommnen und mit Holzwerk immer besser zu stützen suchten.

Benjamin Bergmann

Strange talesKalmyk steppe, east of the Volga1771 (heard 1802 to 1803)

The captain bound in raw cowhide

During the 1771 flight the Kalmyks captured a detachment under Captain Dudin, and, Bergmann says, Ubashi had the officer's head and hands bound in strips of fresh cowhide, which tightened as they dried. Bergmann heard this from one of Dudin's own Cossacks, a Persian by birth, who later escaped from Kalmyk and then Kazakh captivity and saw his officer two months after his capture, still bound and awaiting death. What became of Dudin was never known.

One of his Cossacks, a Persian by birth, from whose own mouth I learned of this act of cruelty, saw his captain again two months after his capture, with the strips of cowhide round his head and hands, in the most terrible state, expecting death at every moment, though death seemed to flee him.

Working translation

Original wording

Einer seiner Kosaken, ein gebohrner Perser, … aus dessen eigenem Munde ich diesen Zug von Grausamkeit erfahren habe: sah seinen Rittmeister zwey Monate nach der Gefangenschaft wieder, mit den Streifen von Kuhhaut um Kopf und Hände, in dem schrecklichsten Zustande, alle Augenblicke seinen Tod erwartend, der ihn zu fliehen schien.

Benjamin Bergmann, from an eyewitness Cossack
