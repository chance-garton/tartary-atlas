The Cuman Codex: a phrasebook from the Golden Horde

A Latin-Persian-Cuman word list made by Italian merchants in 1303, with Franciscan prayers and riddles in the steppe Turkic language.

Full title The Cuman Codex: Latin-Persian-Cuman glossary (c.1294-1330) and German-Cuman religious texts and riddles (c.1330-1340), first complete edition, Budapest 1880

Codex Cumanicus

Anonymous Italian merchants and German Franciscans in the Golden Horde; Count Geza Kuun (editor)

Read the 13 passagesOpen the original book

The Codex Cumanicus is a small manuscript now in Venice. Its first part, dated 11 July 1303, is a Latin-Persian-Cuman glossary compiled by Italian (probably Genoese) merchants trading in Crimea and the Golden Horde; its second part, of about 1330 to 1340, holds German Franciscan missionaries' Cuman translations of prayers and hymns, and a set of Cuman riddles. Cuman (Qipchaq) was the Turkic language of the Horde's steppe population. The corpus copy is Count Geza Kuun's first complete edition (Budapest 1880), whose long Latin introduction gathers medieval testimony about the Cumans and their being called 'Tartars'.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
In the Codex's own Cuman, 'Tatar' means 'Turkic', and the 'Tatar tongue' is the Qipchaq language itself. In Kuun's introduction, medieval Latin and Hungarian writers use 'Tartar' both for the Mongol invaders and, loosely, for the Cumans and for their Islam ('the Tartar rite').
Why read it
It records the everyday words of the Golden Horde's people (for yurt, city, palace, court, shaman) as written down by outsiders who lived among them, and shows 'Tatar' in use as the name of a real Turkic language.
Watch out
The manuscript is a working phrasebook, so it gives words, not descriptions. Kuun's 1880 readings were corrected by later scholars (Radloff, Bang, Gronbech), and much of the quoted material here comes from Kuun's introduction, which assembles earlier authors.

CitiesCrimea (Kaffa or Solkhat)1303

City, castle, palace: the merchants' town vocabulary

The glossary gives, column by column, Latin, Persian and Cuman words for places. A city (civitas) is 'saar' or 'chent' in Cuman, a castle is 'kalaa', a palace is 'saray', and a house is 'eu' (ev). The words for city, castle and palace are loans from Persian and Arabic (shahr, qal'a, saray), next to the Turkic 'kent' and 'ev'. The Italian merchants clearly needed these words for dealing with towns in the Horde's lands.

city: (Persian) shahr / (Cuman) shahr or kent; castle: (Persian) qal'a / (Cuman) qal'a; village: (Persian) deh / (Cuman) qala; palace: saray / saray; house: (Persian) khana / (Cuman) ev.

ciuitas ghachar saar ul chent castrum kala kalaa casale de gala palacium saray saray domus ghana eu

CitiesGolden Horde1303

The court is the 'orda', the queen is the 'khatun'

A page of ranks and offices shows how the Horde's court looked to Italian traders. Latin 'curia' (court) is Persian 'ordu' and Cuman 'orda', the word behind English 'horde'. An empress is 'khan khatun', a queen 'sultan khatun', a lord is 'bey' or 'khoja'. The khan's court, the orda, was the capital in the steppe.

lord: (Persian) khwaja / (Cuman) bey or khoja; crown: taj / tagz; court: (Persian) ordu / (Cuman) orda; empress: (Persian) wife of the padishah / (Cuman) khan khatun; queen: (Persian) wife of the king / (Cuman) sultan khatun.

CitiesSarai (lower Volga)1337

A friar goes to Sarai to learn the language

Kuun cites the Franciscan Pascal of Vitoria, who in 1337 went to the 'ancient city of Sarai on the bank of the Volga' to learn Turkish among the Cumans and studied it there for a whole year. Sarai was the capital of the Golden Horde, and the story shows missionaries living in the Horde's own city.

Friar Pascal of Vitoria in 1337, as he himself says, hurried to the Cumans in the ancient city of Sarai on the bank of the Volga in order to learn the Turkish language, and there worked as hard as he could at the study of the language for one whole year.

Fra Pascal de Victoria anno 1337 in antiquam urbem Serai ad ripam Volgae, uti ipse ait, linguae turcicae ediscendae causa ad Cumanos contendit et illic per unum annum integrum studiis linguae quam maxime operam navabat.

Geza Kuun, Latin introduction to his edition of the Codex Cumanicus (Budapest 1880), citing a letter of Pascal of Vitoria

BuildingsHungaryc.1237

'They have tents, you have castles and cities': a Mongol threat

Kuun quotes a letter from a Mongol commander to King Bela IV of Hungary, brought back by the Dominican Friar Julian. The Mongol calls the Cumans who fled into Hungary 'our slaves' and warns the king: the Cumans live without houses and move about with tents, so they might escape, but a king who lives in houses and has castles and cities cannot. It is a clear contemporary statement of the difference between steppe tents and settled towns, seen from the Mongol side.

For it is easier for the Cumans to escape than for you, because they, going about without houses, with tents, may perhaps escape. But you, living in houses, have castles and cities: how will you escape my hands?

Working translation

Original wording

Facilius enim est Cumanis evadere quam tibi, quia illi sine domibus cum tentoriis ambulantes possunt forsitan evadere. Tu autem in domibus habitans habes castra et civitates, quomodo effugies manus meas?

A Mongol commander's letter to King Bela IV (c.1237), as brought back by Friar Julian; quoted by Geza Kuun (1880)

Keep in mind

BuildingsCrimea1303

The word for a dwelling was 'yurt'

In the merchants' glossary the Latin word 'mansio' (dwelling, lodging) is given in Persian as 'manzel' and in Cuman as 'yurt'. Kuun adds that the word is widespread across the Turkic languages and compares the Mongol 'ger' (house). The steppe word for home and camp-place was the same word English later borrowed for the felt tent.

dwelling (Latin) / manzel (Persian) / yurt (Cuman) … A word widespread in the domain of the languages of Turkic origin. Compare Mongolian ger, 'house', 'dwelling'.

Working translation

Original wording

mansio manxel yurt … Vox late patens in dominio linguarum turcicae originis. Cf. mongolicum ger «domus», «habitatio»

Codex Cumanicus, Latin-Persian-Cuman glossary (Italian part, dated 11 July 1303), ed. Geza Kuun 1880, with Kuun's footnote

Daily lifeCrimea1303 (later annotation)

A sorceress is a 'kam khatun', a shaman woman

Under the verb 'to divine' the glossary gives the Cuman 'kamlik etermen', literally 'I do shamaning', and a later hand added 'incantatrix' (enchantress) as 'kam katun kisi', a shaman-woman. 'Kam' is the old Turkic word for a shaman. The steppe people living around the Italian colonies still had words for shamans and women diviners.

I divine: (Persian) ram mikunam / (Cuman) kamlik etermen, (also) ranzilik etermen … enchantress: she is a kam khatun person (a shaman woman).

Working translation

Original wording

adiuino ram mecunem kamlik etermen ranzilic etarmen … incantatrix kam katun kisi dir

Daily lifeGolden Horde (Crimea or Sarai)c.1330 to 1340

The Lord's Prayer in the language of the Horde

The German Franciscan part of the Codex contains Christian texts turned into Cuman for preaching. This is the start of the Lord's Prayer: 'Our father who art in heaven, blessed be thy kingdom, thy will be done as in heaven so on earth, give us this day our daily bread'. Elsewhere in the Codex the word for God is 'Tengri', the old Turkic name of the sky god, as Kuun's word index shows.

Our father who art in heaven. Blessed be thy kingdom (khanate). Thy will be done, as in heaven so on earth. Give us today our daily bread.

Atamis kim köctä sen. Algiszle bulsun senig hanlechin. bulsû senig tilemegin nezikkim kocta alley ierda. kundegi ötmackimisni bisga bugun bergil.

Codex Cumanicus, German (Franciscan) part, c.1330 to 1340, ed. Geza Kuun 1880

Daily lifePontic steppec.1330 to 1340

A riddle: 'like a white tent, with no door'

The Codex preserves Cuman riddles, the oldest recorded Turkic riddles. This one runs: 'Like a white tent, it has no mouth. That is the egg.' Kuun reads 'akkoy' as 'ak ev', literally 'white house', and renders it 'the tent of a new bride'. The riddle draws on the everyday sight of a tent, the home of the people who told it.

Like a white tent (ak ev), it has no mouth. That is the egg.

Working translation

Original wording

[6,] akkoy mania avzu joh. ol jumurtka.

Codex Cumanicus, German part (riddle 6), ed. Geza Kuun 1880

Daily lifeGolden Horde14th century

One language and one script 'in all these kingdoms'

Kuun quotes a Franciscan missionary from the order's annals: he decided first to learn the language of the land, and 'by the grace of God' learned Cuman and the Uighur script, which were used in common 'throughout all these kingdoms'. It shows the Horde as a literate world with a shared Turkic language and an old Uighur script, not a land without writing.

But I, after taking counsel, wished first to learn the language of the land, and by the grace of God I learned the Cuman language and the Uighur script, which language and script are used in common throughout all these kingdoms.

An anonymous Franciscan missionary, quoted by Geza Kuun (1880) from the annals of the Franciscan order

The nameCrimeac.1303 to 1340

In the Cumans' own language, 'Tatar' simply meant 'Turkic'

Kuun's index of the Cuman words in the Codex gives the entry 'Tatar' as meaning 'a Turk' or 'Turkish', and the phrase 'tatar til' ('Tatar tongue') as meaning plain, sincere speech. So the merchants and friars who compiled the book heard the steppe people use 'Tatar' for their own Turkic language, not for a lost empire. The phrase occurs on page 229 of the Codex.

Tatar: 'a Turk', 'Turkish'; tatar til: 'honest (plain) speech', page 229.

Working translation

Original wording

Tatar «Turca», «turcicus», tatar til «sermo sincerus» pag. 229.

Geza Kuun, Cuman word index to the Codex Cumanicus (1880)

The nameHungary1129 and later

Hungarian hymns called the Cumans 'Tartars'

Kuun shows how loosely the name was used in medieval Hungary. Hymns to St Ladislaus sometimes call the Cumans 'Tartars', and the chronicler Thuroczy says the chief of the Cumans whom King Stephen II took in (in 1129) was himself named Tatar. This is more than a century before the Mongol invasion of 1241, so 'Tatar' here is a personal name and a label for steppe Turks, not for the Mongols.

In the old Latin and Hungarian hymns of St Ladislaus the Cumans are sometimes called Tartars … Thuroczy, in his Chronicle, likewise names the supreme chief of the Cumans received into Hungary by King Stephen II 'Tatar': 'King Stephen loved the Cumans at that time; their chief, named Tatar, had fled with a few men to the king from the slaughter by the (Greek) emperor'.

Working translation

Original wording

In antiquis hymnis latinis et hungaricis sancti Ladislai Cumani interdum Tartari nuncupantur … Thuroczy in suis Chronicis, Cumanorum a rege Stephano II in Hungariam receptorum summum ducem itidem Tatar nominat: «Rex autem Stephanus, diligebat Cumanos tunc temporis; quorum dux nomine Tatar, qui a cede imperatoris (Graecorum), cum paucis, ad regem fugerat»

Geza Kuun, Latin introduction to his edition of the Codex Cumanicus (Budapest 1880), quoting the Hungarian chronicler Janos Thuroczy (1488)

The nameHungary14th century (reign of Louis I, 1342 to 1382)

Islam among the Cumans was called 'the Tartar rite'

Kuun notes that Islam had spread among the Cumans and that later Latin church writers called their religion 'the Tartar rite'. He quotes John, deacon of Kukullo, saying that King Louis I of Hungary had the Cuman people, 'wrapped in the Tartar rite', converted to Catholicism. So in church Latin 'Tartar' could simply mean 'Muslim steppe people'.

The Muhammadan religion … spread also among the Cumans, and later Latin church writers called this religion of the Cumans 'the Tartar rite', for example John, deacon of Kukullo: 'He (namely Louis I, king of Hungary) also had the Cuman people, wrapped in the Tartar rite, converted to the Catholic faith'.

Working translation

Original wording

Religio Muhammedanica … etiam apud Cumanos propagata fuit, eam Cumanorum religionem scriptores ecclesiastici latini posteriores «ritum tartaricum» nominabant, ita e. g. Joannes diaconus Küküllőiensis: «Hic etiam gentem Chomanorum, ritui Tartarico involutam … ad fidem Catholicam converti procuravit (scilicet Ludovicus I. rex Hungariae)»

Geza Kuun, Latin introduction to his edition of the Codex Cumanicus (Budapest 1880), quoting John of Kukullo (14th century)

Strange talesMoravia (Czech Republic)claim printed 1677

Inscribed Cuman tombstones in a Moravian church? The editor doubts it

Kuun quotes the Czech Jesuit Bohuslav Balbin, who said that near Ivancice and the Oslavany convent there was a church whose floor was paved almost entirely with Cuman tombs and stones inscribed in their letters, 'as I saw myself'. Kuun says plainly that he hardly believes it. It is a good example of a striking claim reported and then doubted by the editor himself.

'There survives even today some memory of the barbarians not far from Ivancice and the Oslavany convent: a notable Christian church in which very many tombs of the Cumans and stones carved with their letters are to be seen, with which stones almost the whole floor of the church is paved, as I saw myself.' For my part I would hardly think this story of Bohuslav's true.

Working translation

Original wording

«Superest hodieque aliqua barbarorum memoria non procul Evanczicio et Oslovanensi Coenobio, insigne templum Christianorum, in quo plurima Cumanorum sepulchra et saxa litteris illorum incisa spectantur, quibus saxis totum fere Templi solum stratum est, ut ipse vidi.» Ceterum hanc Bohuslavi narrationem ego quidem vix veram esse putaverim.

Geza Kuun, Latin introduction to his edition of the Codex Cumanicus (Budapest 1880), quoting Bohuslav Balbin, Epitome historica rerum Bohemicarum (Prague 1677)
