Gerard's account of Kinnaur and the 'Tartars' of the Tibetan frontier

A Scottish surveyor's first-hand description of the Himalayan valley of Kinnaur and its Tibetan-speaking 'Tartar' neighbours, 1817 to 1821.

Full title Account of Koonawur, in the Himalaya

Alexander Gerard

Read the 13 passagesOpen the original book

Alexander Gerard (1792-1839) was an East India Company surveyor who, with his brothers James and Patrick, mapped the upper Sutlej valley (Koonawur, now Kinnaur in Himachal Pradesh) between 1817 and 1821 and pushed repeatedly to the closed Chinese-Tibetan frontier. This posthumous book, edited by George Lloyd and printed in London in 1841, has a general account of the country followed by his survey journals. A whole book of it is 'Remarks upon that part of Koonawur inhabited by Tartars': the Tibetan-speaking Buddhist villages of upper Kinnaur and Spiti, and the lands under Chinese rule beyond. Gerard learned some of their language and collected about 1,500 words.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Tibetans and Tibetan-speaking Buddhist highlanders of upper Kinnaur, Spiti, Ladakh and western Tibet ('Chinese Tartary'). Gerard himself notes that neither 'Tartar' nor 'Tibet' was used locally.
Why read it
An unusually warm eyewitness portrait that deliberately contradicts the 'ferocious Tartar' image, with precise notes on stone and sun-dried brick houses, prayer walls, prayer wheels, butter tea and the closed Chinese frontier.
Watch out
'Tartar' here is a British label for Tibetans, not steppe nomads. Gerard admits he may have taken 'too favourable a view'. Some information on places he could not reach (Garoo, Yarkand, Lake Manasarovar) comes from informants.

CitiesGartok, western Tibetc. 1818 to 1821 (reported)

Gartok: a famous market town of black tents

Gartok (Garoo), the chief wool mart of western Tibet, is not a town of houses at all but a camp of black tents occupied for six months of the year. In winter the people move downstream and the Chinese governors live in a fort. Each July a fair of 10,000 to 12,000 people gathers there. Gerard never got there; this comes from informants.

Is a collection of black tents inhabited by pastoral tribes for six months. In winter, the Tartars retire chiefly to Eegoong on the bank of the river, two stages down the stream, and the Chinese governors reside at the Fort of Tuzheegung, where they have houses. Garoo is the most famous mart for wool in Chinese Tartary

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

CitiesYarkandc. 1820 (reported)

Yarkand, a magnificent city 'under the Chinese'

Gerard passes on what he was told about Yarkand, forty days north of Leh. It is described as a large, magnificent city of lofty buildings, ruled by China, where the people are 'Moguls' who speak Turkish and Russian merchants often come. He had not seen it himself.

Yarkund is described as being a large and magnificent city, containing many splendid and lofty buildings ; it is under the Chinese, and the inhabitants are Moguls, and speak the Turkish language. It is said that Russian merchants often visit Yarkund.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

CitiesLehc. 1820

Leh: a town of a thousand houses

Gerard describes Leh, the capital of Ladakh, standing a few miles from the Indus, as a town of nearly 1,000 houses built like those of Spiti, with Muslim and Hindu shopkeepers but mostly Tartar (Tibetan) people. It holds fairs attended by Punjabis, Yarkandis, Kinnauris and Kashmiris.

… contains nearly 1,000 houses, built like those in Speetee. There are some Mahommedan and Hindoo shop-keepers, but the greater part of the inhabitants are Tartars.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

BuildingsKinnaurc. 1818 to 1821

Prayer walls you must always pass on the right

Gerard describes mani walls: long dykes of loose, uncemented stone, topped with slates carved with the prayer 'Om mani padme hum'. The Tartars always keep them on the right hand, even if it means a detour of a quarter of a mile. On nearby pages he describes chortens, prayer flags on every house, and prayer wheels turned by water and wind.

the most common are tumuli, named Mane, consisting of a dyke of different lengths from ten to two hundred feet, two broad, and three or four high; they are constructed of loose stones without cement, and upon their tops are numerous pieces of slate of all shapes and sizes, carved with sentences in the Oochen or sacred character.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

BuildingsSpitic. 1818 to 1821

Houses of stone below and sun-dried brick above

Gerard describes the flat-roofed, two-storey houses of Spiti: stone on the ground floor and unburnt (sun-dried) brick on the upper floor, which he hears is the rule across Ladakh too. In a footnote elsewhere he explains that the climate is so dry that such brick houses last as well as stone. Firewood is so scarce it is brought by yak and stacked on the roofs.

The lower half of the houses here, and I have heard, throughout most part of Ludak, is built of stone, and the upper of unburnt bricks : they are two stories high, and flat roofed.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

BuildingsSunnam (Soongnum), Kinnaurc. 1818 to 1821

A nine-foot prayer wheel worked by a winch

At Soongnum Gerard saw three prayer cylinders kept turning by water, and at Nisung others turned by the wind. The largest, about nine feet high, stood in a square house with a wooden dome, lined with China silk, and needed two people to work it with ropes and a winch.

At Soongnum there are three cylinders kept constantly in motion by water, on the same principle as the water mills, and at Nisung several are turned by the wind.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Daily lifeSpitic. 1818 to 1821

'Very far removed from the ferocity' of the Tartar legend

Gerard spent days travelling with boys from Tibetan villages as guides and describes them with real affection. He directly rejects the reputation for ferocity that the word Tartar carried in Europe. When he fell ill with rheumatism at Peenoo in Spiti, the villagers brought medicine, sugar, spices and tea.

They are of a mild and benevolent disposition, very far removed from the ferocity commonly attached to the character of a Tartar. I have had many instances of their humanity. At Peenoo, in Speetee, where I was confined to my bed for two days with rheumatism, I never experienced more attention

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Daily lifeSpitic. 1818 to 1821

'I'll make you a real Tartar': travelling on sheep

When Gerard could not find porters for his grain on the empty high plateau, his friend Putee Ram brought a flock of sheep. The trick was to load the grain on the sheep, eat the grain first, and then eat the sheep. It is a neat picture of how people actually crossed the high Tibetan uplands.

I'll make you a real Tartar: you have grain for ten or twelve days, and now you have no use for porters; load the sheep with the grain, finish it first, and then kill and eat the sheep : this is the way we travel on the uninhabitable tracts ; we never think of grain as long as we have plenty of sheep.

Putee Ram of Soongnum, quoted by Alexander Gerard (footnote)

Daily lifeKinnaurc. 1818 to 1821

Tea that tastes like soup

Tibetan butter tea, bought at the Gartok fair, is made with butter, salt, spices and a little meal instead of milk and sugar. Gerard grew to love it. Every traveller arriving at a Tartar village was offered a dish of it, and it was kept ready most of the day.

During my stay at Soongnum, Putee Ram insisted on bringing me daily tea, prepared after the Tartar fashion, which tastes like soup, and I took a great liking to it. The Tartars drink it in large quantities ; and when they arrive at their encamping ground, their first occupation is to make the tea

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Daily lifePin valley, Spitic. 1818 to 1821

A frontier chief who refused 150 rupees

At Peenoo in Spiti, Gerard asked the headman (styled Lafa) for leave to return by the Taree pass. The man sent back his 150 rupees and said the travellers were welcome to goats, sheep and blankets, but not to that road, though he would not fight them. Gerard admired his firmness and goes on to say that the Tartars of the Chinese frontier were as strict about strangers as officials at Peking; he had been turned back at four different points the year before.

The Chinese Tartars, on this remote frontier of their vast empire, are just as vigilant respecting the non-admission of strangers as their countrymen at Pekin : no sum of money, however great, will bribe them to infringe the orders of their superiors.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Daily lifeKinnaurc. 1818 to 1821

Brothers sharing one wife

Explaining his population figures, Gerard notes that polyandry (several husbands for one wife) was common in Kinnaur and in 'Chinese Tartary'. His footnote adds that so many people became celibate monks and nuns that in some villages they were almost half the population.

Polyandry also exists in Chinese Tartary and in the hilly tracts towards the plains. Besides this drawback on the increase of the population, there is another peculiar to Chinese Tartary and the adjoining countries ; that is, celibacy, which is professed by numbers of the inhabitants : and in some villages the monks and nuns form almost half the population.

Alexander Gerard (footnote; ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

The nameKinnaurc. 1818 to 1821

'Tartar or Tatar is unknown here, and so is Tibet'

Gerard lists what the people actually call themselves and their land: Bhoteeas or Zhads to the lowlanders, Bhot for the country, Keao for the people of Gnaree, and he notes that Zhad is an insult. Then he says flatly that neither 'Tartar' nor 'Tibet' was known there. 'Tartar' was the European name he was using for convenience.

The Tartars are called by the inhabitants of the lower parts, Zhads, Bhoteeas or Bootuntees, and their country is often named Bhot and Bootunt … Zhad is reckoned a term of reproach, and is applied by the Hindoos to those who eat bullock's flesh … Tartar or Tatar is unknown here, and so is Tibet.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)

Strange talesKinnaurc. 1818 to 1821

Flags that frighten away hobgoblins

At the corner of almost every Tartar house flew a long flag printed with 'Om mani padme hum' and topped with a tuft of black yak hair. Gerard reports that these flags and the stone piles on the roofs were believed to scare off ghosts and evil spirits.

The Durchuts and Lapchas are considered efficacious in frightening away ghosts, hobgoblins, and evil spirits, which are universally believed in.

Alexander Gerard (ed. George Lloyd, 1841)
