Mailla's General History of China, translated from the Chinese annals

A Peking Jesuit's multi-volume French version of the Chinese annals, including the Mongol (Yuan) and Manchu (Qing) conquests, printed in Paris 1777 to 1785.

Catalogue title General history of China, or Annals of that empire, translated from the Tongjian gangmu (13 vols, 1777-1785); vols 9 and 11 cover the Yuan (Mongol) and Qing (Manchu) periods

Joseph de Mailla, translator; Grosier and Deshauterayes, editors

Joseph de Mailla, a French Jesuit in Peking from 1701 to 1748, translated the great Chinese chronicle Tongjian gangmu (from its Manchu version) and continued it through the Ming and early Qing from other Chinese works. The abbés Grosier and Deshauterayes edited it in Paris long after his death, adding notes; some notes on the Qing emperors' journeys into Mongolia draw on Jesuit travel journals. Mailla saw Peking and parts of Tartary, but the history itself is translation and compilation. This corpus has volumes 1, 9, 10, 11 and 12 of the 1777 to 1785 Paris edition (Pierres and Clousier).

Who “Tartar” means here
'Tartares' covers every northern people in the Chinese record: the Mongols (Mongous) of the Yuan, the Khitan and Jurchen, the Khalkha and other Mongol princes of Kangxi's day, and above all the Manchus (Mantchéous) who conquered China in 1644.
Why read it
It gives the Chinese court's view of two 'Tartar' conquests of China, and some vivid editor's notes on Qing emperors meeting Mongol princes in the steppe.
Watch out
A translation of a translation, heavily edited and annotated in Paris; the Chinese annals are dynastic and moralising. Editorial notes are not Mailla's and are labelled here.

CitiesKarakorum1368

The last Yuan heir falls back to Karakorum

In the summary of the fall of the Mongol dynasty, the Ming founder takes Tatou (Dadu, the Mongol capital at Beijing), which the last Yuan emperor had abandoned, and the heir retreats to Holin, 'or Caracorom', founding a new dynasty in Tartary, the Northern Yuan. Karakorum here is still a working Mongol capital more than a century after its founding.

The heir prince, son of this fugitive Emperor, withdrew to Holin or Karakorum, and founded in Tartary a new dynasty called the Northern Yuan

BuildingsHohhot1696

Mongol farmers in earthen houses near Hohhot

Following the Kangxi emperor's march of 1696, the note describes hamlets of a few earthen houses lived in by Mongols who farmed the nearby fields, lining the road to greet the emperor with sheep, butter and cream. Near Kouei-hoa-tching (Hohhot) the garrison knelt on the road and some two hundred lamas in ceremonial dress, with rough musical instruments and banners, waited at the main temple. Not every 'Tartar' lived in a tent.

We passed several hamlets made up of a few earthen houses, inhabited by Mongols who farm the neighbouring fields. These poor inhabitants lined up along the road to greet the emperor, offering him sheep, butter, cream and various fragrant woods.

Daily lifeDolon Nor, Inner Mongolia1691

Khalkha princes spellbound by puppets at the emperor's feast

An editor's note describes the great assembly at which the Khalkha khans submitted to the Kangxi emperor. At the feast, rope-dancers and puppets played 'much as in Europe', and the Khalkhas, who had never seen such a show, forgot to eat; only the Grand Lama stayed grave. The emperor then dressed the princes in Manchu robes, which they wore from then on before him.

The feast was interspersed with entertainments by rope-dancers, who performed various feats of agility. There were also puppets, which played much as in Europe. The Khalkhas, who had never seen this spectacle, forgot to eat, so much did it amaze them. The Grand Lama appeared untouched by these frivolities

The nameChina1644 onward

The Manchu 'Tartars' take China and change nothing

After the rebel Li Zicheng's defeat, a Manchu prince led an army 'of Tartars and many Chinese' through the provinces. The annals, as rendered here, claim the conquerors followed their emperor's line, kept the old tribunals in Peking and simply doubled the posts so that Tartars could hold half; 'China, in changing masters, changed neither its form nor its government'. The passage shows 'Tartar' used plainly for the Manchus.

One of the Manchu princes put himself at the head of a formidable army made up of Tartars and a great number of Chinese … China, in changing masters, changed neither its form nor its government.

Cautions
  • Mailla translated a Manchu version and interpolated; Grosier edited posthumously; the Qing section (vol. 11) is Mailla's own compilation, not the Gangmu. English pass W098 (Grosier) is the abridged companion.
  • Further copies: archive.org/details/A209053

Why it matters

The only full pre-1850 European rendering of the Chinese annalistic record for the Mongol and Manchu dynasties: it is where French readers got the Chinese view of the Yuan and of the Qing conquest of Mongolia and Dzungaria. Grosier's Description (W098) is its companion volume.
