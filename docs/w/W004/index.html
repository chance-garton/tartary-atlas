Captain John Perry on the state of Peter the Great's Russia

An English canal engineer's report on Russia, 1698 to 1712, with eyewitness notes on the Kalmyks and Tartars of the Volga.

Catalogue title The State of Russia under the Present Czar

John Perry

John Perry (1670-1732), an English naval engineer, was hired by Peter the Great in 1698 and spent some fourteen years in Russia, above all trying to cut a canal between the Volga and the Don at Kamyshin, and later at Voronezh. His book (London, 1716) is mostly about Peter's reforms, the navy and Russian customs, but its survey of the tsar's lands describes the Kalmyks who camped across the river from his works, the raiding Kuban Tartars, the Crimean khanate, and the Mordva and Cheremis 'Tartars' who dug his canal. This corpus copy is the first edition of 1716.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The steppe and frontier peoples of Russia's south and east: Kalmyks, Kuban Tartars (Nogais), Crimean Tatars, the Finnic Mordva and Cheremis (Mari) of the Kazan province, and further away the 'Bogdoi' (Manchu), Uzbek and Bukharan Tartars.
Why read it
Short but precise eyewitness pictures of Kalmyk lattice tents, wagon homes and camps laid out like towns, from a practical engineer who lived beside them for years.
Watch out
Only a small part of the book concerns Tartars; his history of the Crimean khanate is second hand and contains errors (Moscow burned in 1571, not 1671). The OCR keeps the long s (ſ) and is patchy in places.

CitiesKamyshin, lower Volgaabout 1698 to 1701

A camp laid out in streets and lanes

Travelling in bands of up to twenty thousand, the Kalmyks pitched their tents 'in Streets and Lanes' like a town or village, each family knowing its place. Perry's homely proof: he saw their cows stop at their own tents when driven home to be milked. A clear eyewitness picture of the steppe camp as a moving town.

…sometimes 20000 of them or more in a Body, and pitch their Tents in Streets and Lanes, in the same regular Manner as in a Town or Village, and every one knows their due Place and Order; so that I have seen the Cows stop at their own Tents, when they drive them home to milk them.

CitiesNear Volgograd (Tsaritsyn), lower Volgaabout 1698 to 1712

The ruins of 'Saroff Gorod'

About forty Russian miles below Tsaritsyn Perry notes the ruins of a large city called 'Saroff Gorod', said to have been the ancient seat of a 'Scythian King'. The name points to Sarai, the capital of the Golden Horde on the Akhtuba, though Perry does not connect it with the Tartars at all. Pallas (W005) describes the same brick ruins in detail.

…there is to be seen the Ruins of a large City, call'd Saroff Gorod, in a very pleasant Situation, and which is said to have been the ancient Residence of a Scythian King.

Heard itCaptain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

CitiesMoscow1571 (Perry writes 1671)

When the Crimean Tartars burned Moscow

Perry recalls that the Crimean Tartars once penetrated to the heart of Russia and burned Moscow, and that the tsars had been forced to pay tribute and even to agree to hold the khan's stirrup if they met. Perry's date is wrong by a century: Khan Devlet Giray burned Moscow in 1571. The tribute to the Crimean khans was indeed paid until the 1680s to 1700.

The said Tartars were formerly victorious against the Russes. They have often penetrated into the Heart of Russia, and in Anno 1671, they burnt the City of Mosco, and by the Articles of Peace … the Russes were obliged to submit to very scandalous Terms; not only to be tributary to the said Tartars, but even … that the Czar's of Russia should hold the Stirrup to the Great Cham,

Heard itCaptain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

Perry's year 1671 is a mistake for 1571. The stirrup clause is what he was told.

BuildingsKamyshin, lower Volgaabout 1698 to 1701

Tents 'round like a pigeon-house'

Perry, an English engineer, watched Kalmyk hordes camp across the Volga every summer while he dug the Volga to Don canal. He describes their tents as round lattice-work frames that fold into narrow panels, covered with thick felt ('wylock'), with a door, a smoke hole and a fire in the middle; with the door and hole shut, he says, the tent is 'as warm as a stove'. He adds that a chief's tent could be lined with Persian silk.

The common Tents, in which the Cullmicks and other forementioned Tartars live and move, with their Camps, from Place to Place, are made of small Lettice-Work, round like a Pidgeon-House, … and which when they strike, folds together in narrow Pannels. They cover them over with Wylock, … matted together like Felt or Hat-Work,

BuildingsLower Volgaabout 1698 to 1701

Homes on eight-foot wheels

When the camp moves, Perry says, women and children ride in covered carriages on two great wheels about eight feet across, high enough to ford small rivers, and they live in these as well as in their tents 'like as in Houses'. Bactrian camels ('dromedaries') pull them. People of rank have several such carriages for baggage and servants.

…Wives and their Children are drawn on covered Machines fixed on two large Wheels of about 8 Foot diameter, … so that they can easily ford over small Rivers; in which, as well as their Tents, they live like as in Houses; and Persons of Distinction have several of these for their Baggage and Retinue. They are drawn by Dromedaries,

BuildingsCentral Asia and the Volga steppeabout 1698 to 1712

Some Tartars in houses, others in tents

Perry sorts the Tartars into those who live in houses and stay put all year (the 'Bogdoi', Uzbek and Bukharan Tartars towards China) and those who live in tents and migrate north and south with the seasons (the Kalmyks and western hordes). He compares the nomads' life to the patriarchs in Moses: they neither plough nor sow.

The Bogdoi, Yousbeck, and Bucharsky Tartars, more towards China, live in Houses, and continue Winter and Summer in the same place; but the Cullmick, and several Hordes of Western Tartars, who border more towards the C'zar's Dominions, live in Tents, and move Northward and Southward, according to the Seasons of the Year,

Heard itCaptain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

Daily lifeSteppe between Saratov and the Caspianabout 1698 to 1701

Raiders with two horses each

Perry explains why the rich land west of the lower Volga lay empty: raids by the Kuban Tartars, who plundered and burned villages and carried off cattle and people. They came in summer, each man with two horses to change as he rode, with sentries all round, and were gone before the Russians could react. A page later he tells how three to four thousand of them reached his own camp at dawn and drove off about 1400 horses.

The said Tartars when they make their Incursions, it is usually in the Summer time, when there is Grass enough on the Ground; and each Man for Expedition sake takes two Horses, which they change as they ride, sometimes sitting upon one, and sometimes upon another. They always travel with Centinels at a convenient Distance from all Sides of their main Body,

Saw itCaptain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

Daily lifeKamyshin and the Kazan provinceabout 1698 to 1712

'If their religion be right, why do they not do right?'

Half the labourers on Perry's canal were Mordva and Cheremis 'Tartars', long under Russian rule. Asked why they would not be baptized, they told him that images terrified them, because God is one and cannot be pictured, and he lives in heaven. They taunted Russians with the question, 'if their Religion be right, why do they not do right?' Perry adds that he found them more honest than the Russians.

…they say, that the Ruses using of Images, is a Terror to them to think of embracing their Religion; for that there is but one God, and that he cannot be pictured or described by Men; they look up towards the Heavens, and say, that he resides there, and of whom they are afraid to do Evil;

Saw itCaptain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

The nameChina1716

The 'Bogdoi Tartars' who rule China

Describing the borders of Siberia, Perry notes that the Great Wall was built against the 'bordering Tartars', who about seventy years before had conquered China anyway, and that the 'Chams of Great Tartary', called the Bogdoi Tartars, were still emperors of China. 'Bogdoi' comes from the Mongol title Bogd Khan used for the Qing emperor.

…to defend them from the Incursions of the bordering Tartars, who have notwithstanding, about 70 Years since, made themselves Masters of the Countrey; and the Chams of Great Tartary, who are called the Bogdi Tartars, continue Emperors of China to this Day.

The nameSamarkand and the steppe east of the Volgaabout 1698 to 1712

A great khan at Samarkand, heir of Tamerlane

Perry reports that the lands between Siberia and the Caspian hold many hordes of Tartars, each with its own chief, but many recognise one principal khan at Samarkand said to descend from Tamerlane. He adds that their religion is close to Islam, except that they eat horse meat, which the Turks and Crimean Tartars refuse. By 1700 Samarkand had no such overlord; this is hearsay.

Many of them acknowledge one principal Cham as their Chief, who has his Residence at Samarcand, … and derives his Pedigree, as 'tis said, from the Great Tamerlane.

Heard itCaptain John Perry (The State of Russia, 1716)

Strange talesPerekop, Crimea1689

Barrels of gold, some of them fake

Perry repeats the story of why Prince Vasily Golitsyn's army turned back from the Crimea in 1689: the Tatars brought him barrels of gold ducats, 'amongst which, as 'tis said, there were many Counters', and he made peace in the field. On the page before, he reports that the Crimean khan would inherit the Ottoman throne if the sultan's male line failed.

…being tempted by a great Sum of Ducats of Gold, which they brought to him in Barrels (amongst which, as 'tis said, there were many Counters) he concluded Conditions of Peace in the Field, and withdrew his Army.
