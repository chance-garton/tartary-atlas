Bergeron's 1634 French collection of the friars' journeys into Tartary

The first French edition of Rubruck, Carpini and Ascelin's reports on the Mongols, bound with Bergeron's own treatise on the Tartars, Paris 1634.

Catalogue title Account of the journeys into Tartary of Friar William of Rubruck, Friar John of Plano Carpini, Friar Ascelin and other religious ... with a Treatise on the Tartars, their origin, customs, religion and conquests

Pierre Bergeron, editor; William of Rubruck; John of Plano Carpini; Ascelin

Pierre Bergeron, a Paris scholar, gathered French translations of three thirteenth-century mission reports: William of Rubruck's journey to the Mongol court (1253 to 1255), John of Plano Carpini's embassy to Güyük Khan (1245 to 1247) and the Dominican Ascelin's mission to the Mongol general Baiju in Persia (1247). The friars saw the Mongols for themselves; Bergeron translated their Latin as printed by Hakluyt and Vincent of Beauvais. He added his own Traicté des Tartares, a compiled history of the Tartars' origin, customs and empires down to his own day. The corpus holds two scans of the 1634 Paris first edition: T0621 (the journeys) and T0564 (journeys plus the Traicté).

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
In the friars' reports 'Tartares' means the Mongols of the 1240s and 1250s, including Batu's Golden Horde on the Volga and the court of the Great Khan in Mongolia. Bergeron uses the word more widely for all the steppe peoples he derives from the Scythians, down to the Crimean 'Precopite' Tatars of his own time.
Why read it
Here are the eyewitness classics in French dress: felt houses on wagons, kumiss, a woman from Metz married to a Russian builder, and Karakorum with its silver drinking-fountain tree, next to a seventeenth-century scholar's struggle to explain where the Tartars and their name came from.
Watch out
The friars' words reach us through Bergeron's French translation of printed Latin texts, so the phrasing is Bergeron's, not theirs. His Traicté is armchair compilation that mixes solid history with Gog and Magog and lost-tribes speculation. The OCR is rough 1634 type with long s and margin notes run into the text.

CitiesPontic-Caspian steppe1253

A rich Tartar's camp looks like a big town

The Mongol camp could look like a settlement. Each wife of a great lord had her own large house with a train of smaller wagon-houses (Rubruck says Batu's wives each had more than 200), and the wives' courts were pitched in a row from west to east, a stone's throw apart. The result, he says, resembles a large town, though few men live in it.

the first wife has her little court set up to the west, then all the others do the same, each in her rank, so that the last is at the east, and the space between them is about a stone's throw: so that the court of one of these rich Tartars looks like a big town, although there will be very few men in it.

CitiesKarakorum1254

Karakorum: worse than Saint-Denis, with two quarters and many temples

Rubruck's famous verdict on the Mongol capital: apart from the Khan's palace, it is not as good as the French town of Saint-Denis, whose abbey is worth ten Mongol palaces. It has a Saracen quarter with markets and a Cathayan (Chinese) quarter of craftsmen, twelve idol temples, two mosques and a church. On the next page (n221) he adds earthen walls with four gates, each with its own market for grain, sheep, oxen and carts, or horses.

except for the Khan's palace, it is not as good as the town of Saint-Denis in France, whose monastery is worth ten times more than the whole palace of Möngke itself. There are two main streets, one called the Saracens', where the markets and fair are held … There are twelve temples of idolaters of various nations, and two mosques of the Saracens

Working translation

Original wording

excepté le Palais du Cham, elle n'est pas si bonne que la ville de S. Denis en France, dont le Monastere vaut dix fois mieux que tout le Palais mesme de Mangu. Il y a deux grandes ruës, l'vne dite des Sarrasins, où se tiennent les marchez & la foire … Là sont douze temples d'Idolatres de diuerses nations; & deux mosquées de Sarrasins

CitiesDon River (as Carpini says)c. 1220s-1230s

A trading city drowned by diverting its river

Carpini tells how the Mongols took the rich, crowded city of Orna, a port full of Christians and Muslim merchants: finding it hard to storm, they dammed the river that ran through it and drowned the city with everything in it. He goes on to the sack of Kiev and the Russian towns.

After that they came before the city of Orna, which was very populous, and there were many Christians there, Khazars, Russians, Alans and others, and some Saracens … The Tartars, seeing that it was hard to take by force, decided to stop the river that runs through the city, and so they drowned it with everything that was in it.

Working translation

Original wording

Aprés cela ils vindrent deuant la cité d'Orna qui estoit fort peuplée, & là y auoit plusieurs Chrestiens Gazares, Russes, Alans, & autres, & quelques Sarasins … Les Tartares voyans qu'il estoit difficile de la prendre de force, ils s'aduiserent d'arrester la riuiere qui passe par ceste ville, & ainsi la submergerent auec tout ce qui estoit dedans.

Heard itJohn of Plano Carpini (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

Keep in mind

Carpini places Orna on the Don, but the story matches the Mongol flooding of Urgench in Khwarazm (1221), and historians have often suspected a confusion; treat the location as uncertain.

CitiesCrimeaearly 17th century

Crimean Tatar towns: Perekop, Bakhchysarai and ruined Caffa

Bergeron sketches the Crimean khanate of his own day. The Tatars' chief fortress is Perekop on the canal across the isthmus, the khans' royal seat is at Bakhchysarai, and they hold other towns such as Crim, from which they are called Crimeans. The Turks hold Mangup and Caffa (ancient Theodosia), the old Genoese trading port, which he says now lies half deserted, with ruins of a very great city.

Now the chief town or fortress of these Precopites, set on this canal to guard the country, is Precop or Perekop; the royal seat of the Khans is at Bacasaray or Almasaray; and they have several other towns in their share, such as Crim, from which they are called Crimenses or Krimski

Original wording

Or la principale ville ou forteresse de ces Precopites, située sur ce canal pour la garde du pays, est Precop, ou Perecop; le siege royal des Chans, ou Cans est à Bacasaray, ou Almasaray; & y a plusieurs autres villes de leur partage, comme Crim, dont ils sont surnommez Crimenses, ou Krimski

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Houses built on wheels and covered in white felt

Rubruck opens his description of the Tartars with their homes. The sleeping-houses sit on wheels, are made of interlaced wooden laths that rise to a smoke-hole like a chimney, and are covered with white felt smeared with lime, white earth or powdered bone so that they gleam. He stresses that the Tartars have no fixed dwelling and divide the steppe from the Danube eastward into seasonal pastures.

The houses in which they sleep are set on wheels … [made of] interlaced pieces of wood, and they come together at the top in an opening like a chimney, and they cover this with white felt, which they coat with lime or earth

Les maisons où ils habitent pour dormir sont fondées sur des rouës … des pieces de bois entrelassées, & abboutissent en haut en vne ouuerture comme vne cheminée, & couurent cela de feutre blanc, qu'ils enduisent de chaulx ou terre

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Twenty-two oxen to pull one house

Rubruck took a tape to a Mongol wagon-house. He measured one at twenty feet between the wheels, with the house overhanging them, and counted twenty-two oxen, eleven abreast on each side, hauling it, with an axle as thick as a ship's mast. On the same page he says the felt roof was painted and the door-felt embroidered with vines, trees, birds and beasts.

They have some of these houses so large that they are a good thirty feet long: for I once took the trouble to measure one that was a good twenty feet from one wheel to the other, and when the house was set on it, it overhung the wheels. … And I counted up to twenty-two oxen drawing one of these houses, eleven on one side and eleven on the other.

Working translation

Original wording

Ils ont de ces maisons-là de telle grandeur, qu'elles ont bien trente pieds de long : car i'ay pris la peine quelquefois d'en mesurer vne qui auoit bien vingt pieds d'vne rouë à l'autre : & quand ceste maison estoit posée dessus, elle passoit au delà des rouës. … & ay conté iusqu'à vingt-deux bœufs pour traisner vne de ces maisons, vnze d'vn costé, & vnze de l'autre.

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

BuildingsKarakorum1254

The silver tree that poured four drinks at Karakorum

Beside Möngke's palace at Karakorum stood a great silver tree made by a captive Parisian goldsmith, Master Guillaume. Four silver lions at its foot and pipes hidden in the trunk delivered wine, kumiss, honey-mead and rice beer, and a silver angel on top blew a trumpet (worked by a man hidden inside) to call the servers. It is a vivid case of a Mongol showpiece built by a foreign craftsman.

this Master Guillaume had made him a great silver tree, at whose foot were four lions, also of silver, each with a pipe or channel from which mare's milk flowed. The four pipes were hidden in the tree, rising to the top and flowing down from there. On each of these conduits were gilded serpents whose tails wound round the trunk of the tree.

Working translation

Original wording

ce M. Guillaume luy auoit fait vn grand arbre d'argent, au pied duquel estoient quatre lyons aussi d'argent, ayans chacun vne pipe ou canal d'où sortoit du lait de iument. Les quatre pipes estoient cachées dans l'arbre, montans iusqu'au sommet, & de là s'escoulans en bas. Sur chacun de ces muids ou canaux y auoit des serpens dorez dont les queuës venoient à enuironner le corps de l'arbre.

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

BuildingsMongolia (Möngke's court)1254

A Russian husband who built houses for the Tartars

At the Mongol court Rubruck met Paquette, a woman from Metz captured in Hungary, who had married a young Russian. She told him her husband was expert in building, 'an art much valued and needed among the Tartars', and that a Parisian goldsmith was working at Karakorum. The Mongols' building work, in other words, was often done by captives from conquered lands.

having a young husband from Russia, by whom she had three fine children; and her husband was very skilled at building, which is an art much esteemed and in demand among the Tartars. She also told us that at Karakorum there was a Parisian goldsmith named Guillaume Boucher

Working translation

Original wording

ayant vn ieune mary de Russie, dont elle auoit trois beaux enfans, & son mary s'entendoit fort aux bastimens, qui est vn art bien estimé & requis entre les Tartares. Elle nous donna encor aduis qu'à Caracarum il y auoit vn Orfeure Parisien, nommé Guillaume Boucher

Heard itWilliam of Rubruck, reporting what Paquette told him (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

BuildingsPontic-Caspian steppe1253

Cuman grave mounds, statues and brick towers on the steppe

Crossing the steppe, Rubruck describes the tombs of the Cumans, the Turkic people the Mongols had conquered there. They raised a mound over the dead with a statue facing east holding a cup; for the rich they built little pointed houses, and he saw great brick towers and stone houses in a land without stone. The same page notes that nobody knew where Chinggis Khan was buried.

As for the Cumans, they are accustomed to raise a mound over the grave of the dead man and set up a statue for him facing east … For the rich and great they put up pyramids, or little pointed houses, and in some places I saw great towers of brick, and in others houses built of stone, although no stone is found in those parts.

Working translation

Original wording

Pour les Comans, ils ont coustume d'esleuer vne motte ou tertre sur la sepulture du mort, & luy dressent vne statuë la face tournée à l'Orient … Aux riches & Grands ils dressent des pyramides, ou petites maisons pointuës, & ay veu en des endroits de grandes tours de brique, & en d'autres des maisons basties de pierres; encores qu'en ces quartiers là on n'y trouue point de pierres.

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

BuildingsSira Ordo, near Karakorum1246

A tent for two thousand people at Güyük's election

At the assembly that elected Güyük Great Khan, Carpini found a huge tent of white purple cloth which he reckoned could hold more than two thousand people, ringed by a wooden palisade painted with figures. The chiefs changed the colour of their robes each day. A few pages on he describes another tent, the 'Golden Horde', on pillars covered with gold plates fixed with gold nails.

There we found a very large tent of white purple, so large, in our opinion, that it could hold more than two thousand people. Around it they had set up a scaffold or palisade of wood, covered with various figures and paintings.

Original wording

Nous trouuames là vne tente de pourpre blanc tres-grande, voire telle, à nostre aduis, qu'elle estoit capable de tenir plus de deux mil personnes. A l'entour d'icelle on auoit fait esleuer vn eschaffaut ou palissade de bois, remply de diuerses figures & peintures.

BuildingsMongolia1245-1247

Round felt tents, some carried whole on carts

Carpini gives his own short description of Mongol dwellings: round, tent-shaped, built of slender rods, with a round window at the top for light and smoke, a central fire, and felt walls, roof and door. Some can be taken apart and loaded on pack animals; others travel whole on carts pulled by one ox or by several.

Their dwellings are round, shaped like tents, and made with very slender rods and sticks; and on top, right in the middle, there is a round window through which the light comes in and the smoke goes out, for they always make their fire in the middle; the walls and roofs of these dwellings are covered with felts … Some are very easy to take down and put up again

Original wording

Leurs logemens sont ronds, en forme de tentes, & faits auec des verges & bastons fort deliez; & au dessus, droit au milieu, y a vne fenestre ronde, par où la lumiere entre, & la fumée sort; car ils font tousiours leur feu au milieu: les parois & toits de ces logis sont couuerts de feutres … Quelques-vnes sont fort aisées à deffaire & refaire

Daily lifePontic-Caspian steppe1253

How kumiss is made, and what it tastes like

Rubruck explains the Mongols' summer drink, fermented mare's milk. Foals are tethered on a line so the mares let themselves be milked; the milk is churned in a skin with a club until it bubbles like new wine and turns sour. He found it prickled the tongue, left a taste of almonds and could make a weak head drunk; a clear black kind, cara-cosmos, was kept for the great.

they pour it into a skin or other vessel, where they beat and stir it very well … it begins to bubble like new wine, then to turn sour like leaven … it stings the tongue like rough wine when you drink it. When you have finished drinking, it leaves a taste of almonds on the tongue and greatly cheers the heart

Working translation

Original wording

ils le versent dans vn outre, ou autre vaisseau, là où ils le battent & remuent tres-bien … cela commence à boüillir comme du vin nouueau, puis à s'aigrir, comme du leuain … cela pique la langue comme feroit du vin raspé quand on le boit. Lors que l'on a acheué de boire, cela laisse sur la langue vn goust d'amande, & resioüit grandement le cœur

Saw itWilliam of Rubruck (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

Daily lifeMongolia1245-1247

A great man buried sitting in his tent, with a mare and foal

Carpini describes the secret burial of a Mongol noble: he is buried out on the steppe with his tent, seated before a table with a bowl of meat and a cup of mare's milk, with a mare and foal and a saddled horse. Another horse is eaten and its skin, stuffed with straw, is raised on four poles, so the dead will have a home, milk and horses in the next world. His name may not be spoken for three generations.

they bury him secretly in the open country, with his tent, and he is seated in the middle of it, with a table in front of him and a bowl full of meat and a cup of mare's milk; they also bury with him a mare and her foal, and a horse saddled and bridled; and they eat another horse, whose skin they stuff with straw

Working translation

Original wording

on l'enterre secrettement en la campagne, auec sa loge, & est assis au milieu d'icelle, auec vne table deuant luy, & vn bassin plein de chair, & vne tasse de lait de iument; On enterre aussi auec luy vne iument, & son poulain; & vn cheual sellé & bridé: & mangent vn autre cheual, dont ils remplissent la peau de paille

Heard itJohn of Plano Carpini (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

The name1634

Bergeron's guesses: a river Tartar, or Syriac for 'abandoned'

In his Traicté, Bergeron lists Hayton's seven Mongol nations and offers two origins for the name: a river called Tartar, or a Syriac word meaning 'the abandoned', favoured by those who made the Tartars descendants of the ten lost tribes of Israel. He reports claims that some northern hordes bore the tribal names Dan, Zabulon and Naphtali, but on the next page calls the theory weak, resting on an apocryphal book.

The name Tartar, Tatar or Totar comes from the river Tartar, or from the word Totar or Tatar, which in the Syrian language means 'forsaken' or 'abandoned', according to the opinion of those who would make them descend from the ten tribes of Israel exiled in Media and afterwards come to the land of Arsareth.

Working translation

Original wording

Le nom de Tartar, Tatar, ou Totar, vient du fleuue Tartar; ou du mot Totar, ou Tatar, qui en langage Syrien veut dire delaissez, ou abandonnez, suiuant l'opinion de ceux qui les veulent faire descendre des dix lignées Israëlites releguées en Medie, & depuis venuës au pays d'Arsareth.

Read itPierre Bergeron (Traicté des Tartares, 1634)

Strange talesArctic Siberia (vaguely located)1245-1247

Men with ox feet and dogs' faces by the northern ocean

In his account of Mongol campaigns Carpini passes on marvels: far north, beyond the Samoyeds who live by hunting and wear only skins, the Tartars met at the Ocean monsters shaped like men but with ox feet and dogs' faces, who spoke a few words and then barked. On the facing page are the Parossites, with tiny mouths, who live only on the steam of boiled meat.

From there they came as far as the Ocean sea, where they found monsters which in every other respect had the form of men, but they had the feet of oxen and faces like a dog.

Working translation

Original wording

De là ils paruindrent iusqu'à la mer Oceane, où ils trouuerent des monstres, qui en tout le reste auoient forme d'hommes, mais ils auoient des pieds de bœuf, & le visage fait comme vn chien.

Legend or rumourJohn of Plano Carpini (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

Strange tales13th century (retold 1634)

Gog and Magog shut behind a rock at the end of the world

Bergeron retells what French chroniclers such as Joinville and Nangis had heard from envoys: the Tartars said that at the northern end of the earth a rock no one could pass shut in the peoples Gog and Magog, whom Bergeron equates with the Mongal of other writers and the Moal the Tartars call themselves, destined to come with Antichrist at the end of the world. Behind the rock lay the sandy desert where the Tartars lived as tributary herders until Chinggis roused them.

the peoples called Goths and Magoths were shut in (who are the Gog and Magog of Scripture, the Mongal of other writers and the Moal of the Tartars themselves). And that these people were to come at the end of the world with Antichrist.

Working translation

Original wording

estoient enclos les peuples appellez Goths & Magoths (qui est le Gog & Magog de l'Escriture, & le Mongal des autres Escriuains, & le Moal des Tartares mesmes). Que ces gens-là deuoient venir à la fin du monde auec l'Antechrist.

Legend or rumourPierre Bergeron (Traicté des Tartares, 1634), paraphrasing Joinville, Nangis and other French chroniclers

Strange talesMongolia (Möngke's court)1254

A shaman's night séance and a demon on the roof

Rubruck describes how Mongol diviners answered the Khan's questions: meat is laid in the middle of the house at night, the shaman mutters charms, beats a drum on the ground, raves and is tied up, and the 'devil' comes in the dark to eat and give answers. He then repeats a story from the goldsmith Guillaume about a hidden Hungarian who heard the demon shrieking on the roof, unable to enter because a Christian was present.

the one who performs the invocation begins to mutter his charms and, holding a drum in his hand, beats it hard against the ground, and throws himself about so that he becomes as if beside himself and begins to rave; then he has himself bound tight; then the devil comes in the darkness of the night and gives him some of the meat to eat

Working translation

Original wording

celuy qui fait l'inuocation commence à murmurer ses charmes, & tenant vn tabourin en la main, le frappe fort contre terre, & se demene & agite en sorte qu'il deuient comme hors de soy, & commence à resver, puis il se fait lier bien serré; lors le diable vient durant l'obscurité de la nuict, & luy donne à manger de ces chairs

Heard itWilliam of Rubruck (French translation by Pierre Bergeron, 1634)

About Tartars (Mongol shamans)

Keep in mind

Rubruck did not attend such a séance; the roof-demon story comes secondhand from Master Guillaume, and he frames it all as devil-worship.

Why it matters

First vernacular corpus of the Franciscan Mongol missions, and the book that fixed "voyages en Tartarie" as a French bibliographic category; Bergeron's Traicte des Tartares is an early attempt to define who the Tartars were from the medieval sources alone.
