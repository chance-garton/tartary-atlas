The Hommaire de Hells in the Caspian steppes and the Crimea

A French engineer and his wife describe the Kalmyks, Nogais and Crimean Tatars they lived among in southern Russia, 1838 to 1841.

Full title Travels in the Steppes of the Caspian Sea, the Crimea, the Caucasus, etc.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell; Adèle Hommaire de Hell

Read the 17 passagesOpen the original book

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (1812-1848), a French civil engineer and geologist, spent about five years in southern Russia, partly on Russian government missions, travelling with his wife Adèle (1819-1883). They crossed the Kalmyk and Nogai steppes, stayed with the Kalmyk Prince Tumene near Astrakhan, and toured the Crimea. The preface says Adèle wrote all the descriptive parts; Xavier supplied the science, history and statistics. The French original appeared 1843 to 1845. This corpus copy is the condensed English edition, 'with additions from various sources', London 1847. It has whole chapters on the Kalmyks, on the origin of the Tatars and Mongols, on the Nogais, and on Bakhchysarai and the history of the Crimean khanate.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
'Tatar' (they refuse to write 'Tartar') for the Turkic Muslim peoples of southern Russia: Crimean Tatars of the mountains and coast, Nogais of the steppe, and the Tatars of Astrakhan. They argue these are mostly Turks once ruled by Mongols, and that 'Tatar' is really a European name. The Kalmyks are treated as Mongols.
Why read it
One of the clearest pre-1850 discussions of where the name Tatar came from, including the Tartarus pun, plus eyewitness detail on felt kibitkas, Nogai mud-brick cottages, the wooden palace of Bakhchysarai and the Russian demolition of Tatar and Genoese buildings in Caffa.
Watch out
The English edition is shortened and adds material from other sources. The history chapters rely on Klaproth, D'Ohsson and others. The authors openly dislike Russian officialdom, and they record, without shame, robbing a Kalmyk tomb.

What it says: 17 passages

CitiesFeodosia (Caffa), Crimea1840

Caffa: Tatar and Genoese buildings blown up by Russian officials

Xavier describes what happened to Caffa after the Russian annexation of 1783. The Genoese walls and towers were pulled down for barracks, the great Turkish baths and the old cathedral-turned-mosque were demolished with pickaxe and gunpowder in 1833, and the Tatar population was expelled. It is a first-hand record of who actually destroyed the old buildings of a Tatar-era city.

In 1833, the ignorance of a civil governor, Kasnatcheief, completed this afflicting work of destruction, which extended at the same time to the great baths that still remained untouched. A fortnight's work with the pickaxe and gunpowder razed to the ground the two admirable monuments with which the Genoese and the Turks had adorned the town.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About Crimean Tatars; Genoese; Turks

Keep in mind

The authors are hostile to Russian officials; the demolition itself is widely recorded, but the blame is their own judgement.

CitiesBakhchysaraic. 1840

Bakhchysarai kept for the Tatars by imperial decree

The old Tatar capital, the authors explain, kept its eastern look because Catherine II granted the Tatars sole possession of the town. Walking its narrow streets of mosques, shops and cemeteries felt like the old quarters of Constantinople. On the same page they give a potted history: Batu Khan's Tatars founded the kingdom in the 1200s, and Bakhchysarai became the capital in the early 1500s.

Bagtche Serai has completely retained its national character in consequence of an ukase of Catherine II., empowering the Tatars to retain exclusive possession of their own capital. You would fancy yourself in the heart of the East, in walking through the narrow streets of the town, the mosques, shops, and cemeteries of which so much resemble those of the old quarters of Constantinople.

Xavier and Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

CitiesAstrakhan1839

Astrakhan, 'ancient capital of the Tatar kingdom'

The authors sketch Astrakhan's history: part of Batu Khan's Kipchak empire (the Golden Horde), then an independent Tatar khanate, taken by Ivan the Terrible in 1554, after which its prosperity faded. In their day it was a provincial town, but its old walls and battlemented towers still recalled its past. Over 5,000 Tatars lived there as traders, and their mosques and bath domes gave the town an oriental look.

At present the ancient capital of the Tatar kingdom is merely the chief town of a government … It contains a great number of squares, churches, and mosques. Its old embattled towers and its walls, which still include a considerable space of ground, remind the traveller of its ancient warlike renown.

Xavier and Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About Tatars of the Golden Horde and the Astrakhan khanate

Keep in mind

The history is the authors' summary; they do not say who built the walls and towers they saw.

BuildingsKalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)1839 to 1840

The Kalmyk kibitka: a felt house two camels can carry

A precise description of the felt tent (kibitka) from people who stayed in them: a cylinder as high as a man's shoulder, a conical roof open at the top for smoke, a wooden ring held up by many spars. The whole thing comes apart and can be carried by two camels. On the next page they add that the felts kept out the fiercest storms, as they learned from experience.

Their dwellings are felt tents, called kibitkas by the Russians. They are four or five yards in diameter, cylindrical to the height of a man's shoulder, with a conical top, open at the apex to let the smoke escape. The frame is light, and can be taken asunder for the convenience of carriage.

Xavier and Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

BuildingsMolochna River (southern Ukraine)1838 to 1841

A Nogai cottage of sun-dried brick and reeds

Settled by Russian officials only a generation earlier, the Nogais now built their own cottages. The walls are sun-dried brick, whitewashed yearly; the roof is rafters covered with reeds and branches loaded with earth and ashes. Inside are two rooms, and a man with two wives builds a separate room for each.

The Nogai constructs his own cottage with bricks dried in the sun, and whitewashes it regularly once a year within and without. Its dimensions are scarcely more than two or three-and-thirty feet by thirteen. The roof consists of a few rafters on which are laid reeds and branches of trees loaded with earth and ashes.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

BuildingsBakhchysaraic. 1840

The Khan's palace: 'an assemblage of wooden houses'

Adèle defends the palace of the Crimean khans against visitors who sneered that it was only a cluster of wooden houses with crude paintings, divans and carpets. She agrees that is what it is made of, but says its charm lies in feeling, not materials. It is a useful witness to how the Tatar capital's palace was actually built: timber, painted walls, fountains and gardens.

" How," said they, " can any one apply the name of palace to that assemblage of wooden houses, daubed with coarse paintings, and furnished only with divans and carpets?" And these people were right in their way.

Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About Crimean Tatars (the Giray khans)

Keep in mind

The palace had been restored under Tsar Alexander I, as the authors say on p.363, so not everything seen was original.

BuildingsSudak coast, Crimea1840

Terraced Tatar villages with clay roofs

In the mountain villages of Toklouk, Kooz and Otouz, Xavier found flat-roofed Tatar houses built against the valley sides, so that the roofs formed terraces where people worked and visited each other. The roofs are stout timber covered with a thick bed of clay. At evening whole villages gathered on them.

the terrace tops of their houses, where they regularly carry on their work, and which are formed of stout carpentry covered with a thick bed of clay. Nothing can be more picturesque than the appearance, at evening, of all these terraces rising in gradations one above the other.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

BuildingsVolga delta near Astrakhan1839

A Buddhist temple of stuccoed brick on the Volga

At Prince Tumene's island seat, Adèle attended a service in the Kalmyk 'pagoda'. She describes the din of drums, gongs, conch shells and three-metre trumpets, and the building itself: large windows, slender columns of stuccoed brick, a domed gallery and walls hung with painted tapestries of spirits and monsters. This was a settled Kalmyk prince's temple, not a tent.

The temple, lighted by a row of large windows, is adorned with slender columns of stuccoed brick work, the lightness of which reminds one of the graceful Moorish architecture. A gallery runs all round the dome, which is also remarkable for the extreme delicacy of its workmanship.

Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

Daily lifeMolochna River (southern Ukraine)before 1808 (written 1840s)

'To us he has given wheels'

Describing the Nogais before their settlement in villages, Xavier says a family could pack wife, children and goods into its felt-covered wagon (araba) within an hour and roll off towards the horizon. He quotes a Nogai saying that sums up the old nomad view of the world: God gave wheels to them and houses and ploughs to others.

Such is the order established by God himself," cried the Nogai, " to us he has given wheels, to other nations fixed dwellings and the plough."

a Nogai saying, quoted by Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

Daily lifeVolga delta near Astrakhan1839

A ten-year-old breaks a wild stallion

At Prince Tumene's, Kalmyk riders lassoed wild horses from the herd and rode them bareback, clinging to the mane, until they were caught up by other horsemen. Then a boy of about ten did the same on a huge white stallion. Adèle adds that even Kalmyk women practise the sport.

But what was our amazement when we saw a boy of ten years come forward to undertake the same exploit! They selected for him a young white stallion of great size, whose fiery bounds and desperate efforts to break his bonds, indicated a most violent temper.

Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

Daily lifeMolochna River (southern Ukraine)1838 to 1841

The price of a Nogai bride

Among the Nogais a groom never saw his bride before the wedding and bargained with her family for her price. Xavier gives the going rates: four or five hundred rubles and a couple of score cows for a handsome girl of good family, less for widows. On the same page he adds that Crimean mountain Tatar and Kalmyk wives cost less and were bought by poorer men.

A handsome girl of good family costs four or five hundred rubles, besides a couple of score of cows and a few other beasts. Young widows are cheaper, and old women are to be had for nothing. The bride's price is paid on the spot by the wooer

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About Nogais

Keep in mind

Written from outside and with some disdain; how widely these prices applied is not stated.

The name1840s

Why 'Tartar' is a pun, not a name

In a footnote to their chapter on the origin of the Tatars, the authors refuse to use the spelling 'Tartar' at all. They say it comes only from a play on words by St Louis, King of France, who in the 1240s punned on Tatar and Tartarus, the classical underworld. This is the famous Tartarus pun, stated plainly by a 19th-century writer.

We have entirely rejected from our discussion the word Tartar, which owes its origin only to a jeu de mots, of which St. Louis was the author.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (footnote, English translation, 1847)

About Tatars and Mongols

Keep in mind

The St Louis story is traditional (it is told in Matthew Paris's chronicle of the 1240s). The pun was widespread, and the story does not prove where the spelling began.

The nameMongolia1100s to 1200s (written 1840s)

A people made famous by those who destroyed it

Drawing on the historian D'Ohsson, the authors explain that 'Tatar' was first the name of a real Mongolian tribe, whom Genghis Khan wiped out. Yet the name spread from nation to nation and stuck to Genghis's own armies. By a strange accident, they say, a destroyed people became famous through the conquests of its destroyers.

It is a fact established by the statements of many writers, and by D'Ohson himself, that Genghis Khan annihilated the white Tatars, and thus it has come to pass by a most curious freak of accident, that this extinguished people became celebrated all over the East by the conquests of its very destroyers.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About the Tatar tribe and the Mongols of Genghis Khan

Keep in mind

A summary of 19th-century scholarship, not the authors' own research.

The nameKazan, Astrakhan and Crimea1840s

Europeans kept calling the Muslim Turks 'Tatars'

The authors argue that after the Mongol conquests Europeans went on calling all the Muslim, Turkic-speaking peoples of Kazan, Astrakhan, the Crimea and Turkestan 'Tatars', which caused endless confusion. On the same page they conclude that the name lost all meaning in Asia and survives 'only in the European vocabulary', and that the Tatars of southern Russia are really Turks, Cumans or Pechenegs once ruled by the Mongols.

After the Mongol torrent had subsided, Europeans persisted in giving the appellation of Tatars to all those Mussulman nations originally of Turkish origin, that to this day occupy the territory of Kasan and Astrakhan, the Crimea and the region called Turcomania

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

The nameMolochna River (southern Ukraine)1838 to 1841

'Mayst thou never know happiness': where the Nogais got their name

European books said the Nogai horde was named after a Tatar general called Nogai. Xavier collected the Nogais' own traditions, which say nothing of such a general and derive the name from 'neogai', a curse on their hard wandering life. He prefers the Nogai version, arguing that a people so proud of their ancestry would have remembered a founder.

their chronicles allege that the name of the nation is derived from neogai (which may be translated by the phrase, mayst thou never know happiness), and that it was bestowed on them in their old country, on account of their precarious and vagabond life.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About Nogais

Keep in mind

A folk etymology; modern historians generally connect the name with the 13th-century Mongol commander Nogai, the view Xavier rejects.

Strange talesKalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)1840

Breaking into a Kalmyk tomb

Adèle describes how the party tricked their Kalmyk camel-drivers, broke into a satza (a small tomb-shrine for a high priest's ashes) through a window, 'took possession' of it with a silk handkerchief, and carried off two clay statues and some images. She knows the Kalmyks would see it as the worst sacrilege and treats it as a lark. A startling reminder of how European travellers collected 'curiosities'.

Like generous victors we contented ourselves with taking two small statues, and a few images. According to the notions of the Kalmucks, no sacrilege can compare with that of which we were now guilty. Yet no celestial fire reduced us to ashes

Adèle Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

Strange talesKalmyk steppe (Kalmykia)1840s

The white elephant with thirty-three heads

Summarising Kalmyk Buddhist books, the authors describe the world-mountain and its guardian elephant, two leagues long and white as snow, with thirty-three red heads, six trunks each spouting fountains, and a young virgin on every star above them. They note, in a footnote, similar marvels in medieval European cosmography. This is religious myth they report, not something anyone claimed to see.

where dwells an elephant two leagues long, white as snow, and named Gasar Sakitschin Koven (protector of the earth). This fabulous animal has thirty-three red heads, each furnished with six trunks, whence spout forth as many fountains, all surmounted with six stars.

Xavier Hommaire de Hell (English translation, 1847)

About Kalmyks (Buddhist cosmology)

Keep in mind

A second-hand summary of Kalmyk religious books; the authors do not name the text they used.
