D'Anville's maps of China and Chinese Tartary

The atlas of d'Anville's maps of China, Chinese Tartary and Tibet (1737), with a foreword on how the Jesuit survey was made and an eyewitness 'Relation of Bukharia'.

Catalogue title Memoir of M. d’Anville, first geographer to the king, on China (defence of his China and Chinese Tartary maps and their sources, 1776; 47 pp.)

Jean-Baptiste Bourguignon d'Anville

Read the 4 passagesOpen the original book

Jean-Baptiste Bourguignon d'Anville (1697-1782), the French king's geographer, drew the maps for du Halde's Description of China (1735) from the survey that Jesuit missionaries made for the Kangxi emperor between 1708 and 1717. The catalogue record is for d'Anville's 1776 memoir defending those maps, but the corpus copy under this record is the related Nouvel atlas de la Chine, de la Tartarie chinoise et du Thibet (1737), issued as a fifth, atlas volume to a quarto edition of du Halde. Its foreword explains which maps were measured and which compiled, and it prints an anonymous 'Relation de la Boucharie' by a writer who says he had been in Little Bukhara under Kalmyk rule.

Who “Tartar” means here
'Tartarie' is the map label for the lands north and west of China proper (Manchuria, Mongolia, Dzungaria). In the Relation, 'Tartares' are corrected to 'or rather the Kalmyks' who ruled Little Bukhara, and the Manchus are the 'Tartars' who conquered China.
Why read it
It shows how the standard 18th-century map of 'Chinese Tartary' was made, from Jesuit triangulation and Gerbillon's travel notes, and admits that the lands towards the Caspian were known only imperfectly. The Relation adds a rare eyewitness look at town life in Kashgaria.
Watch out
This is not the 1776 memoir named in the catalogue. The atlas OCR is poor, and the Relation de la Boucharie is anonymous and undated.

Bukhara, 'twice the size of Moscow', in Great Tartary

The Relation opens by noting that geographers count Bukharia as part of Great Tartary, because 'the Tartars, or rather the Kalmyks' had invaded part of it; the author insists it is a separate state, just as China remained China under its Tartar conquerors. Great Bukharia has several towns, each with its own khan, of which Bukhara is said to be twice as large as Moscow.

Geographers usually regard Bukharia as part of Great Tartary. Indeed the Tartars, or rather the Kalmyks, have invaded part of it … There are several towns in Great Bukharia, and each has its own khan or regent. The chief ones are Bukhara, which is said to be twice as large as the city of Moscow in Russia

BuildingsKashgarearly 18th century

Stone houses and mud-brick ovens in Little Bukharia

The anonymous author, who says he lived in Little Bukharia, describes its townspeople's houses as built of stone and fairly good, with few furnishings: no tables or chairs, only Chinese chests on which mattresses were laid. Slaves bought from the Kalmyks, Russians and others cooked in iron pots set into a hearth, and some houses had small ovens of clay or unbaked brick, like their walls.

The houses of the Bukharans are of stone and passably good. Their furniture is scanty and plain. They have neither tables nor chairs. … Some also have small ovens, which are made, like the rest of their walls, of clay or of unbaked bricks.

Daily lifeKashgarearly 18th century

Cutting out the illness with a knife

The Relation describes the Bukharans' cure for sickness: a mullah reads from a book, blows on the patient several times and makes passes with a sharp knife around his face, believing this cuts the root of the illness, which they hold to be the Devil's work. The dead, it adds, are buried with a Koran on the chest in a pleasant grove fenced with a hedge or palisade.

When a Bukharan falls ill, this is the remedy he uses: a mullah reads him a passage from some book, blows on him several times, and with a well-sharpened knife makes various passes all round the sick man's face. They imagine that by this operation they cut the root of the illness, which they say is the work of the Devil.

How the map of Tartary was put together

The atlas foreword, summarising du Halde, says the Jesuits' manuscript maps passed through d'Anville's hands before engraving. For the general map of Tartary he used the travel memoirs of Father Gerbillon checked against the sheet maps, and for the lands between Tibet, Tartary and the Caspian, where the missionaries had only imperfect information, he combined their notes with whatever else he could learn.

they passed through the hands of Mr d'Anville … who then made the general maps from them. For that of Tartary he used the memoirs of Father Gerbillon, comparing them with the particular maps … as the missionaries had some knowledge, though imperfect, of the countries lying between Tibet, Tartary and the Caspian Sea, Mr d'Anville made use of it

Why it matters

D'Anville's own statement of the sources behind the standard eighteenth-century maps of Chinese Tartary and Tibet (M013, M016, M022): needed to judge what on those maps is survey, what is Jesuit report, and what is inference.
