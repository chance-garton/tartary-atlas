Evliya Celebi's Book of Travels: Crimea, the Kalmyks and the Volga (vol. 7)

An Ottoman traveller's vivid, embroidered account of Crimea, the Kalmyk steppe, Kazan, Astrakhan and the Tatars, 1665 to 1667.

Catalogue title Book of Travels, volumes 7 and 8 (Crimea, the Caucasus, the Kalmyk lands, the Volga, Azov, the Balkans), Istanbul: Turk Tarih Encumeni / Devlet Matbaasi, 1928

اولیا چلبی سیاحتنامه‌سی، جلد ۷ و ۸

Evliya Celebi (1611-c.1685)

Evliya Celebi (1611 to c.1685) travelled across the Ottoman world for four decades and wrote his ten-volume Book of Travels in Cairo in the 1670s and 1680s. Volume 7 covers his journeys with the Crimean khan Mehmed Giray IV in 1665 to 1667: the Crimean capital Bahcesaray and other towns, campaigns against Cossacks, Kalmyks and Russians, and a journey he says he made to the Kalmyks and up the Volga to Astrakhan, Sarai and Kazan. He writes from what he saw mixed with hearsay and embroidery. The corpus copy is the 1928 Istanbul printed edition (Kilisli Rifat), whose OCR is partly readable; quotes were checked against the page images.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Tatar means above all the Crimean Tatars (subjects of the Giray khans) and the Nogays, but Evliya also uses it for the Volga Tatars of Kazan ('Hashdek'), Astrakhan and Sarai. He calls the Kalmyks a separate people, not Muslims, but groups them with Chinggis Khan's heirs.
Why read it
The richest non-European picture of 'Little Tartary' in the 1660s: a stone-and-tile Tatar capital, the khan's palace, the slave market, Kalmyk houses on carts, and Kazan's mosques and madrasas standing in ruins under Russian rule.
Watch out
Evliya exaggerates numbers and sometimes invents episodes; the Volga, Kazan and some Kalmyk passages are doubted by scholars. The 1928 print is uncritical and censored; quotes follow its text.

CitiesBakhchysarai, Crimea1666

Bahcesaray: a city of red-tiled houses in a gorge

Evliya describes the Crimean khans' capital as a great city lying in a wide gorge between two cliffs, about eight thousand paces long. The Churuk Su stream runs through the middle, crossed by forty-three stone and timber bridges. On both sides, storey above storey, stand masonry houses roofed with red tiles and set in gardens, climbing to the foot of the cliffs.

Through the very middle of the city runs the Salachik stream … and over this Churuk Su, inside the city, one crosses from one side to the other at forty-three places by stone and timber bridges; and to right and left of the city, storey upon storey, lower and upper, all are masonry buildings roofed from end to end with ruby-red tiles.

CitiesKazan, Tatarstan1666

Kazan: stone ruins of a Tatar capital, now Muscovite

Evliya calls Kazan a former throne city of Chinggis Khan's line, now 'an ill-fated capital in Muscovite hands'. He says thousands of khans (caravanserais), mosques, madrasas, baths and shops lie as domed and arched ruins, while people now live in some three thousand houses roofed with rushes and reeds, with twenty churches and seven mosques for the many Nogay and Hashdek Muslims.

Formerly this too was the throne city of Chinggis Khan, passing from king to king; now in Muscovite hands it has become an ill-fated capital. The domes and Chosroes-like arches and fortresses of many thousands of khans, great mosques, charitable foundations, mosques, madrasas, dervish lodges, baths, shops and cloth-halls stand abandoned … Today there are some three thousand houses roofed with rushes and reeds.

CitiesAstrakhan1666

Astrakhan: Tatar nomads outside, reed houses inside

Evliya says thousands of Astrakhan Tatars live outside the city along the Volga in their oba (tent camps), moving like the Nogays, and adds the local legend that the name comes from a dragon (azhder) that ravaged the country until a khan killed it. Inside the walls he counts monasteries, churches and a thousand-odd reed-roofed houses, and in the outer town thirteen thousand houses roofed with reeds and earth and seven mosques.

Many thousands of them live outside the city on the bank of the Volga in their tent-camps; these too are Tatars by origin, and like the Nogays they camp and move with their obas … In ancient times this city lay in ruins, and a dragon was born here … then a heroic khan killed this dragon, and because the land became safe they call this country Azhder-khan.

CitiesSarai (lower Volga)1666

Sarai in 1666: nine thousand reed-roofed houses under Moscow

Evliya says the Sarai of his day was a city full of all kinds of Tatars, now in Muscovite hands. Half the subjects and soldiers were Muslim Kazan Tatars ('Hashdek') and half Muscovite Christians. The town had about nine thousand wretched houses of planks, rushes and reeds and seven monasteries. He names Nogay, Yaman, Teb and Yishdak Tatars living there as subjects of Moscow since its conquest.

(On the buildings of the city of Sarai.) In all there are nine thousand wretched houses covered with planks, rushes and reeds, and there are seven great monasteries in all … Today this city of Sarai is a city filled with every kind of Tatar people.

BuildingsBakhchysarai, Crimea1666

The khan's palace: stone walls and iron gates

Evliya's tour of the Crimean khan's palace (the Khan Saray) says it is surrounded on all four sides by gardens, like a fortress, with walls of masonry cut stone but no battlements or ramparts. It has four strong iron gates, a mint, kitchens, a garden gate and the harem gate guarded by black eunuchs, and many storeyed pavilions and halls, each built by a different khan.

First: because this heart-delighting palace has gardens like Iram on all four sides, it is a garden palace (bagche saray). On all four sides it is a most private palace with walls of masonry cut stone, square like a fortress, like the palace of Shaddad; but around this palace there are no battlements or ramparts … It has strong, solid iron gates in four places in all.

Daily lifeKarasubazar (Bilohirsk), Crimea1666

The slave market at Karasu

Evliya describes a great slave market beside the walls of Shirin Bey's khan at Karasubazar, where mothers were sold away from sons and brothers from sisters amid weeping. He calls the traders a merciless people, cursed in a saying as 'sellers of men', and warns buyers about the tricks of dealers and midwives. The Crimean economy rested heavily on captives taken in raids.

At the head of the great bridge, in a wide square below the wall of Shirin Bey's khan, there is a huge slave market; this too is an ill-omened market, a lesson to behold, for of them it is said: 'The seller of men, the cutter of trees and the breaker of stone, God curse him in this world and the next' … They are slave-dealing peoples, for this people is exceedingly merciless.

Daily lifeLower Volga (Sarai region)1666

Horsemeat, mare's milk and 'no lies' among the Sarai Tatars

On the Tatars around old Sarai, Evliya writes that they love horseflesh above all, some boiling it and some eating it raw with the blood running, and drink boza, kumiss (mare's milk) and camel's milk. He claims they know no backbiting, slander or lies, since they fear no one. All these Tatars are desert-dwellers who move with their camel-carts, and they became Muslims (Hanafi and Shafi'i) after Chinggis Khan.

What all of them love most is horse meat: many tribes cook horse meat, and many tribes eat the meat raw with the blood running. Instead of water they drink boza, mare's-milk kumiss and camel's milk … and all the Tatars are desert-dwellers; they mount their camel-carts and migrate.

Daily lifeKalmyk steppe, lower Volga1666

Kalmyks who live in houses on carts

Evliya says the Kalmyks have no fleas, lice, bedbugs, snakes, scorpions or tarantulas among them, because their round houses stand on carts and they live on the open steppe. He then begins his description of their appearance: small eyes, said to come from the salt mines of the region.

Among them there are no fleas, lice, bedbugs, snakes, tarantulas or scorpions at all, because their round houses are on carts and they are desert-dwellers.

The nameBakhchysarai, Crimeafounding legend, told 1666

How Bahcesaray was founded, 'as the Tatar historians say'

Evliya reports the Crimean Tatars' own story of their origins: the first conquerors of Crimea settled at Eski Yurt near Salachik, coming from Mahan, Ahlat, Kerman and Yesu, and from Kazan, Astrakhan and Sarai. As the Tatars multiplied and the old site grew too small, they settled at Bahcesaray and spread over the whole peninsula, while the 'Genoese infidels' stayed in their seaside fortresses. He names Jochi Khan and 'Artogmuz Khan' as the conquerors behind its first building.

According to the Tatar historians, the first conqueror of Crimea settled at the Eski Salachik yurt; Tatars came to Crimea from the cities of Mahan, Ahlat, Kerman and Yesu and from the lands of Kazan, Astrakhan and Sarai, and as they multiplied they no longer fitted into the old yurt, and settled at this Bahcesaray site.

The nameKalmyk steppe, lower Volga1666

Kalmyks and the hadith of the Turks

Evliya lists the many spellings of the Kalmyk name and says all historians call them the 'yellow folk' (Bani Asfar). He applies to them a hadith about God's soldiers in the east 'whose name is Turk'. He adds that twenty years earlier the Kalmyks were not on this steppe and 140 Nogay tribes grazed it, sending butter to Taman to be sold cheaply in Istanbul; when the Kalmyks arrived the Nogays were routed and butter became dear.

Kalmuk, Kalmak, Kalmaq, Kalmakh and Kalmagh: all the historians call them the people of Bani Asfar [the 'yellow folk'] … Indeed, twenty years ago this Kalmyk people were not present on this vast steppe, and all the hundred and forty tribes of the Nogay people camped and migrated on the open steppe.

Strange talesBeyond the Kalmyk steppe (unspecified, far east)told 1666

The Kalmyk 'Kaaba' and Alexander's wall

Evliya records what Kalmyks told him about a domed shrine they call their Kaaba, far to the east beyond a sea of ice. He adds his own learned gloss: Alexander built the Wall of Gog and Magog from every kind of metal, the mountains the Kalmyks call the Yildirak Dag, then built this dome with ancient sages and hid his treasures in it. On a following page Kalmyk elders say their ancestors died from a deafening noise behind the metal mountains.

[Alexander] built the Barrier of Alexander, the mountains, out of every kind of metal; it is the Barrier of Gog, which the Kalmyk people call the 'Yildirak Dag' [the glittering mountain]; afterwards Alexander built the dome which they call their Kaaba.

Card wording

firsthand observation of the 1660s journeys (Crimea 1665-1666, Kalmyk and Volga lands, Azov, Caucasus) mixed with hearsay, literary embellishment and invented episodes; the author's veracity varies by passage

Cautions
  • IA catalogue date 1896 conflicts with the 1928 imprint of vols 7-8 (preface and colophon confirm 1928). The 1928 edition is censored and uncritical; the Yapi Kredi transcription (2003) and the Topkapi manuscript are the scholarly base. Evliya's Volga-Kazan itinerary and some Kalmyk passages are considered invented or exaggerated.

Why it matters

The most extensive Ottoman eyewitness description of Crimea, the Nogay and Kalmyk steppe and the Volga in the 1660s, including the famous (and partly fictional) journey through the Kalmyk lands; a key non-European voice on 'Little Tartary'.
