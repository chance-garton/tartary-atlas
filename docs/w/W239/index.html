Gemelli Careri's round-the-world journey: China under the 'Eastern Tartars'

A Neapolitan traveller's eyewitness account of Kangxi's China in 1695 to 1696, including the Great Wall and Beijing's 'Tartar City'.

Catalogue title Voyage round the World, part IV: the most notable things seen in China (with chapters on the origin of the Eastern Tartars and the Manchu conquest, the Lama and Tartary)

Giovanni Francesco Gemelli Careri

Giovanni Francesco Gemelli Careri (1651 to 1725), a Neapolitan lawyer, travelled round the world between 1693 and 1698, paying his own way. Part IV of his Giro del Mondo (Naples 1699 to 1700) covers China: he went from Canton to Beijing in 1695 to 1696, met the Kangxi emperor, visited the Great Wall and talked with Jesuits such as Filippo Grimaldi. He adds compiled chapters on religion and on the origin of the 'Eastern Tartars', the Manchu dynasty. The corpus holds the 1699 and 1700 printings of part IV and a 1727 edition; quotes come from the 1699 copy and one from the 1700 copy.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Manchus ('Eastern Tartars') ruling China as the Qing, and the Mongols of 'Imperial Tartary' under them; also the 'Western Tartars' (Oirat Dzungars, 'Eluth') and Chinese Muslims said to come from 'Great Tartary'.
Why read it
A lay eyewitness who walked on the Great Wall, saw the Tartar and Chinese cities of Beijing, and records how the Manchu conquest changed Chinese dress and hair.
Watch out
Critics once doubted he went at all; his journey is now generally accepted, but he borrows freely from Jesuit books, especially on Tartar history, religion and the Dalai Lama.

Beijing's two cities: the Tartar City and the Chinese City

Careri describes Qing Beijing as two joined cities: the old walled 'City of the Tartars' around the palace, where the Manchu bannermen, officials and ministers lived, and the 'City of the Chinese', built later beside it for the overflow of inhabitants, about the same four leagues round and joined to the Tartar City by a single shared wall.

The City of the Chinese (which was built next to it to make room for the multitude of inhabitants) is of the same size as the City of the Tartars, being four leagues round; but its shape is not like that one ... on which side it joins the Tartar City by a single wall that divides them.

BuildingsGreat Wall of China (north of Beijing)1696 (visit); wall credited to Qin Shi Huang

The Great Wall is brick, built against the Tartars

Careri rode out from Beijing and walked four miles up the mountains to see the Wall himself. It stood 15 to 20 feet high, higher and wider in the valleys, room for six horses abreast, built 'of large fire-baked bricks with few stones', with strong square towers two bowshots apart running to the sea. He credits the emperor 'Xi-hoamti' with building it more than 1800 years earlier 'as a defence against the raids of the Tartars'.

That wall is 15 feet high in some parts and 20 in others; but in the valleys it is much higher and wider, so that six horses can comfortably go abreast on it. The construction is of large bricks baked in the fire, with few stones, and from time to time it has its towers, very strong and square, one as far from the next as two bowshots

Tartars in huts, now made to build villages

Describing the lands between Beijing and the Russian frontier, Careri says the vast 'Imperial Tartary' owned by the emperor of China was uncultivated and wooded, and its 'poor Tartars' lived in huts without walled houses, like the ancient nomads with portable dwellings. But the present emperor was now 'civilising' them, beginning to have villages built and gathering them in, with forty tributary princes including the Khalkha.

those poor Tartars living there under huts, without walled houses, in the manner of the ancient Numidians or Hamaxobians, who, all given to the pastoral life, did not even have houses but went to live wherever it was most convenient with their portable huts. But the present Emperor of Tartary, now civilising them well, is beginning to have villages built for them and to gather them into these in communities

Daily lifeChina1644 onward (described 1696)

The Tartar haircut forced on the Chinese

Before the Tartars began to rule, Careri says, the Chinese wore their hair long, wound behind the head in a knot pinned with silver. After 'the empire of the Tartar' everyone was ordered, on pain of death, to cut it and adopt the Tartar style: shaved head with one tuft, which the Chinese wore braided, sometimes down to the legs. Loose wide-sleeved robes were also banned for Tartar dress, 'which the Chinese felt, and still feel, in their hearts'.

Before the Tartars began to rule, the Chinese wore their hair long, winding it behind the head ... but after the empire of the Tartar, everyone was ordered on pain of death to cut it, and to go in the Tartar manner, with the head shaved and a tuft

Daily lifeNanjing1695

Two million Muslims 'from Great Tartary'

In Nanjing Careri notes that not all inhabitants are Chinese: there are many 'Moors come from Great Tartary', two million across China according to Father Grimaldi, who marry their daughters only within their own sect and so multiply 'like locusts'. The royal palace sits in a citadel held by a Tartar (Manchu) garrison that let no one in.

The inhabitants of Nanking are not all Chinese: there are also many Moors who came from Great Tartary (Father Filippo Grimaldi assuring me that in all China there are two million of them), whose policy is not to marry their daughters except within their own sect

'Eastern Tartars': a Manchu dynasty from a small horde chief

Careri opens his chapter on the 'Eastern Tartars' by saying their princes' origin is so obscure that everyone who writes of it tells fables. They began only in this century from 'a small Horde chief, or captain of outlaws or wandering Tartars' called Tien-mim (Nurhaci). He goes on to divide Tartary into Eastern (Manchu lands, poor, with only two or three small towns) and a far more powerful Western Tartary, both mostly living in tents.

The origin of these princes is so obscure that whoever has tried to speak of it has fallen into fables. They had their beginning in this century from a small chief of a Horde, or captain of outlaws or wandering Tartars, who was called Tien-mim

Original wording

E' così oscura di questi Principi l'origine, che chiunque ha voluto parlarne ha dato nelle favole. Eglino hanno avuto principio in questo secolo da un picciol Capo d'Horda, o Capitano di fuorusciti, o Tartari erranti, che si chiamava Tien-mim

Strange talesGreat Wall of China1696

A wall up a cliff no Tartar horse could climb

Careri found the Wall one of the greatest and 'most senseless' works ever made. What made him laugh was seeing it carried to the top of a precipitous mountain where 'the Tartar cavalry' could never have climbed; and if the Chinese thought raiders could scramble up the cliffs, it was foolish to think so low a wall would stop them. A rare eyewitness who was unimpressed.

But what struck me as altogether laughable was to see the wall carried even to the peaks of a very high and precipitous mountain, where hardly even woodpeckers could cling, let alone the Tartar cavalry climb up to enter the country.

Original wording

Ma quel, che affatto mi sembrò da ridere, fu a vedere il muro tratto ancor fino alle cime d'un'altissimo, e straripevol monte, ove appena vi sarebbero appiccati i picchi: non che montar vi potesse la cavalleria Tartara per entrar nel paese.

Late-seventeenth-century Italian lay eyewitness of Qing China and of European beliefs about Tartary and the Lama, independent of the Jesuit relations; frequently cited on the Manchu-Chinese dual administration.
