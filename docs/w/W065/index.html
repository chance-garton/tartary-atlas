John Cook's travels as a doctor on Russia's Tartar frontier

A Scottish physician's memoir of Russian service at Astrakhan in the 1740s and a Russian embassy through Dagestan to Nadir Shah's Persia.

Full title Voyages and Travels through the Russian Empire, Tartary and Part of Persia

John Cook

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

John Cook (c. 1712 to after 1770), a Scottish doctor from Hamilton, served in Russia from 1736, spent years as a physician at Astrakhan, and in 1746 to 1747 went with a Russian embassy through Kizlyar, Dagestan and Derbent into Persia. He published his memoirs at Edinburgh in 1770. This corpus copy is Volume II only, which covers 1741 onward: Astrakhan politics, the Kalmyk khanate and its princess, raids on the Volga, and the embassy's march south. Most Tartar material is about Kalmyks, Karakalpaks and the Muslim peoples of Dagestan, drawn from his own experience plus local report.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
A general word for the non-Russian peoples of the steppe and Caucasus: Kalmyks (whom he calls Tartars alongside Cossacks), Karakalpaks, Nogai and other Muslim Tatars serving Russia as irregular cavalry, the 'Dagestan Tartars' of the hills north of Derbent, the 'Lezgi Tartars' to the south, Turkmen, Uzbeks and Crimean Tatars.
Why read it
A doctor's-eye view of steppe politics in the 1740s: a Kalmyk princess in her felt tent, salted butter tea, Kalmyk and Karakalpak raiding on the ice, and Tatar villages with houses on the Caucasus frontier.
Watch out
Only Volume II is in this corpus. Cook writes decades later, has personal quarrels (notably with Jonas Hanway), and reports much hearsay about Kalmyk 'barbarities'. The 1770 OCR prints the long s as f.

CitiesEndirey (Andreyevo), DagestanDecember 1746

A 'Tartarian or Circassian' village of four hundred houses

Marching south from the Terek, the embassy reached a large village of 300 to 400 houses called Andreofske Derevna, ruled by a prince named Alish under Russian protection. Cook is unsure whether to call it Tartar or Circassian. The prince, drunk from celebrating, showed the scars of many raids and boasted of horses stolen from Turkey and Georgia.

at seven o'clock at night we arrived, very cold, in a large Tartarian or Circassian village, called Andreofske Derevna, consisting of between three and four hundred houses. The prince of this village is called Alish, and is under the protection of Russia.

John Cook (published 1770)

BuildingsLower Volga near Astrakhan1742

A Kalmyk princess in a silk-lined felt tent

Sent to treat the widowed Kalmyk princess, held under Russian guard in a dispute over the khanate, Cook entered a very large beehive-shaped tent covered with felt and lined with Persian silk. She lay on a down bed covered with silk, with priests and chiefs standing round. He decided her illness was confinement and grief.

In company with the major, the governor's only son, I went to a very large tent, in form of a bee-hive, covered with felt, and lined with Persian silk. When I entered into the tent, the Princess was lying on a bed of down covered with silk; her face was from us. Many Kalmucks well dressed were standing with great decorum

John Cook (published 1770)

BuildingsDerbentJanuary 1747

Towers on the hills credited to Alexander

Cook describes Derbent's huge walls of natural shell-stone and then notes towers on the hills to the west. The inhabitants told him Alexander the Great built them and ran them all the way to the Black Sea to hold back the Dagestan Tartars. This echoes the old legend of Alexander's wall against Gog and Magog.

Upon the tops of the hills, west from Derbent, are to be seen some towers, which the inhabitants say were built by Alexander the Great; and that he continued these towers from Derbent west to the Black Sea, in order to curb the Dagestan Tartars.

John Cook (published 1770)

About 'Dagestan Tartars' (Muslim highlanders)

Keep in mind

Daily lifeLower Volga near Astrakhan1742

Tea with salt and butter

The Kalmyk princess offered Cook a dish of tea. The tea was good, he says, but the Kalmyks put salt and butter into it; he drank it out of politeness and then hurried off to get rid of it, while the Russian major laughed at him. It is an early Western description of Mongolian salted milk tea.

Accordingly a cup of tea was brought, but such as I never had tasted before this time. The tea, I dare say, was good, but the Kalmucks put salt and butter into it ; nevertheless I drank it, though extremely disagreeable

John Cook (published 1770)

Daily lifeAstrakhan1744

Two horses each, and the Volga swum

After a Kalmyk raid on the Karakalpaks, Cook describes how Kalmyks went to war: bow, quiver and scimitar, at least two horses per man, herds grazing as they marched. He says he had seen thousands cross the Volga by swimming their animals, and that lame or dead horses were cut up and shared as food.

The Kalmucks in time of war are armed with a bow and a quiver full of arrows of different shapes, and a large scymitar. Every man has at least two horses … They need neither bridges nor boats to cross rivers, for I have seen thousands cross the Volga by swimming their cattle over

John Cook (published 1770)

Daily lifeVolga deltaWinter 1744 to 1745

Prisoners tied beneath the horses' bellies

In revenge for a Kalmyk raid, some 15,000 Karakalpaks crossed the frozen Volga, killed Kalmyk old and young and carried off the youths. Cornered by a Russian force, they broke through and fled, leaving about three hundred Kalmyk prisoners bound to horses by their feet. The Kalmyks then rode off with thousands of the Russians' own horses and camels.

they, being about fifteen thousand, made a very sudden unexpected attack upon the Russians, and actually, bravely broke through them, got over the ice, and fled to the desart, leaving about three hundred Kalmuck prisoners fixed to their horses, by tying their feet with cords to one another under the horses bellies.

John Cook (published 1770)

Daily lifeLower Volga1740s

Allies, not subjects: the Kalmyks and Russia

Explaining why Russian generals let irregular cavalry plunder freely, Cook says these troops were Cossacks, Kalmyks or other Tartars on the borders. The Kalmyks owned no subjection, only Russian protection, and could not be forced to move; even their khan could not act without the consent of his many elders, some of them lords of five or six thousand fighting men.

The Kalmucks acknowledge no subjection, but only live under the protection of Russia. They are barbarous to a great degree, but yet they must be treated with great lenity ; for, if they do not incline to leave their own country, the whole force of Russia is not able to oblige them, nor even their prince, unless with the approbation of their seniors

John Cook (published 1770)

Daily lifeDagestan1747

Muslim Tartars with stone crosses and Christian feast days

Cook says all the Tartars from Astrakhan to Derbent were Sunni Muslims, but he suspected many had once been Christians. They held yearly gatherings they could not explain, which he says fell on Greek Orthodox feast days, and he was told there were many stone crosses in the hills where they still met.

The Tartars from the borders of Astrachan to Derbent, are all Mahometans, of the sect of the Turks; but some things make me believe, that a majority of them had formerly professed the Christian religion. They have meetings at certain times of the year, where they divert themselves, without being able to account for the cause, any other way than that their ancestors did so before them

John Cook (published 1770)

About 'Tartars' between Astrakhan and Derbent (Kumyks and Dagestani peoples)

Daily lifeMughan Plain, Azerbaijan1747

A hundred thousand tents on the Mughan plain

Crossing the plains near the Aras River, Cook was told that the kings of Persia bred their best horses there, and that the plains had held more than 100,000 tents of roving people 'as the Kalmucks do in Astrachan' when Nadir Shah took the throne. He passed a few of the last groups being moved to Khorasan on Nadir's orders.

these plains were formerly inhabited by roving people living in tents, as the Kalmucks do in Astrachan ; and that upwards of a hundred thousand tents were in these happy plains when Kouli Khan mounted the throne of Persia.

John Cook (published 1770)

The nameDagestan1740s

Dagestan Tartars versus Lezgi Tartars

Cook admits writers were confused about the peoples of the eastern Caucasus and sets his own rule: 'Dagestan Tartars' for the hill people between Derbent and Circassia, 'Lezgi Tartars' for those south and south-east of Derbent to the Kura. It shows 'Tartar' being used simply as a label for Muslim mountaineers who spoke many languages.

The difference between the Dagestan and Lesgee Tartars, I think is not clear in authors; I therefore desire my readers to remember, that I call the Dagestan Tartars, those who inhabit the hills between Derbent and Circassia; and I call those who inhabit the hills south, and south-east of Derbent, all the way to the north banks of Cura, Lesgee Tartars.

John Cook (published 1770)

Strange talesNear Astrakhan1742

A jar of old Tartar coins falls out of a cliff

Resting under red gravel cliffs on the Volga near Astrakhan, Cook was told by his colonel that merchants picnicking there had seen part of the cliff collapse, releasing an earthen jar full of old Tartar coins in gold and silver. The colonel said he had seen some of them.

when they were taking their dinner, part of the cliff fell down, and with it, an earthen jar full of old Tartar coin, some of which he had seen, both gold and silver.

John Cook, reporting a Russian colonel (published 1770)
