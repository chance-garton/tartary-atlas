Clarke's travels: the Greek and Balkan volume

The Cambridge traveller's account of Greece, Macedonia, Thrace and the road to Vienna in 1801 to 1802, where 'Tartars' are mostly Ottoman couriers.

Catalogue title Travels in Various Countries … Part I: Russia, Tartary, and Turkey

Edward Daniel Clarke

Edward Daniel Clarke (1769-1822), a Cambridge mineralogist and antiquary, travelled from 1799 to 1802 through Russia, the Crimea, Turkey, the Levant, Greece and the Balkans, and published his Travels in six parts from 1810. The catalogue card is for Part I (Russia, Tartary and Turkey, 1810), with its famous Crimean chapters. The corpus copy, however, is a different volume: Part II, Section III (London, 1816), covering Greece, Macedonia, Thrace, Constantinople and the ride to Vienna in 1801 to 1802. Here Tartars appear mainly as the Ottoman mounted couriers called 'Tatars', plus a few backward glances at the steppe tumuli and Crimean peoples Clarke had seen earlier.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
In this volume, mostly the Ottoman government couriers, a job title not an ethnic name, as a quoted footnote explains. Elsewhere 'Tartary' means the southern Russian steppe and Crimea Clarke had crossed earlier, known for its burial mounds.
Why read it
A vivid eyewitness picture of the 'Tartar' couriers who carried the Ottoman post, and a clear statement that this 'Tartar' was a profession, not a people.
Watch out
This is not the Crimea volume named on the catalogue card: the Crimean and steppe material of Part I is not in this corpus copy. Clarke refers back to it only in passing.

CitiesChufut-Kale, Crimea1800 (recalled 1816)

Chufut-Kale, the 'Castle of Miscreants'

Contrasting the Jews of Constantinople with the Karaite Jews of the Crimea, Clarke praises the Karaites' character. He notes that their town was known by a Turkish insult, Tchufut-kaleh, the 'Castle of Miscreants' (his footnote glosses Tchifuit as 'a vile miscreant'), and says their good name had turned it into an honourable title.

How different from the character borne by the Karaite Jews of the Crimea! whose virtues have rendered even the opprobrious name, derived from their Turkish brethren, an honourable title ; for their place of residence is called Tchufut-kaleh, the "Castle of Miscreants."

BuildingsMalkara, Thrace (Turkey)January 1802

Burial mounds 'precisely similar to those of Tartary'

Riding across the bare plains of Thrace, Clarke is reminded of the steppes of southern Russia and its tumuli (kurgans), which he had seen on his earlier journey. He does not say who built either set; he treats them simply as the same kind of ancient earth mound.

This part of Thrace resembles the steppes in the South of Russia ; and to add to the similitude of the two countries, there are here tumuli precisely similar to those of Tartary.

BuildingsLarissa, Thessaly1801

Unrobbed mounds, unlike those of Tartary

On the plain near Larissa Clarke saw many large tumuli in good condition, some with shepherds' huts built on top. He notes that, unlike the mounds of 'Tartary', they did not seem to have been dug open for treasure. The steppe kurgans were well known for being plundered for their gold.

They did not seem to have been opened and ransacked for the hidden treasure which it is possible some of them contain, as it has often happened in Tartary ; but, upon some of them, small huts had been constructed, as dwellings for the shepherds.

Daily lifeFarsala, Thessaly1801

Ten minutes, a pipe and a spoonful of coffee

At a khan in Pharsalus Clarke watched 'Tartar' couriers change horses. Each squatted against the wall for about ten minutes, smoked hard, gulped a spoonful of muddy coffee, said 'God be praised' and rode on, travelling night and day. Clarke says no couriers in the world could match their endurance, and that Europeans travelling alone in Turkey disguised themselves in their dress.

the Tartar squats (for it cannot be called sitting) with his back against the wall, supporting himself upon the soles of his feet, and inhaling rapidly the fumes of his pipe upon his lungs; sending it back in curling volumes through his nostrils. Then, if he can procure about as much muddy coffee as would fill the bowl of a table-spoon, he utters his Alhandilla! (God be praised !)

The nameOttoman Empirec. 1800 (quoted 1816)

'Their name by no means indicates their origin'

In a footnote Clarke quotes Thomas Thornton's book on Turkey (1809) to explain the couriers: 'Tartar' was the name of the job, not of a nation. The men came from every province and were marked out by the Tartar calpac (fur cap) they wore instead of a turban. A good reminder that 'Tartar' could mean a role or a costume.

The Tartars are public couriers, much respected for their good conduct and fidelity. Their name by no means indicates their origin, as they are taken indifferently from all the provinces in the empire, and are distinguished by the Tartar calpac, which they wear instead of the turban.

Strange talessouthern Russian steppec. 1800 (written 1816)

Russian treasure hunters in 'Tartarian tombs'

In a long footnote on tomb treasures, Clarke suggests that Turkish beliefs about gold hidden in ancient ruins may come from real finds in tombs. He adds that the Russians had found 'immense treasure' in 'Tartarian tombs', referring readers to his earlier book on the Tomb of Alexander (1805). The graves he means are the steppe kurgans, not necessarily Tatar at all.

The immense treasure found by the Russians in Tartarian tombs has been alluded to by the author, upon a former occasion.

Strange talesBoeotia, Greece1801

No poets on the flat steppe

Admiring the mountains near Thebes, Clarke argues that great poetry needs great scenery, and that flat countries like Scythia or Belgium never produced it. Even Homer, he says, would not have been a poet if raised in 'oriental Tartary'. It is a period prejudice, not an observation, but it shows how Tartary served as a byword for dull, flat emptiness.

Even Homer himself, if he had been a native of oriental Tartary, and had been cradled and brought up under the impressions made by such scenery, and under the influence of such a climate, would never have been a poet.
