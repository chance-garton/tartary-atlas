The Book of Ser Marco Polo (Yule's edition)

The Venetian merchant who served Kublai Khan for some seventeen years describes the Mongol 'Tartars', their camps, capitals, customs and empire, c. 1271 to 1295.

Catalogue title The Book of Ser Marco Polo

Marco Polo; Rustichello of Pisa

Marco Polo (1254-1324) travelled with his father and uncle across Asia to the court of Kublai Khan, stayed about seventeen years, and returned to Venice in 1295. In a Genoese prison around 1298 he dictated his book to the romance writer Rustichello of Pisa. It mixes his own observation (much of China and the Mongol court), information gathered in service, and hearsay about distant lands. His Tartar chapters cover Karakorum, Mongol houses and wagons, marriage, religion, war, law, Kublai's palaces at Shangdu and Khanbaliq, and the Golden Horde. This corpus copy is Henry Yule's 1871 English translation in two volumes, with very extensive notes by Yule.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongols of Chinggis Khan and his heirs, including Kublai's court in China ('Tartars of the Levant' for the Ilkhans of Persia, 'Tartars of the Ponent' for the Golden Horde on the Volga). He also tells a legend of the Tartars' early days under 'Prester John'.
Why read it
The most famous medieval account of the Mongols at the height of their power, from someone who served them: felt houses on carts beside marble and cane palaces, planned capitals and the empire's customs.
Watch out
Polo's text survives in many differing manuscripts and passed through Rustichello's romance style. He repeats hearsay and legends (Prester John, Gog and Magog, the Land of Darkness) alongside what he saw. Many notes quoted on the same pages are Yule's 19th-century commentary.

CitiesKarakorum13th century

Karakorum: an earthen rampart, for stone is scarce

Polo calls Karakorum a city about three miles round, enclosed by an earthen rampart because stone was scarce, with a citadel and a governor's palace, and 'the first city that the Tartars possessed'. Yule's note adds that Ögedei built its walls and palace in 1234, and quotes Rubruck's famous verdict that apart from the palace it was no better than the village of St Denis.

Caracoron is a city of some three miles in compass. [It is surrounded by a strong earthen rampart, for stone is scarce there. And beside it there is a great citadel wherein is a fine palace in which the Governor resides.] 'Tis the first city that the Tartars possessed after they issued from

CitiesMongolian steppe, bordering Manchuriabefore c. 1200 (legendary history)

Once a land with no towns at all

Polo begins his history of the Tartars by saying they originally lived on wide plains near 'Chorcha' (Manchuria) with no towns or villages, only rich pastures, and paid tribute to 'Unc Can', whom Europeans called Prester John. Yule's note identifies Chorcha as the Jurchen country and Unc Can as Toghrul, Wang Khan of the Keraits.

Originally the Tartars dwelt in the north on the borders of Chorcha. Their country was one of great plains ; and there were no towns or villages in it, but excellent pasture-lands, with great rivers and many sheets of water ; in fact it was a very fine and extensive region.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Keep in mind

Polo's early Mongol history is second- or thirdhand and mixed with the Prester John legend.

CitiesBeijing (Khanbaliq / Dadu)1267 onward

Kublai builds Taidu (Beijing) beside the old city

Polo explains that Kublai, warned by astrologers that the old city of Cambaluc would rebel, built a new, square city called Taidu (Dadu, the core of later Beijing) right beside it and moved the people there. He gives its walls as 24 miles round, of rammed earth ten paces thick. Yule's notes compare the plan with the Beijing of his own day and adjust the figures.

So he caused the present city to be built close beside the old one, with only a river between them. And he caused the people of the old city to be removed to the new town that he had founded ; and this is called Taidu. … it is all walled round with walls of earth which have a thickness of full ten paces at bottom

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Kublai Khan (Mongol emperor of China)

Keep in mind

Polo's measurements are too large; Yule's survey-based estimate is about 18 miles round. The city was designed and built by Chinese and other craftsmen for the Mongol court.

BuildingsMongolian steppec. 1275-1292

Round houses of wands and felt, doors to the south

Polo describes the classic Mongol dwelling: a round frame of light, tightly bound wands covered with felt, carried from camp to camp, always pitched with the door facing south. Families also had felt-covered wagons, rainproof and drawn by oxen and camels, in which women and children travelled. Yule's note compares the Kirghiz yurts of his own day.

Their houses are circular, and are made of wands covered with felts. These are carried along with them whithersoever they go ; for the wands are so strongly bound together, as well as so well combined, that the frame can be made very light. Whenever they erect these huts the door is always to the south.

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Tartars (Mongols)

Keep in mind

Yule notes the manuscripts differ on 'felts' (one reads 'ropes'); he chose felt as the right reading.

BuildingsShangdu (Xanadu), Inner Mongoliac. 1275

Xanadu: a marble palace and a portable palace of cane

At his summer capital Kublai had a marble palace with gilded, painted rooms inside a sixteen-mile walled park. He also had a second palace made of bamboo cane: gilded, roofed with split-cane tiles, held up by gilt dragon columns, and designed to be taken down and moved, with more than 200 silk cords to hold it against the wind. It is the steppe tent reimagined as palace architecture. This passage later inspired Coleridge's 'Kubla Khan'.

The construction of the Palace is so devised that it can be taken down and put up again with great celerity ; and it can all be taken to pieces and removed whithersoever the Emperor may command. When erected, it is stayed [against mishaps from the wind] by more than 200 cords of silk.

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871; bracketed words from other manuscripts)

Daily lifeMongoliac. 1275-1292

Natigay, the felt god of the household

Polo says the Tartars worship a high god of heaven and an earth god, Natigay, who guards children, cattle and crops. Every household had felt figures of him, his wife and children, and at meals they greased the idols' mouths with fat and sprinkled broth outside the door. Yule links Natigay to the Mongol 'ongon' spirit figures that Pallas later saw among the Buryats.

They show him great worship and honour, and every man hath a figure of him in his house, made of felt and cloth ; and they also make in the same manner images of his wife and children. … And when they eat, they take the fat of the meat and grease the god's mouth withal, as well as the mouths of his wife and children.

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Daily lifeMongol Empirec. 1275-1292

Units of ten, blood from a horse's vein and dried milk

Polo explains the decimal army (officers over ten, a hundred, a thousand and ten thousand, the 'toman'), scouts on every side, and how little gear a rider carried: two leather milk bottles, a small pot and a small tent. In emergencies they rode ten days without a fire, drinking blood from their horses' veins, and carried dried milk paste that churned into a meal inside the bottle as they rode.

And in case of great urgency they will ride ten days on end without lighting a fire or taking a meal. On such an occasion they will sustain themselves on the blood of their horses, opening a vein and letting the blood jet into their mouths, drinking till they have had enough, and then staunching it.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Tartar (Mongol) armies

Keep in mind

Blood-drinking is also reported by Herberstein, Schiltberger and others; Polo describes the practice rather than a scene he witnessed.

Daily lifeChina and Persiac. 1290s

The 'genuine Tartars' are going native

After praising Tartar warfare, Polo adds a striking remark: in his day the Mongols were changing. Those settled in China had taken up the religion and ways of the Chinese, and those in the Middle East had adopted Muslim customs. It is an eyewitness observation that the conquerors were being absorbed by the settled peoples they ruled.

But I must add also that in these days they are greatly degenerated ; for those who are settled in Cathay have taken up the practices of the Idolaters of the country, and have abandoned their own institutions ; whilst those who have settled in the Levant have adopted the customs of the Saracens.

Saw itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Mongols settled in China and in the Ilkhanate

Keep in mind

'Idolaters' means Buddhists and followers of Chinese religion; 'Saracens' means Muslims.

Daily lifeMongol Empire and Chinac. 1275-1292

Ghost weddings for dead children

Polo reports that when a boy and a girl died unmarried, their families arranged a marriage between them, drew up a contract, burned it so the couple would know in the next world, and burned paper pictures of the dowry. Yule notes this is really a Chinese custom, though he trusts Polo that Mongols practised it too.

And marry them they do, making a regular contract ! And when the contract papers are made out they put them in the fire, in order (as they will have it) that the parties in the other world may know the fact, and so look on each other as man and wife.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

The namePontic-Caspian steppe (Golden Horde)1237 to c. 1300

The Tartars of the West and their kings

Polo calls the Golden Horde the 'Tartars of the Ponent' (of the West). Their first king, 'Sain', conquered Russia, the Cumans, Alans, Circassians, Goths of Crimea and Khazaria. He lists the later khans down to Toqta, who reigned in Polo's time. Yule's note points out that 'Sain' (the Good) was really Batu's own surname, so Polo turned one khan into two.

The first lord of the Tartars of the Ponent was Sain, a very great and puissant king, who conquered Rosia and Comania, Alania, Lac, Menjar, Zic, Gothia, and Gazaria ; all these provinces were conquered by King Sain.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Strange talesAltai mountains (burial ground of the khans)1259

Twenty thousand killed on the road to a khan's grave

Polo says all the Great Khans were carried, however far away they died, to a mountain called Altay for burial, and the funeral escort killed everyone they met on the road to serve the dead ruler in the next world, along with his best horses. He claims more than 20,000 people died this way on Möngke's funeral journey. Yule notes Rashid al-Din reports the same custom for Chinggis.

When they are carrying the body of any Emperor to be buried with the others, the convoy that goes with the body doth put to the sword all whom they fall in with on the road, saying : "Go and wait upon your Lord in the other world !" … when Mongou Kaan died, more than 20,000 persons, who chanced to meet the body on its way, were slain

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

Strange talesTenduc (Inner Mongolia, near the Yellow River bend)c. 1275

Gog and Magog are really Ung and Mungul

In Tenduc, which he says was once Prester John's capital, Polo meets the legend of Gog and Magog, the wild nations Alexander walled in. He offers a down-to-earth explanation: the local names Ung and Mungul, two peoples who lived there before the Tartar migration, had been misheard in the West as Gog and Magog. Yule's notes identify the Ung as the Ongut.

Here also is what we call the country of Gog and Magog ; they, however, call it Ung and Mungul, after the names of two races of people that existed in that Province before the migration of the Tartars. Ung was the title of the people of the country, and Mungul a name sometimes applied to the Tartars.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Ongut and Mongols

Keep in mind

Polo's 'Prester John' line here is King George of the Ongut, a Nestorian Christian prince.

Strange talesCentral Asia (realm of Kaidu)1280

The khan's daughter who wrestled her suitors for horses

Kaidu Khan's daughter Aijaruc ('Bright Moon') refused to marry anyone who could not beat her. Each challenger staked horses, and she won more than 10,000. When a handsome prince staked 1,000 horses and her parents begged her to lose, she threw him on the palace floor anyway. She then rode to war with her father, snatching enemies from their ranks 'as deftly as a hawk'.

When both had taken post in the middle of the hall they grappled each other by the arms and wrestled this way and that, but for a long time neither could get the better of the other. At last, however, it so befel that the damsel threw him right valiantly on the palace pavement.

Heard itMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Aijaruc (Khutulun), daughter of Kaidu Khan

Keep in mind

Polo was not present; Persian sources confirm a warrior daughter of Kaidu named Khutulun.

Strange talesFar north beyond Russia (Arctic)reported c. 1290s

Raiding the Land of Darkness on mares with foals

Far to the north, Polo says, lies a Land of Darkness where no sun, moon or stars are seen. Tartars raid it riding mares whose foals they leave at the border, trusting the mares to find the way back to their young. The pale inhabitants hunt sables, ermine and black fox for the fur trade. Yule suggests the story garbles the Arctic winter night.

The Tartars however sometimes visit the country, and they do it in this way. They enter the region riding mares that have foals, and these foals they leave behind. After taking all the plunder that they can get, they find their way back by help of the mares, which are all eager to get back to their foals

Legend or rumourMarco Polo (translated by Henry Yule, 1871)

About Tartars (Golden Horde raiders) and northern fur hunters

Keep in mind

Pure hearsay; the mare-and-foal trick is an old travellers' tale also told of Alexander the Great.
