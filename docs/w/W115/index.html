Friar John of Marignolli's mission to the Great Khan

A Florentine friar and papal legate remembers the Tartar courts, Khanbaliq and the roads between them, 1338 to 1353, in asides scattered through his chronicle of Bohemia.

Full title Recollections of Eastern Travel

Giovanni de Marignolli

Read the 13 passagesOpen the original book

Giovanni de' Marignolli, a Florentine Franciscan, led the embassy Pope Benedict XII sent to the Great Khan in answer to a Mongol embassy. Travelling by Constantinople, Caffa, the Golden Horde court of Ozbeg Khan, Almaliq and Kamul, he reached Khanbaliq (Beijing) in 1342, stayed about three years, and came home by sea through Zayton, Quilon and Ceylon, reaching Avignon around 1353. Later, as chaplain to Emperor Charles IV, he wrote a rambling universal chronicle of Bohemia and dropped memories of the journey into it. This corpus copy is the Latin text printed by Gelasius Dobner in Monumenta historica Boemiae, vol. 2 (Prague, 1768), plus a second scan of the same edition. A third volume (Fontes rerum Bohemicarum) contributes only unrelated Bohemian chronicle pages.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
The Mongols and their empires: the Great Khan at Khanbaliq (the Yuan emperor of China), Ozbeg Khan of the Golden Horde, whom he calls the first emperor of the Tartars he reached, and the Chagatai 'middle empire' at Almaliq. He also uses 'Tartars' as a religious group next to Jews and Saracens.
Why read it
One of very few Western eyewitnesses to the Yuan court in its last decades. The Tartar rulers appear as masters of great cities, generous hosts and patrons of Christian churches with bells beside the imperial palace.
Watch out
The travel notes are digressions in a chronicle full of biblical legend, and he reports miracles and prophecies as fact. The Latin OCR prints long s as f, and the 1768 editor's footnotes contain real howlers (Almaliq and Sarai placed in Japan, Hangzhou confused with Cambay in India).

What it says: 13 passages

CitiesBeijing1342 to 1345

Khanbaliq, with a cathedral beside the palace

We came to Khanbaliq, the chief seat of the Empire of the East; of its incredible size, its people and the order of its soldiers, let nothing be said. … The Friars Minor also have in Khanbaliq a cathedral church right next to the palace, and a solemn archbishopric, and several other churches in the city, with bells, and they all live very honourably at the Emperor's expense.

Working translation

Original wording

pervenimus in Cambalec, ubi est summa Sedes Imperii Orientis, de cujus magnitudine incredibili, & populo, ordine militum sileatur. … Habent etiam fratres Minores in Cambalec Ecclesiam Cathedralem immediate juxta pallacium, & solempnem Archiepiscopatum, & alias Ecclesias plures in civitate, & campanas, & omnes vivunt de mensa Imperatoris honorifice valde.

Editorial context

Marignolli refuses even to try to describe the size, population and military order of the Khan's capital, the seat of the 'Empire of the East'. What he does describe is the Christian presence: a Franciscan cathedral right next to the imperial palace, an archbishopric, several other churches with bells, all supported from the Emperor's table. The city is the Yuan capital (Dadu) on the site of modern Beijing.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

CitiesAlmaliq1339 to 1340

From Ozbeg's horde to a new church at Almaliq

From there we came to the first emperor of the Tartars, Ozbeg, and presented letters, cloth, a warhorse, a 'cytiaca' (the editor guesses a strong liquor) and the Pope's gifts; and after the winter, well fed, clothed and magnificently rewarded, we travelled with his horses and at his expense to Almaliq of the middle empire, where we built a church, bought a plot of ground, made wells (or fonts), sang masses and baptised many, preaching freely and in public

Working translation

Original wording

Inde ad primum Tartarorum Imperatorem Usbec pervenimus, & obtulimus literas, pannos, dextrarium, cytiacam, & dona Pape, & post hiemem bene pasti, vestiti, & remunerati magnifice, & cum ejus equis & expensis pervenimus in Armalec Imperii medii, ubi fecimus Ecclesiam, emimus aream, fecimus fontes, cantavimus missas, baptisavimus plures, libere, & publice predicantes

Editorial context

The legates wintered with Ozbeg, khan of the Golden Horde, who fed, clothed and rewarded them and sent them on with his own horses to Almaliq, a city of the Chagatai khanate in the Ili valley (near modern Huocheng, Xinjiang). There, only a year after a bishop and six friars had been killed, they bought a plot, built a church and preached openly. The Tartar khans appear as hosts who moved envoys across their realms at their own cost.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

BuildingsHangzhouabout 1346

Hangzhou's temples and ten thousand stone bridges

And among them is the most famous city called Campsay, more marvellous, more beautiful, richer and larger, with a greater population, more riches and delights, more buildings and above all temples of idols … than any city that exists in the world, or perhaps ever existed. There, writers write, are ten thousand fine bridges of stone with sculptures and images of armed princes; it is incredible to one who has not seen it, and yet perhaps they do not lie.

Working translation

Original wording

Et inter illas est civitas famosissima nomine Campsay mirabilior, pulchrior, dicior, & maior cum maiori populo, & pluribus diviciis, & deliciis, edificiis, & maxime ydolorum templis … quam aliqua civitas, que sit in mundo, vel forte fuerit unquam. Ubi scribunt scribentes esse decem millia poncium nobilium de lapide cum sculpturis & ymaginibus principum armatorum, incredibile est non videnti, & tamen forte non menciuntur.

Editorial context

The Khan sent the legates home through Manzi, southern China, with its 'thirty thousand great cities'. Campsay (Hangzhou), the old Song capital, is to Marignolli the finest city in the world, full of buildings and temples, with thousands of monks living together. The ten thousand carved stone bridges he takes from earlier writers and cannot quite believe. These were Chinese buildings in a Chinese city under Mongol rule; he does not credit any of them to the Tartars.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

Keep in mind

The bridge count is taken from earlier writers, as he says. Dobner's footnote wrongly identifies Campsay with Cambay in Gujarat.

Daily lifeBeijing1342

A friar with a cross before the Great Khan

The great Khan, seeing the warhorses, the Pope's gifts and the sealed letters … rejoiced with great joy … And I, solemnly vested, with a most beautiful cross carried before me with candles and incense, singing 'I believe in one God', we went in before the Khan as he sat in his glorious palace; and when the singing was over I gave him a full blessing, which he received humbly.

Working translation

Original wording

Maximus autem Kaam visis dextrariis, & donis Pape, & literis bullatis … gavisus est gaudio magno … Ego autem solempniter indutus cum Cruce pulcherrima, que me precedebat cum luminaribus & incenso cantando: Credo in unum Deum; intravimus coram illo Kaam in glorioso pallacio residente, & cantu finito, largam dedi recipienti humiliter benediccionem.

Editorial context

The Pope's gifts included big European warhorses, and the Khan was delighted with them. Marignolli entered the palace in full vestments behind a cross, with candles and incense, singing the Creed, and blessed the Khan. He adds that the court then lodged the 32 members of the mission and paid for everything, food, drink and even paper for lanterns, for three or four years.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)
See the original page (p. 87)

Keep in mind

Marignolli's own account of his success; 'humbly' is his view of how the Khan received the blessing.

Daily lifeBeijing1340s

Thirty thousand Christian Alans at the Khan's court

The highest princes of the whole empire, more than thirty thousand, who are called Alans … are Christians, in fact or in name, and they call themselves the Pope's slaves, ready to die for the Franks; for so they call us, not from France but from 'Franquia'.

Working translation

Original wording

Summi eciam principes … Imperii tocius, plus quam triginta millia, qui vocantur Alani, … sunt Christiani, re vel nomine, & dicunt se Sclavos Pape, parati mori pro franquis, sic enim vocant nos, non a francia, sed a franquia.

Editorial context

Among the Khan's great men, Marignolli counts more than thirty thousand Alans, a people from the Caucasus whom the Mongols had taken into their service, who were Christians 'in fact or in name' and called themselves the Pope's slaves. He also explains that the peoples of the East called all Western Europeans 'Franks'. The passage shows how mixed in religion and origin the Tartar empire's elite was.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

Keep in mind

The number is Marignolli's estimate, and he is keen to show the East as friendly to the Pope.

Daily lifeHamiabout 1341

Baptisms at Kamul, on a promise of no church taxes

where at first the converts would not be baptised until we swore that we would extract nothing worldly from them after baptism, but would rather provide for the needy from our own; which we did, and so a multitude of the city, of both sexes, received baptism most willingly.

Working translation

Original wording

ubi primo conversi nolebant baptizari, donec juravimus, nihil temporale post baptismum extorquere ab eis, ymo de nostro indigentibus providere, quod & fecimus, sic multitudo civitatis utriusque sexus baptismum libentissime receperunt.

Editorial context

In an aside on church tithes, Marignolli recalls that at Kamul (Hami), where there were 'many Tartars and other nations', converts refused baptism until the friars swore they would never demand anything worldly from them afterwards. Once the friars promised, and even helped the poor from their own funds, crowds of men and women were baptised. He uses the story to argue against forcing tithes on new converts.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

Daily life14th century

To Tartars, Latin Christians looked like idol-worshippers

Yet Jews, Tartars and Saracens judge us the worst of idolaters, and not only the heathen but even some Christians. For although those Christians venerate paintings, they abhor masks, faces and horrible sculptures such as there are in our churches, as can be seen at the tomb of Saint Adalbert in Prague.

Working translation

Original wording

Judei tamen, Tartari, & Sarraceni iudicant nos pessimos ydololatras, & non solum gentiles, sed eciam Christiani quidam. Nam licet illi Christiani venerentur picturas, abhominantur tamen larvas, facies, & horrendas sculpturas, sicut sunt in Ecclesiis: patet in sepulcro sancti Adalberti in Praga.

Editorial context

In a chapter on how idols began, Marignolli admits an uncomfortable fact he learned abroad: Jews, Tartars and Saracens, and even some Eastern Christians, think Western Christians are the worst idolaters. Eastern Christians honour painted images but loathe the carved faces and frightening sculptures found in Latin churches, such as those on Saint Adalbert's tomb in Prague.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

Daily life14th century

Robes 'in the Tartar fashion'

she devised for herself clothes made in the Tartar fashion, folded over across the chest so that her breasts would not show, with long sleeves so that a woman's hands would not be seen, long robes to cover the feet, and trousers for when she mounted a horse; and she ordered in her son's name that everyone should wear such clothing

Working translation

Original wording

ipsa invenit sibi vestes thartarico modo formatas, ante pectus replicatas, ne mamille apparerent, longas manicas, ne mulieris manus viderentur, longas vestes, ad cooperiendum pedes, bracas, quando equum ascendebat, & precepit nomine filii, quod omnes tali veste uterentur

Editorial context

Retelling the old legend of Queen Semiramis, who disguised herself as her son, Marignolli explains her disguise with clothing he had seen on his travels: robes 'in the Tartar fashion', wrapped over across the chest, with long sleeves, long skirts covering the feet and trousers for riding. The legend is classical; the comparison to Tartar dress is his own.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

About Tartars (Mongols), as a model for a dress style

Keep in mind

A legend about ancient Assyria; only the description of the 'Tartar fashion' reflects what he saw.

The nameBeijing1338

Lord of half the eastern world

I was sent with apostolic letters and gifts as envoy and legate to the Khan, supreme emperor of all the Tartars, who holds lordship over almost half of the eastern world, whose power and wealth in cities, lands, languages, riches and rule over countless peoples is beyond all telling.

Working translation

Original wording

missus fui cum Apostolicis literis & donis nuncius & legatus ad Kaam, summum omnium Thartarorum Imperatorem, qui tenet quasi dominium medietatis orbis orientalis, cujus potestas & copia civitatum, terrarum, linguarum, diviciarum, & regiminis infinitorum quodammodo populorum excedit omnem narracionem.

Editorial context

Marignolli opens his travel memories by describing the ruler he was sent to: the Great Khan at Khanbaliq, 'supreme emperor of all the Tartars'. For this Florentine friar the Tartar ruler was not a tent chief but the master of countless cities, languages and peoples. The Khan was Toghon Temur, last Yuan emperor of China.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

The nameCentral Asiaearly 13th century

Chinggis Khan hires the Alans to punish the world

from whom [Elam] the noble race of the Alans in the East is said to have sprung; and it is today the greatest and noblest nation in the world, the handsomest and strongest men, with whose help the Tartars won the Empire of the East, and without whom they never won a glorious victory. For Chinggis Khan, first king of the Tartars, hired seventy-two of their princes when he wished, at God's command, to chastise the world.

Working translation

Original wording

a quo nobilis generacio Alanorum in oriente dicitur exorta, & est hodie maior & nobilior nacio mundi, & homines pulcriores, & forciores, quorum auxilio Tartari optinuerunt Imperium orientis, & sine quibus nunquam habuerunt victoriam gloriosam. Conduxit enim Cingwis Caam primus Thartarorum Rex de eis LXXII. principes, quando voluit precepto Dei castigare mundum.

Editorial context

In his world history after Noah, Marignolli derives the Alans from Elam, son of Shem, calls them the noblest nation on earth, and says the Tartars won the Empire of the East only with their help. Chinggis Khan, 'first king of the Tartars', hired 72 of their princes when he set out, by God's command, to chastise the world. The Tartars are fitted into Bible history as God's scourge.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)
See the original page (p. 110)

About Tartars (Mongols) under Chinggis Khan, and the Alans

Keep in mind

Biblical genealogy plus praise of the Alans, who were in fact conquered by the Mongols in the 1220s and 1230s rather than hired by Chinggis.

Strange talesGobi Desertabout 1341

The Tartars crossed the 'impassable' torrid zone

we came to the Cyollos Kagon, that is, to mountains of sand made by the winds, beyond which, before the Tartars, nobody thought there was habitable land, nor was it believed that any land lay beyond. But the Tartars, by God's will and with marvellous skill, crossed them and came into a vast plain, the place called the Torrid Zone, held by the philosophers to be impassable

Working translation

Original wording

pervenimus ad Cyollos Kagon, id est, ad montes arene, quos faciunt venti, ultra quos ante Tartaros nullus putavit terram habitabilem, nec putabatur ultra aliquam terram esse. Tartari autem voluntate Dei mirabili industria transierunt, & fuerunt in campo maximo, ubi dicitur Torrida Zona, & inpertransibilis a Philosophis

Editorial context

East of Almaliq the legates reached wind-built 'mountains of sand' beyond which, Marignolli says, nobody before the Tartars believed there was habitable land. The Tartars crossed them into a vast plain that philosophers called the Torrid Zone and thought impassable; on the next page he adds that he crossed it twice himself. He is mixing a real desert crossing with the old scholarly theory of uninhabitable climate zones.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

Strange talesAlmaliq1330s

A martyr's prophecy against the Tartars of Sarai

Brother Pascal the Spaniard, who was a prophet, saw heaven opened and foretold martyrdom for himself and his companions, and that the Tartars of Sarai would be destroyed by a flood, that Almaliq would perish because of their martyrdom, and that that emperor would be killed on the third day after their martyrdom

Working translation

Original wording

frater Paschalis Hyspanus, qui fuit Propheta, & vidit celum apertum, & predixit sibi, & Sociis suis martirium, & Thartaros de Saray destruendos diluvio, Armalec perituram, propter ipsorum martirium, Imperatorem illum tercio die occidendum post ipsorum martirium

Editorial context

Marignolli names the Franciscans killed at Almaliq the year before he arrived and credits one of them, Pascal the Spaniard, with visions and prophecies: that the Tartars of Sarai on the Volga would be destroyed by a flood, that Almaliq would perish, and that the ruler would be killed three days after the martyrdom. He presents the dead friars as holy martyrs 'shining with miracles'.

Giovanni de' Marignolli (Latin text ed. Gelasius Dobner, 1768)

Strange talesAlmaliq1768

An editor moves Almaliq and Sarai to Japan

And since the Martyrology of Saint Francis says they suffered in Japan, Almaliq was most probably one of the islands of Japan, just as Sarai, named just above, is most probably the present-day island of Sayrock Xymo, one of the three chief islands of Japan in the eastern Ocean.

Working translation

Original wording

Et cum Martyrologium S. Francisci Seraphici dicat eos passos in Japonia, verosimillime Armalec una ex insulis fuit Japonie, quemadmodum mox supra nominata Saray verosimillime est hodierna insula Sayrock Xymo una inter tres precipuas Japonie insulas in Oceano orientali.

Editorial context

Faced with unfamiliar names, the Prague editor Gelasius Dobner trusted a Franciscan martyrology that placed the martyrs 'in Japan' and concluded that Almaliq and Sarai were Japanese islands, Sarai being 'Sayrock Xymo' (probably Kyushu, which Jesuit writers called 'Saicoku' or 'Ximo'). In fact Almaliq lay in the Ili valley of Central Asia and Sarai on the lower Volga. By the eighteenth century the geography of the medieval Tartar empires had become hazy even for scholars.

Gelasius Dobner, editor (footnote, 1768)
