Fyodor Baikov's embassy from Tobolsk to Beijing

The official route diary of the first Russian envoy sent to the Qing court, travelling up the Irtysh through Oirat (Kalmyk) and Mongol lands to Beijing, 1654 to 1657.

Catalogue title Official embassy report (stateinyi spisok) of the Baikov mission to China, 1654-1657

Статейный список посольства Ф. И. Байкова в Китай (1654-1657)

Fyodor Baikov, Russian envoy

Fyodor Baikov led Tsar Alexei's embassy from Tobolsk to China in 1654 to 1657. His stateinyi spisok, the formal report every Muscovite envoy filed, is a day-by-day itinerary: river mouths, forts, where he waited for carts, what he was fed, and short notes on the towns, fields and buildings he passed. He wintered among the farming Bukharans of the Oirat prince Ablai taisha, crossed the Mongol steppe, and spent half a year shut in a Beijing compound after refusing Qing court protocol. This corpus copy is the 1788 printing in Novikov's Drevniaia rossiiskaia vivliofika, part 4, from a manuscript copy.

How they knew
Eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Baikov uses 'Tatars' only for the tribute-paying and service Tatars of the Tobolsk and Tara districts on the Irtysh. Kalmyks (Oirats), Mongols and Bukharans are named separately, and he calls the Manchu Qing emperor 'of Mongol race'.
Why read it
A sober, practical eyewitness who notes exactly what buildings were made of and who built them, including an Oirat prince's new town raised by craftsmen sent from China.
Watch out
The 1788 text follows a later copy (the 1966 critical edition uses the archival original), and the OCR of this old typeface is badly garbled; quotes here have been normalized letter by letter.

CitiesHohhotJanuary 1656

Kokotan (Hohhot): earth walls, brick towers, glazed temple roofs

The first Chinese-ruled town Baikov reached was Kokotan, today's Hohhot in Inner Mongolia. He says its people spoke Mongol and its governors were sent from Beijing. The wall was of earth with towers of burnt brick and iron-bound oak gates, but no cannon; inside and outside were many brick temples with green-glazed tile roofs, and a big market of stone shops.

and the town of Kokotan is of earth, with towers of brick, burnt brick; the gate towers are large, with two gates in each tower; … the gates are of oak, bound with iron; … there are no firearms, cannon or muskets; and there are many idol-temples in the town and outside it: the temples are of brick, their tops made as in Russia, and the roofs of glazed tile.

BuildingsUpper Irtysh, near Ust-Kamenogorsk (Oskemen), Kazakhstan1655

An Oirat prince builds a town with craftsmen from China

Travelling with the camp of the Oirat prince Ablai taisha, Baikov found him building a town on the Beshka stream between stone hills. Baikov could not tell whether it would be of stone or wood, but saw lots of timber cut, and was told the master builders had been sent from China. It is a precise, first-hand note of a steppe ruler commissioning a fixed town from foreign craftsmen.

And on that stream, the Beshka, Ablai taisha is building a town; whether of stone or of wood is not known for certain, but much timber has been felled, thin pine … and he is putting that town up between stony hills; and the master builders for it were sent from the Chinese Tsardom.

BuildingsUpper Irtysh, eastern Kazakhstan1654

Brick temples and clay houses on the Irtysh

On the way up the Irtysh Baikov noted a Kalmyk 'mosque' (a Buddhist temple) of burnt brick, then a Kalmyk lama with two large 'Burkhan' halls, also of burnt brick, and clay houses to live in. The fields around grew wheat and millet, but the ploughing was done by Bukharans, the settled Central Asian farmers who worked for the Oirats.

and at Kaban-Gusan a Kalmyk mosque has been built: of burnt brick … And a Kalmyk lama lives here beside the Irtysh on the left bank. Two great Burkhan (Buddha) halls have been built for that lama, of burnt brick; and the houses they live in are of clay; and much wheat and millet grows for that lama, and Bukharans do the ploughing.

The nameBeijing1656

A Beijing emperor 'of Mongol race'

Baikov calls Beijing by its old Mongol name Kanbalyk (Khanbaliq) and reports that the reigning emperor, the 'Bogda', was of Mongol race, and that the last Chinese emperor hanged himself when the 'Mongols' took over China. He means the Manchus, who had taken Beijing in 1644. It is the Russian counterpart of the Western habit of calling the Manchus 'Tartars'.

And in the Chinese Tsardom, in Kanbalyk, the tsar is the Bogda, of Mongol race; and before him there was a tsar of Chinese race, Daba; and when the Mongols overcame the Chinese Tsardom, that Tsar Daba hanged himself
