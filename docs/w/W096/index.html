Mir Izzet Ullah's journey from Kashmir to Bukhara

An Indian Muslim traveller's route journal through Ladakh, Yarkand, Kashgar, Kokand, Samarkand and Bukhara in 1812 to 1813.

Catalogue title Travels beyond the Himalaya

Mir Izzet Ullah

Mir Izzet Ullah, a Persian-writing agent of the English traveller William Moorcroft, was sent ahead in 1812 to scout the road from Kashmir over the Karakoram to Chinese Turkestan and on to Bukhara. He kept a Persian journal of stages, towns, taxes, horses and peoples. H. H. Wilson translated part of it for a Calcutta magazine in 1825 and reprinted it, with notes, in the Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society in 1843; this corpus copy is that reprint. It includes a route from Peking to Kashgar copied from a local man's journal. He meets Kalmyks, Kirghiz, Kazakhs, Uzbeks and Nogais, and describes Timur's tomb at Samarkand.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Izzet Ullah names peoples precisely (Kalmaks, Kirghiz, Kazzaks, Kapchaks, Uzbeks, Nogais) and the English word 'Tartar' appears only a few times, for nomad camps on the road and in the translator's notes. The Kalmaks are the Oirat Mongols (Dzungars) who once ruled Yarkand and raided Ladakh.
Why read it
A non-European eyewitness of 1813 shows Inner Asia as a patchwork of named peoples, Chinese-run oasis towns and ruined Timurid monuments, with no one called simply 'the Tartars'.
Watch out
This is an English translation by H. H. Wilson of a Persian journal, with Wilson's notes and some omitted sections. Parts, such as the Peking route, are copied from another man's journal, and some wonders (the rain stone) are hearsay, which the traveller admits.

CitiesSamarkand1813

Samarkand's colleges, once haunted by lions and wolves

Timur's old capital had shrunk. Izzet Ullah found it defended only by a mud wall and an earth citadel, Ulugh Beg's observatory reduced to a mound of rubbish, and the great 'Madressa Khanum' (the Bibi Khanum complex) in ruins. He says that before Shah Murad repopulated it, the city had been so run down that lions and wolves haunted the colleges.

Samarkand is defended by a mud wall, as well as a citadel of earth… There are many large colleges in the city ; the largest is the Madressa Khanum, but it is now in ruins… Before the reign of Shah Murad Be, Samarkand was in an entirely dilapidated condition, and the colleges were haunted by lions and wolves.

CitiesLeh1680s (recalled in 1812)

Kalmyks in Leh, and the mosque that came of it

The only mosque in Leh, Izzet Ullah was told, was founded by Ibrahim Khan, a Mughal noble sent to help the king of Ladakh when the Kalmaks had seized the city. The king converted to Islam and swore vassalage, then soon went back to his own faith. This is a memory of the Tibet-Ladakh-Mughal war of 1679 to 1684. On the same page he describes Leh's thousand houses of stone or unburnt brick, three or four storeys high.

It was founded by Ibrahim Khan, one of the nobles of the Mogul Court, at a time when the Kalmaks … had got possession of the city, and the Raja of Tibet had recourse to the Sultan of Hindustan for succour. Ibrahim Khan, who was sent to his assistance, defeated the Kalmaks, and restored the Raja;

CitiesYarkand1640s to 1750s (recalled in 1813)

Yarkand: from Mughal khans to Kalmyks to China

Izzet Ullah sketches Yarkand's history: once ruled by Moghul khans, it fell to the Kalmaks, whose chief lived at Ili. Then an epidemic killed the Kalmak ruler, his family and vast numbers of his people, rival Khoja factions fought, and China conquered the country about sixty years before his visit. This matches the collapse of the Dzungar state in the 1750s.

fell into the hands of the Kalmaks. The chief of the Kalmaks, entitled the Tura, resided at Ila, and a deputy on his part was sent to Yarkand. After some time an epidemic occurred amongst the Kalmaks, which carried off the Tura, his children, his chief men, and a vast number of people.

CitiesKazan1813

Kazan, 'capital of the Khan of the Dasht Kipchaks'

Describing the lands north of Ferghana, Izzet Ullah says the Kazakhs and Karakalpaks there depend on Russia, and that the capital of the Khan of the Dasht-i Kipchak, 'Bulghar, now known by the name of Kazan', lies in Russian territory. He runs together two different Volga cities (old Bulghar and Kazan), but the line shows how the Tatar khanates were remembered in Central Asia.

To the north of the mountains of Andejan the country is a barren plain ; to the north of which are the tribes of Kazzaks and Kara Kalpaks, dependant on Russia. The capital of the Khan of the Dast Kapchaks, Bulghar, now known by the name of Kazan, is in the Russian territory.

Heard itMir Izzet Ullah (translated by H. H. Wilson)

CitiesIli (Yining)about 1813

Ili: the old Kalmyk capital becomes a Chinese garrison

In the route copied from a Kashgar man's journal, the note on Ili says the old city by the river, once the Kalmaks' capital, is deserted, and that the name now covers two towns: Gulja, mostly Muslim, and Kura, the Chinese garrison and seat of the governor-general. It also mentions the grave of Tughluk Timur, the first Chaghatai khan to become Muslim, and a burning stone (coal) found nearby.

Ila is properly the name of the river on the borders of which stood the city, thence so called, and which is now deserted. There is the grave of Tughluk Timor, the first of the Chaghatai Sultans who adopted Mohammedanism.

Heard itMir Izzet Ullah, from Mullah Nazr of Kashgar's route notes (translated by H. H. Wilson)

BuildingsSamarkand1813

Timur's tomb, stripped of its gold

Izzet Ullah visited the Gur-i Amir, the tomb of Timur (Tamerlane), whose court history calls his army 'the Tartars'. He saw the great dome, the famous dark green stone slab and the inscribed plate, and was told the gilding had been stripped off by the Bukharan ruler Shah Murad. The poor attendants asked him whether any descendants of Timur still lived in India who might help them.

the seat of the sepulchre of Amir Timur ; a lofty building with an immense cupola : within it is a remarkable slab of a brilliant green stone, and above it is a plate with an inscription, with the name of Timur engraved on it. Formerly the dome was gilt, but Shah Murad Be stripped off the gilding, and took it away ;

BuildingsKarakoram route to Yarkand1812

Ruined buildings of the Kirghiz, 'a Mongol tribe speaking a Turkish dialect'

On the road from Ladakh to Yarkand, Izzet Ullah passed a place called Kirghiz Jangal (Kirghiz thicket) with the ruins of Kirghiz buildings. He explains that the Kirghiz had lived there until they were expelled for plundering caravans, before China took the region. Elsewhere on his route the Kirghiz live in felt tents, so the note that these nomads also left buildings behind is worth noticing.

On the road half way is a place called Kirghiz thicket, where are the remains of some of their ruined edifices, that tribe having been resident here before the country was subjected to China: being in the habit of plundering the Kafilas they were expelled. The Kirghizes are a Mongol tribe, speaking a Turkish dialect.

Saw itMir Izzet Ullah (translated by H. H. Wilson)

Felt tents pitched beside a domed tomb

Crossing the mountains west of Kashgar in deep winter, Izzet Ullah notes Kirghiz felt tents lining the road (p. 321). At the stage called Mizar Gumbaz he found a domed building with Kirghiz tombs and the living Kirghiz camped in tents beside it: permanent structures for the dead, portable ones for the living.

Daily lifeFerghana Valley1813

Turks and Kipchaks versus the Kirghiz

Near Andijan, Izzet Ullah passes the spring and summer pastures of the Turks and Kipchaks, some 10,000 to 12,000 families, whom he admires as prosperous, well dressed and soldierly. He is scornful of the poorer Kirghiz. A page earlier he notes 'many Tartar stations' where herds of horses graze, and just after this he meets Kalmaks who 'are Musalmans', a surprise since most Kalmyks were Buddhist.

Daily lifeTian Shan, west of Kashgar1813

Kirghiz snow-treaders keep the pass open

In winter the snow in the passes west of Kashgar lay a man's height deep or more. Izzet Ullah describes Kirghiz posted along the road whose job was to trample the snow hard so caravans could cross. Even so, several horses died, including his own.

The Kirghizes tread the snow down so as to make it hard, and then the caravans pass; a certain number of Kirghizes are appointed to this duty,

Strange talesYarkand1812 to 1813

The rain stone of Yarkand

Among the curiosities of Yarkand, Izzet Ullah lists the 'Yedeh', a stone taken from the head of a cow or horse that can bring rain or snow. He admits he never saw it work, but says many people swore to it. Rain-stone magic (yada) was an old practice of Turkic and Mongol peoples. His closing line is a neat mix of belief and doubt, and Wilson's footnote praises his honesty.

The individuals who employ the stone are numerous; they are called Yedejis. The stone is to be smeared with the blood of some animal, and then thrown into water: a charm is read at the same time, upon which a strong wind springs up, and then rain and snow ensue… The truth of the story is known to God alone.

Heard itMir Izzet Ullah (translated by H. H. Wilson)

About Turkic Muslims of Yarkand (the 'Yedejis', rain-makers)
