Staunton's official account of the Macartney embassy to China and Tartary

The British embassy's official history of its 1792 to 1794 mission, including the journey through the Great Wall to the Qing emperor's summer court at Chengde.

Full title An Authentic Account of an Embassy … to the Emperor of China

George Leonard Staunton

Read the 13 passagesOpen the original book

Sir George Leonard Staunton (1737-1801) was secretary and second-in-command of Lord Macartney's embassy to the Qianlong emperor. His official account (1797) was compiled from his own experience and from the journals of Macartney, Captain Parish, Dr Gillan and others, whom he often quotes. This corpus copy is Volume II, which covers Beijing, the ride north through the Great Wall into 'Chinese Tartary', the audience in a tent at Zhe-hol (Chengde), the lama temples there, and the return south. 'Tartar' here almost always means the ruling Manchus and their Mongol allies.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Manchus, the conquering 'Tartar dynasty' of the Qing, and their bannermen; also the Mongol princes tributary to the emperor, and 'Tartary' as the lands beyond the Great Wall. Once, the 'Mongol Tartars' of the thirteenth century.
Why read it
A close, dated eyewitness picture of Manchu 'Tartar' power at its height: who ruled China, how Tartars and Chinese differed, and what the court at Chengde looked like.
Watch out
Staunton blends his own observations with other members' journals and with what mandarins told him, and he writes as a British official judging a foreign court. Only Volume II is in this corpus.

CitiesBeijing1793

Why part of Beijing was called the Tartar city

Editorial contextStaunton describes Beijing as two walled cities side by side. The northern and larger one, with the imperial palace at its centre, was called the Tartar city; he explains the name by its being laid out anew under the first Tartar (Mongol) dynasty in the thirteenth century. The page after describes the 'Chinese city' adjoining its southern wall, half empty and partly under cultivation.

The principal part of it is called the Tartar city, from the circumstance of having been laid out anew in the thirteenth century in the time of the first Tartar dynasty. It is in the form of a parallelogram … in the centre of which is the imperial palace, occupying within the yellow wall at least one square mile.

George Leonard Staunton

Keep in mind

The origin of the name is Staunton's explanation; other European writers tied 'Tartar city' to the Manchu banner garrison that lived there under the Qing.

CitiesChengde1793

Hovels and palaces at the emperor's summer town

Editorial contextZhe-hol (Jehol, modern Chengde), beyond the Great Wall, was the Qing emperors' summer capital in Tartary. Staunton contrasts the poor, crooked town with the grandeur of the imperial palaces, gardens and temples just beyond it. He adds that the principal buildings 'in this part of Tartary' differed little from those of China.

The town of Zhe-hol, excepting the houses of mandarines, consisted of miserable hovels full of people. The streets also were crooked, unpaved, and dusty. Immediately beyond it, the imperial garden, the palaces, the temples displayed much grandeur; and magnificence and wretchedness knew no medium.

George Leonard Staunton

BuildingsChengde1793

An emperor who preferred a tent to a palace

Editorial contextThe Qianlong emperor received the British envoy not in a hall of his summer palace but in a great tent in the garden, with small round tents pitched in front for the tributary princes. Staunton explains the choice: the Manchu rulers had adopted Chinese customs but kept a fondness for their old movable dwellings when they were on 'Tartar ground'.

The Tartar dynasty, in conforming in most instances, to the customs of a much more numerous and more civilized, tho vanquished nation, retained still a predilection for its own ancient manners, in which occasionally, and upon Tartar ground, it took a pleasure in indulging. The moveable dwelling of a tent was, more than a permanent palace of stone and timber, the favourite residence of a Tartar sovereign.

George Leonard Staunton

BuildingsChengde1793

A Tibetan temple in Tartary that looked European

Editorial contextNear the summer palace the party visited the 'Poo-ta-la', the great lama temple the emperor had built (today's Putuo Zongcheng temple, modelled on the Potala of Lhasa). Staunton found it unlike any Chinese building and oddly like a European front, eleven stories of windows high, with a golden chapel inside whose roof plates were said to be solid gold. He credits the building to the emperor himself.

the outside very much resembling the front of an European edifice. It was of great height, containing eleven rows of windows, implying as many stories, or ranges of apartments, one above another. … The square, or principal building of Poo-ta-la, includes a quadrangle, in the centre of which is the golden chapel, so termed from the materials in which it, apparently at least, abounds.

George Leonard Staunton
See the original page (p. 258)

About Manchus and Mongols (Qing court); Tibetan Buddhist lamas

Keep in mind

The 'European' look is Staunton's comparison; the page before says the temples round Zhe-hol 'had been erected by the Emperor'. The gold was 'said to be' solid.

BuildingsTongzhou, Beijing1793

A solid brick tower thought to guard against Tartars

Editorial contextAt Tong-choo-foo, the river port of Beijing, the embassy examined an old eleven-storey brick pagoda with no door or window in its solid lower stories. Nobody knew what it had been for; the guess Staunton reports is that it was an ancient watch tower against sudden Tartar raids.

It is built of brick, and in its exterior form resembles what are called in Europe Chinese pagodas … It is thought most probable that this building was erected prior to the existence of Tong-choo-foo, and perhaps of the great Chinese wall, and was intended for a watch tower, to guard against the sudden approach of the Tartar enemy.

George Leonard Staunton
See the original page (p. 99)

About Chinese; 'the Tartar enemy'

Keep in mind

Staunton admits the tower's 'original destination is not known with certainty'; the watch-tower idea is a conjecture, and many solid brick pagodas in north China were Buddhist relic towers.

Daily lifeGubeikou, Great Wall1793

'No Chinese may punish me on the Tartar side of the wall'

Editorial contextAlthough Chinese and Tartars had one master, Staunton found the Great Wall still treated as the boundary between two peoples. Just past it, a Tartar attendant facing punishment by Chinese mandarins loudly refused, claiming they had no authority over him on the Tartar side. On the next page a Chinese mandarin dares not sit before a Tartar officer of equal rank.

The line it has traced, continues still to be considered as a demarkation between the respective nations of Chinese and Tartars. … one of the Tartar attendants was about to be punished for some misdemeanour, by order of the Chinese mandarines; but he resisted with much vehemence, loudly exclaiming, that no Chinese had a right of exercising authority over him on the Tartar side of the great wall.

George Leonard Staunton

Daily lifeBeijing1793

Tartar women with unbound feet

Editorial contextCrossing Beijing, the embassy noticed that most women in the watching crowds were said to be of Tartar race. Unlike Chinese women their feet were not bound, and they wore broad-toed shoes with thick soles. On the next page Staunton adds that some Tartar ladies rode on horseback astride, like men.

Among the spectators of the novel sight, some women were observed. The greatest number were said to be natives of Tartary, or of a Tartar race. Their feet were not cramped, like those of the Chinese; and their shoes with broad toes, and soles above an inch in thickness, were as clumsy as those of the original Chinese ladies were diminutive.

George Leonard Staunton

Daily lifeChina1793

Tartar soldiers paid double

Editorial contextFrom a Chinese officer's figures Staunton reports that much of the cavalry was Tartar, that Tartars held the posts of trust, and that a Tartar horseman drew seven ounces of silver and twenty measures of rice a month, against three and three-tenths ounces and fifteen measures for a Chinese horseman.

Of the troops, especially cavalry, a vast number are Tartars, who have a higher pay than their Chinese fellow soldiers. The principal officers of confidence in the army are Tartars also. … A Tartar horseman, seven similar ounces of silver, and twenty measures of rice for the same period.

George Leonard Staunton

Daily lifeMongolia1793

The Tartar princes and their fiefs

Editorial contextAfter the birthday festivities the tributary Tartar princes went home. Staunton describes them as chiefs of large clans who owed troops in wartime under the 'grand banners of Tartary', held hereditary fiefs that needed the emperor's investiture, and usually married women of the imperial family.

They were the chiefs of numerous clans dependent on them, and could bring large bodies of troops into the field. They were often called upon in time of war, and had their respective stations, rank, and duty, assigned to them under the grand banners of Tartary. Their lands, or fiefs, were properly hereditary by primogeniture

George Leonard Staunton

The nameBeijing1793

Born in Beijing, but still Tartars

Editorial contextStaunton explains that the Qianlong emperor, though four generations removed from the conquest and born in Beijing, was seen by everyone, and saw himself, as a Tartar. His ministers, generals, wives and servants were mostly of the same race, and every male of Tartar descent drew a stipend from birth. Here 'Tartar' plainly means the Manchu conquerors.

Tho the Emperor Chen-Lung, be the fourth in descent from him who successfully invaded China in the last age, and tho the last three succeeding generations were all born at Pekin, yet they are universally regarded by their subjects, and they regarded indeed themselves, as Tartars. Their principal ministers, their confidential servants, the chiefs of their armies, most of their wives, concubines, domestics, and eunuchs, are of that race.

George Leonard Staunton

The nameManchuria1793

A story that the Manchu emperors came from Kublai Khan

Editorial contextThe Tartar princes at court, Staunton says, revered the emperor as a descendant of Kublai Khan: Kublai's heirs, driven out of China in the fourteenth century, fled to the Manchus, intermarried, and produced the 'Bog-doi Khans' who conquered China. It shows how the Qing dynasty was presented to, or understood by, its Mongol allies.

They hold the Emperor in the greater veneration, as considering him descended from Kublai-Khan, the conqueror of China in the thirteenth century. His descendants being in the fourteenth century expelled from the throne of that empire, fled into the country of the Man-choos in Eastern Tartary; and from their intermarriages with the natives, sprung the Bog-doi Khans

George Leonard Staunton
See the original page (p. 268)

About Mongol tributary princes; Manchus

Keep in mind

A genealogical belief reported at second hand; the Qing ruling clan is usually traced to Jurchen (Manchu) chiefs, not to Kublai.

The nameChengde1793

Kalmyk chiefs who fled Russia for China

Editorial contextAt the emperor's birthday theatricals most of the guests who came to meet the British envoy were Tartars, since few Chinese were invited to Zhe-hol. Among them were two chiefs of 'Calmoucks' whose people had recently left Russian rule on the Caspian and migrated to Chinese Tartary, an event of 1771.

Most of them were Tartars ; few original Chinese being invited to Zhe-hol. There were likewise two Mussulmen, chiefs of some hordes of Calmoucks, who, not long since, on occasion of discontent or misunderstanding with the government of Russia, migrated in great numbers from the northern coast of the Caspian sea into Chinese Tartary, and put themselves under the Emperor's protection.

George Leonard Staunton
See the original page (p. 265)

About Manchus and Mongols at court; Kalmyks from the Volga

Keep in mind

The Kalmyks (Torghuts) who made the 1771 migration were Tibetan Buddhists, so calling their chiefs 'Mussulmen' looks like a confusion.

Strange talesBeijing13th century (story told in 1793)

Horses stabled in the emperors' palaces

Editorial contextOn the Grand Canal Staunton notes that the Chinese still looked on Tartars in general as barbarians. As proof they told a four-hundred-year-old story about the Mongol capture of Beijing: the conquerors lived in tents and put their horses in the imperial palaces.

they repeat the story, popular amongst them for four centuries, that when the Mongol Tartars seized first upon Pekin, they erected tents for their own accommodation, while they put their horses in the palaces of the Chinese emperors.

George Leonard Staunton
See the original page (p. 419)

About Mongols ('Mongol Tartars'), as remembered by the Chinese

Keep in mind

A popular Chinese tale, reported as such; not evidence of what happened in 1215.
