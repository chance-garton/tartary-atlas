Álvaro Semedo's Empire of China

A Portuguese Jesuit's description of late Ming China, written after two decades there, with the first Manchu ('Tartar') attacks seen from inside the empire.

Full title The Empire of China and the Evangelical Culture in it by the Jesuits (Spanish first edition of Semedo’s Relação, written in Portuguese)

Álvaro Semedo, Portuguese Jesuit; Manuel de Faria e Sousa, editor

Read the 12 passagesOpen the original book

Álvaro Semedo was a Portuguese Jesuit who reached China in 1613 and lived there, with breaks, until about 1637, at Nanjing, in Jiangxi and elsewhere. Back in Europe he wrote an account of the country and the mission in Portuguese; that manuscript is lost, and the text first appeared as this Spanish edition by Manuel de Faria e Sousa (Madrid, 1642), followed by Italian (1643) and English (1655) versions, all in the corpus. It covers the provinces, customs, government and army, then the Christian mission year by year. The Tartars appear as the old Mongol (Yuan) rulers of China and as the new enemy in Liaodong from 1618. This entry quotes the 1642 Spanish copy, plus one page of the 1655 English.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Two groups: the Mongol conquerors who ruled China from the thirteenth century until 1368, and the Manchus (Jurchen) of the north-east, whose war on the Ming began in Liaodong in 1618. Semedo calls both simply 'Tartaros'.
Why read it
It shows how a well informed resident of Ming China understood the Tartars just before the Manchu conquest: an old dynasty of foreign rulers, and a frightening new enemy beyond the Wall. It also preserves the text of a Chinese war memorial of 1618 in translation.
Watch out
Semedo saw little of the frontier himself; the war news comes from Chinese documents and Jesuit letters. The 1655 English edition adds Martino Martini's Bellum Tartaricum on the Manchu conquest, which is Martini's work (catalogued separately) and is not used here.

CitiesBeijingearly 1400s (written c. 1640)

Beijing made capital to face the Tartars

Editorial contextSemedo explains that Beijing, called Cambalu by the Muslims, became the court when Yongle ('Yunlo'), who lived in that region, seized the throne after Hongwu's death and moved the court from Nanjing, both to be among his own supporters and to stand up better to the neighbouring Tartars. The Mongol name Khanbaliq survives in 'Cambalud'.

it now has the sovereign privilege of enjoying the Court, in the city of the same name; its proper name is Xunthienfu; the Moors call it Cambalu … a grandson of his named Yunlo … [both to trust those he had always dealt with] and to be better able to face the neighbouring Tartars, took the Court out of Nanjing and planted it here.

Royal palaces and tombs burned, 1635

Editorial contextIn his year-by-year mission history Semedo notes that in 1635 the Tartars raided widely and set fire to royal palaces and the tombs of ancient princes, adding (as a moralist) a second fire to the one in which the princes' souls already burned. Mandarins were punished for negligence. He does not name the place.

The Tartars roamed about with their assaults, causing various destruction, and went so far as to send up in the smoke of a horrible fire the royal palaces and the tombs of the ancient princes who lay there

A hundred thousand guards for the emperor's ploughing

Editorial contextWhen the Chongzhen emperor went out to plough a furrow at the temple of agriculture as an example to his people, the fear of a Tartar 'flood' was so great that a hundred thousand men guarded him, even though the temple lay inside the city's second wall. BuildingsLiaodongc. 1637 to 1642

The famous Wall: 'more fame than effect'

Editorial contextEnding his tour of the provinces with Liaodong, famous for ginseng, Semedo notes that as the frontier with the Tartars it is badly wasted and partly held by them. Here, he says, runs the celebrated wall of three hundred leagues, which has more fame than effect: for all its size the enemy has reduced it to a miserable state. The Wall is Chinese, and in his view failing.

It [the root] is called ginseng. But being the frontier with the Tartars, [the province] is much destroyed, and even partly possessed by them. Here is where one sees that so celebrated wall, running for the space of three hundred leagues, with more fame than effect: for in spite of its greatness and capacity, the enemy has put it in a miserable state.

Daily lifeLiaodongAugust 1618

Captives sacrificed before the battle of 1618

Editorial contextSemedo translates a memorial sent to the Wanli emperor by the President of the Council of War in 1618. It reports that the Tartars had massed to 'take this world of China', broke through the walls, seized Chinese prisoners and sacrificed them the day before the battle, acclaiming their own king as king of Beijing. Semedo says he translated it at the time and later found it printed in Europe.

At this time, with great power and multitude, entering the walls, they seized some of our men in order to sacrifice them; and they sacrificed them the day before the battle, with great acclamations of their king, calling him already King of Peking.

En este tiempo con grã poder i multitud, entrando los muros, prendieron algunos de los nuestros para sacrificarlos; i sacrificaron el dia antes de la batalla con grãdes aclamaciones de su Rey, llamandole ya Rey de PeKim.

President of the Chinese Council of War, memorial of 1618, as translated by Álvaro Semedo (Spanish edition, Madrid 1642)

Daily lifeLiaodong1621

Portuguese cannon against the Tartars, 1621

Editorial contextIn 1621 the city of Macau sent the emperor three large cannon with gunners. One burst at a demonstration in Beijing, killing three or four men, but the guns were still sent to the Tartar frontier. The Tartars, not knowing the new weapon, came too close, were hit by a shower of iron fragments, and fled; afterwards they were more careful.

Nevertheless they were sent to the frontier of the Tartars, who, not knowing of the new weapon and coming nearer than was good for them, tasted a shower of pieces of iron, from which many fell, and all fled. Afterwards they became cautious.

Daily lifeChina1300s (written c. 1640)

Christians backed the Tartars and lost

Editorial contextSemedo explains why Jesuits found no trace of the Christians Marco Polo described. When the Tartar ruled China there were many Christians with fine churches, favoured by him. When Hongwu fought to recover the kingdom, the Muslims sided with the Chinese and kept their liberties and mosques, while the Christians sided with the Tartar and went down with him: some died, some converted, some hid.

When the Tartar ruled China there were many Christians in it, with quite sumptuous churches, who found themselves favoured by him, as appears from the accounts of the Venetian [Marco Polo]. … The Christians, taking the side of the Tartar, and he being lost in that action, necessarily were also lost in their standing

Original wording

Quando el Tartaro dominò la China, avia en ella muchos Christianos, con Iglesias bien suntuosas, que se hallavan favorecidos dèl, como parece de las Relaciones del Veneto. … Los Christianos tomando la voz del Tartaro, i quedando èl perdido en aquella accion, precisamente quedaron tambien perdidos en su estado

The nameLiaodong1368 to 1618

How the Tartar borderlands split into three kingdoms

Editorial contextSemedo's origin story for the new enemy: after expelling the Tartars, Hongwu made the Tartar borderlands tributary and divided them into states with titles. These grew until they split into three kingdoms, East, West and North. The western and northern ones broke away at once; the eastern one stayed loyal until the Chinese, for no reason, decided to crush it, and it turned on them. The eastern kingdom is the Jurchen (Manchu) state.

When Hum Vu, the founder of the family that today holds the Chinese sceptre, shook off from his empire the Tartar [who had ruled it for ninety years], … leaving the borders of Tartary as vassals, he reduced them to tributaries, dividing them into states with different titles and dignities. These afterwards grew so much that they separated into three kingdoms: one to the East, another to the West, and another to the North.

Working translation

Original wording

Quando el Hum Vu, Trõco de la Familia q̃ oy tiene el cetro Chino, sacudiò de su Imperio al Tartaro, … dexando avassallados los cõfines de la Tartaria, los reduxo a tributarios, dividiẽdolos en Estados de titulos i dignidades diferẽtes. Crecierõ ellos despues tãto, q̃ se separarõ en tres Reynos: uno al Oriente, otro al Ocaso, i al Norte otro.

The nameJiayuguan1605 to 1606

Cathay is China: Goes reaches the Wall

Editorial contextSemedo retells how the Jesuit brother Bento de Goes, travelling from India to find Cathay, learned from a caravan that Cathay and China were the same land under different names. He then passed Turfan and Hami to 'Quiaicion' (Jiayuguan), where one sees the wall dividing the Chinese from the Tartars, and on to Suzhou. This settled that 'Cathay' was simply northern China.

and the other, that China was the same as Cathay, there being no difference in the lands, but only in the names. … he passed through these towns: Puchan, Turfan, Aramuth, Camul, Quiaicion (from where one sees that much-repeated wall that divides the Chinese from the Tartars), Suzhou

The nameChina1206 to 1368

Ninety years of Tartar rule, 1206 to 1368

Editorial contextIn his outline of Chinese dynasties Semedo says that after two thousand years of regional lords, the Tartars began conquering China piece by piece in 1206 and ruled it for ninety years until 1368, when Hongwu ('Hum Vu') found them grown careless and hated, drove them out and even subjected their borderlands. The Tartars here are the Mongols of the Yuan dynasty.

The lords of particular states remained in China more than two thousand years, until in the year 1206 of our Redemption the Tartars (then with another power and government), beginning to conquer it piece by piece, came to rule it for the space of ninety years, which ended in 1368, when Hum Vu, catching them now slack in their care and inflamed with tyranny

Working translation

Original wording

Permanecieron en la China los señores de Estados particulares mas de dos mil años, hasta que el de 1206. de nuestra Redencion, los Tartaros (entonces con otro poder i govierno) empeçando a conquistarla por partes, la vinieron a dominar por espacio de noventa años, que fenecieron el de 1368. en que el Hum Vu cogiendolos ya floxos de cuidado, i de tirania encendidos

Álvaro Semedo, S.J. (Spanish edition by Manuel de Faria e Sousa, Madrid 1642)

Strange talesBeijing1618

Cannibal famine, mice and two suns: omens of the Tartar war

Editorial contextThe same 1618 memorial explains the defeat by heaven's anger and lists recent prodigies: no rain in the capital province, famine so bad in another province that people ate one another, a plague of mice in Nanjing, fires in the palace and two suns in the sky, one eclipsing the other. It shows how the Ming court read the Tartar war as a sign of cosmic disorder.

the Prodigies, which we have lately all seen, do demonstrate no lesse, In the Province of Pekim the last yeare it raigned not at all … in the Province of Xankim, there was so great a dearth and famine, that the people did eat one another: and into Nankim came an infinite multitude of Mice … we saw two Sunnes together, and the one did Ecclipse the other.

President of the Chinese Council of War, memorial of 1618, in Semedo's translation (English edition, London 1655)
See the original page (p. 102)

Strange talesChinawritten c. 1640 about the 1200s to 1300s

The wonder-working magicians of Tartar times

Editorial contextDiscussing Chinese divination, Semedo says some are said to keep familiar spirits, but he never saw any. In the time of the Tartar, he adds, there were many deeply sunk in such arts, and according to Marco Polo they worked marvels; today nothing of the kind is clearly known. He is sceptical and leans on Marco Polo for the Mongol period.

It is said that some have familiar spirits and consult them. Of these I saw none. Anciently, in the time of the Tartar, there were many, and very deeply sunk in this; and, according to Marco Polo, they worked marvels. Today I do not know that there is any such thing clearly.

Working translation

Original wording

Dizese, que algunos tienen Familiares, i q̃ los consultan. Destos no vi. Antiguamente en tiempo del Tartaro huvo muchos, i muy empantanados en esto: i, segun Marco Veneto, hazian maravillas. Oy no sè que aya cosa alguna destas con claridad.

Álvaro Semedo, S.J. (Spanish edition by Manuel de Faria e Sousa, Madrid 1642)

How we know

firsthand The author saw the places or people described.

firsthand observation (Semedo lived in China 1613-1637) with compilation of Jesuit records; Faria e Sousa edited the Portuguese manuscript into Spanish

Research notes
  • Spanish text is a translation-edition by Faria e Sousa; Portuguese original lost; the Italian 1643 Relatione differs (includes Tartar war material).
  • Portuguese Jesuit 'Cartas' on the Manchu conquest (e.g. Relação da guerra dos Tartaros) not located open this pass; see candidates.

Why it matters

Standard pre-conquest Jesuit description of Ming China with the Great Wall and the Tartar frontier on the eve of the Manchu conquest; the Italian 1643 edition added a report on the Tartar war.
