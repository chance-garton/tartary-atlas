Elphinstone's account of Kabul and its Tartar neighbours

A British envoy's survey of the Afghan kingdom, 1808 to 1809, with chapters on the Uzbeks, Aimaqs and Hazaras built from informants.

Catalogue title An Account of the Kingdom of Caubul and its Dependencies

Mountstuart Elphinstone

Read the 11 passagesOpen the original book

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1779-1859), an East India Company official, led a British embassy to Shah Shuja, King of Kabul, in 1808 to 1809. The mission met the king at Peshawar and never reached Kabul, so Elphinstone built this book from what he saw on the road, from reports by his officers, and from long interviews with Afghan, Uzbek and other travellers, some carried on afterwards at Delhi. Most of it describes the Afghans. A final part covers the Uzbeks of Balkh and Bukhara and the Aimaq and Hazara hill peoples, whom he describes as Tartar in features and descent. This corpus copy is the first edition, London 1815, in a Google scan with heavy OCR damage on some pages.

How they knew
Part eyewitness
Who “Tartar” means here
Mainly the Turkic and Mongol-descended peoples north and west of Afghanistan: the Uzbeks of Balkh and Bukhara, the Kalmyks, and the Aimaq and Hazara hill tribes he traces to Mongol armies. 'Tartary' is the European map name for Turkestan, which he says is not used there.
Why read it
A careful, fair-minded observer who says plainly that the word 'Tartary' is a European label, describes the lattice-and-felt tent used 'over all Tartary', and pushes back against the image of the Uzbeks as savage nomads.
Watch out
Elphinstone never visited Balkh, Bukhara or the Hazara country: everything about the Uzbeks, Aimaqs and Hazaras comes from informants. The Google scan is badly garbled on many pages, so several quotes are OCR-normalized.

CitiesBalkhc. 1809

Balkh: a great ruin with one corner lived in

Balkh, the old capital of the Uzbek-populated province south of the Oxus, is called by Asians the oldest city in the world and the 'mother of towns' (the scan is garbled here). By Elphinstone's day it had shrunk to a small inhabited corner inside a vast walled ruin, with a citadel held by the Afghan governor. The rich countryside was still watered by eighteen canals.

Its ruins still cover a great extent, and are surrounded with a wall, but only one corner is inhabited.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

BuildingsBalkhc. 1809

The round felt tent used 'over all Tartary'

Elphinstone contrasts two ways of living among the Uzbeks. Their houses, villages and towns look just like Afghan ones, but their tent is the round lattice tent covered with felt (the yurt), which he calls the kirgah and says is used across all of Tartary. On the same page he adds that it is warmer than the Afghan black tent and hardly harder to carry.

Their houses, villages, and towns, exactly resemble those of the Afghauns, but their tents are widely different The Uzbek tent is of the kind called Kirgah, which appears to be in use over all Tartary, in part of Persia, and even in part of China. It is round, and formed of lattice work … covered with black or grey felts.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

BuildingsHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

Hazara villages guarded by loop-holed towers

Most Hazaras live in villages, not tents, though some use 'Tartar tents' like the Aimaqs. Each village has a defensive tower with a kettle-drum to sound the alarm. On the page before, Elphinstone says their houses are thatched and half sunk into the hillsides.

The Hazaurehs generally live in villages of from twenty to two hundred houses, though some live in Tartar tents like the Eimauks. Each village is defended by a high tower, capable of containing ten or twelve men, and full of loop-holes.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

Daily lifeBalkhc. 1809

Kumiss: drunk all summer, never sold

Elphinstone describes how the Uzbeks make kimmiz (kumiss) by leaving mare's milk overnight in a skin and then beating and rolling it until morning. He notes that it can only be had in the last two months of summer, that people who can afford it stay drunk for much of that time, and that it is never sold. Nearby he adds that Uzbeks love horse-flesh but usually have to make do with beef.

The liquor thus made is of a whitish colour and a sourish taste: it is only to be had in plenty during the two last months of summer, and those who can afford it are generally drunk for the greater part of that period; but kimmiz is not sold, and those only can enjoy it who have mares enough to make it in the house.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

Daily lifeBukharac. 1809

Not savages: a defence of the Uzbeks

Elphinstone argues that the European picture of the Uzbeks as ferocious barbarians comes from confusing them with the Kalmyks (the garbled word in the scan) and 'other rude Tartar tribes' further north. He does not hide the slave trade in Bukhara, but on the word of Afghan and Tajik travellers he calls the Uzbeks 'as good a people as any in Asia'. The rest of the page, badly scanned, says that anyone who imagines the Uzbeks as savages will be surprised to learn that Bukhara rivals Peshawar in population, has numerous colleges and caravanserais, and tolerates all religions.

of the Uzbeks, appear to be unjust, and is probably owing partly to our confounding them with … other rude Tartar tribes between them and Russia … men are sold in Bokhaura like cattle; but in other respects, by all that I can learn, both from Afghaun travellers, and from Taujiks of Bulkh and Bokhaura, I have reason to think the Uzbeks as good a people as any in Asia.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

Daily lifeHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

Hazara wives who run the household

Elphinstone is surprised to find that among the Hazaras, a tribe he calls nearly savage, women have more standing than anywhere nearby. They manage the house and property, are consulted on everything, are never beaten and are not secluded. He also records, from hearsay, lurid claims about their morals which he admits are contradictory.

The wife manages the house, takes care of the property, does her share of the honors, and is very much consulted in all her husband's measures. Women are never beaten, and they have no concealment.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

The nameTurkestan (Uzbekistan and Xinjiang)c. 1809

'The name of Tartary is unknown in those regions'

Setting out the geography north of the Hindu Kush, Elphinstone divides the lands into Turkestan (west of the Pamirs) and 'Chinese Turkestan' (east of them), using the local name for Turk-speaking country. Then he adds a blunt note: the people who live there do not call it Tartary. The word was a European map label, not a local one.

All the country west of Beloot Taugh, and north of the Oxus, is called Toorkistaun, a term which may be extended to the east of Beloot Taugh, as far as there is reason to think the Turkish language is spoken … The name of Tartary is unknown in those regions.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

The nameHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

Hill people with 'Tartar' faces who say they came from the Moguls

Elphinstone is puzzled by the Aimaqs and Hazaras: they live in the heart of Afghanistan but look, speak and behave quite differently. They have no history of their own origin and speak a dialect of Persian, yet their features and a tradition point to Mongol ancestry. He goes on to cite the historian Abu'l-Fazl, who says they are remnants of a Mongol prince's army.

Their features, however, refer them at once to the Tartar stock, and a tradition declares them to be the offspring of the Moguls. They are, indeed, frequently called by the name of Moguls to this day, and they are often confounded with the Moguls and Chagatyes, who still reside in the neighbourhood of Heraut.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

The nameBukharaearly 1500s (reported c. 1809)

The Uzbeks sweep south and take Bukhara

Elphinstone explains who the Uzbeks were: a people who crossed the Jaxartes (Syr Darya) around 1500 and drove the heirs of Tamerlane out of Bukhara, Khwarazm and Ferghana. The next sentence is badly damaged in the scan, but it seems to say that the Uzbeks belong to the great division of mankind known in Asia as Turks, who together with the Moguls (and a third people whose name is illegible) 'compose what we call the Tartar nation'. So for him 'Tartar' is a European umbrella word for Turks and Mongols together.

The Uzbeks first crossed the Jaxartes about the beginning of the sixteenth century, and pouring on the possessions of the descendants of Tamerlane, soon drove them from Bokhaura, Khoarizm, and Ferghauna, and spread terror and dismay to the remotest parts of their extended empire.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

Strange taleswestern Afghanistan (Aimaq country)c. 1809

Victims' blood smeared on faces and beards

The Aimaqs are quiet under their chiefs, Elphinstone says, but savage in war. He reports throwing prisoners off cliffs and shooting them with arrows, and one occasion, told him by a man who was there, when they drank their victims' blood. A footnote (garbled) compares this to the Mongols' treatment of prisoners.

I have authentic accounts of their throwing their prisoners from precipices and shooting them to death with arrows; and on an occasion at which a … with whom I have conversed assisted, they actually drank the warm blood of their victims, and rubbed it over their faces and beards.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (1815)

About Aimaqs

Keep in mind

An atrocity story from a single informant, the kind of tale told about enemies; Elphinstone could not check it.

Strange talesHazarajat (central Afghanistan)c. 1809

The evil eye that eats a man's liver

In a footnote Elphinstone records an Afghan belief that some Hazaras have a power of 'fascination': reading the damaged scan, they can eat out the liver of anyone they stare at. He calls it a fable, common in India and Persia and pinned on various tribes. It is a good example of a travelling rumour attached to a people seen as outsiders.

who … the liver of any person on whom they fix their eyes. This fable is very common in India and Persia, and is attributed to various tribes.

Mountstuart Elphinstone (footnote, 1815)
