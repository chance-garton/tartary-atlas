Northern Asia1559 (Ramusio's commentary)

Prester John between Gog and Magog on the old sea charts

Ramusio notes that Marco Polo called the northern lord Uncan 'Prester John' and put his seat in two regions, Og and Magog. He adds that on all the sea charts made 200 and 300 years earlier, Prester John is placed in the north above India, between the Ganges and the Indus, with no mention of an Ethiopian Prester John; on the next page he calls these 'the provinces of Gog and Magog, that is, of the Tartars'.

where Messer Marco Polo says that in the north there was a great lord called Uncan, whose name some take to mean Prester John in our language, and that his principal seat was in two regions, Og and Magog: it should be known that on all those sea charts one sees today, made 200 and 300 years ago, this Prester John is placed in the north and above India

Mongolia1840s

Royal tombs with poisoned children and arrow traps

Huc passes on a lurid report about Tartar kings' burials: a brick building with statues, a central cavern full of treasure, children killed with mercury to keep their faces fresh and set up around the corpse holding the king's pipe and snuff, and a spring-loaded battery of bows to shoot any grave robber. He says bow-makers sold such traps and that Chinese sometimes bought them to guard their houses.

These monstrous interments sometimes cost the lives of a great number of slaves. They take children of both sexes, remarkable for their beauty, and make them swallow mercury till they are suffocated; in this way they preserve, they say, the freshness and ruddiness of their countenance, so as to make them appear still alive.

Who Mongol princes (reported)

Legend or rumourÉvariste Huc (translated by William Hazlitt, 1852)

Keep in mind. Huc did not see this; 'they say' marks it as hearsay, and it echoes older Chinese tomb legends.

Tomsk16th century legend; reported 1721

The 'piebald horde' of Siberia

A 'piebald horde' was famous in 16th-century Siberia, and some doubted it existed because people thought its members were themselves spotted. Klaproth says the name only described their horses, and quotes the explorer Messerschmidt's diary: in 1721 the governor of Tomsk still claimed the Kistym Tatars had been piebald people who slowly 'degenerated' into white ones.

the piebald horde, … whose existence has often been doubted because people believed that the men who made it up were themselves piebald, whereas it concerned only their horses. … the voevoda of Tomsk asserted 'that the Kistym Tartars were called the piebald horde (pegaya or pestraya orda), that they had been piebald, but had gradually degenerated by becoming white.'

Siberia1783 (written by 1800)

Heaps of monster bones across Tartary and Siberia

Discussing why lions are carved on Tibetan buildings though none live nearby, Turner remarks that if lions ever lived there wild, it was in the age of the huge animals whose bones lie in heaps in Tartary and Siberia. He takes the bones (mammoths) as proof of a great convulsion of the globe. This is Turner's own speculation, a common idea in his day.

it must have been at the same time with those vast monsters, whose bones are found in huge heaps in various parts of Tartary and Siberia at this day, and clearly point to some great convulsion, and change, in the order of our globe.

Who (none: fossil animals)

Read itSamuel Turner (1800)

Keep in mind. Turner's own inference from reports of mammoth bones; he did not see them.

Mountains beyond the Caspian (Caucasus in the legend)legendary past

The hare, the owl and the fortress of wind

Riccoldo's origin tale: the Tartars lived shut in by mountains whose only pass held an empty fortress that roared with the noise of horses, men and trumpets, a trick of the wind. A hunter chasing a hare followed it in, saw an owl singing on the gate, reasoned that no one lives where a hare runs and an owl sings, and led his people out. So, Riccoldo says, Tartars honour the hare and paint it on arms and tents, and nobles wear owl feathers and owl skins, which Western merchants now sell them for up to twenty pounds each.

'There is no dwelling of men where a hare flees and an owl sings.' And so, entering confidently, he found no one.

Legend or rumourRiccoldo of Monte Croce, Liber peregrinationis, ed. J. C. M. Laurent (Leipzig, 1864)

Keep in mind. A legend Riccoldo heard; Hayton of Corycus (W229) tells a different owl story in which the bird saves Genghis Khan in a bush.

Tumenevka, Astrakhan regionOctober 1829

Kalmyk musicians play Rossini at a steppe banquet

At dinner with the Kalmyk prince, attended also by Khan Jangir of the Inner (Bukey) Kazakh Horde, the food was cooked by a Russian chef and champagne was served. A band of Kalmyks led by a Russian bandmaster played overtures, including Rossini, marches and dance music, and Rose found it a remarkable sight to see Kalmyk musicians handle European instruments so skilfully.

…a band of Kalmyks, led by a Russian bandmaster, performed overtures by … and Rossini, as well as marches and dance music, with great skill.

Camp of the Kalmyk taisha Ablai1650 to 1651

Yermak's armour and the Tatars' vow of silence

In 1651 the Tobolsk officer Ulian Remezov took Yermak's famous mail coat, reclaimed from Tatar princes, as a gift to the Kalmyk ruler Ablai, who kissed it and raised it to his head. Ablai then told his own version of Yermak's death: drowned at the ford, found and shot at, his blood flowing, his armour divided up, miracles from his armour and clothes, and a Tatar oath 'on pain of death' not to tell the Russians about him. Ablai said that as a sick child he was given earth from Yermak's grave to drink and was cured.

And Ablai began to tell stories about him, according to his own history: how he came to Siberia, and fled from Kuchum at the ford, and drowned, and was found, and was shot at, and blood flowed, and they divided up his armour and carried it off, and how there were miracles from the armour and the clothing, and how the Tatars made a vow on pain of death not to tell the Russians about him

Original wording

И нача Аблай повѣсти дѣяти о немъ по своей исторіи, какъ пріѣхалъ въ Сибирь, и отъ Кучюма на перекопѣ побѣжа, и утопе, и обрѣтенъ, и стрѣлянъ, и кровь течаше, и пансыри раздѣлиша и развезоша, и какъ отъ пансырей и отъ платья чюдесъ было, и какъ Татара смертной завѣтъ положиша, что про него Русакамъ невѣщати

Central Asia1843

Flood geology and the 'Plateau of Tartary'

Humboldt explains how a geographic guess became a myth. If the highest lands had been the first to emerge from the waters, they must also have received the first seeds of civilisation; 'diluvial' geology built on ancient myths encouraged this, and so the vaguely named Plateau of Tartary gained a 'moral interest'. Travel, measurement and the study of Asian languages, he says, have corrected these exaggerations.

The high regions that had been the first to be left by the waters must also have been the first to receive the seeds of civilisation. Systems of diluvial geology, founded on ancient myths and local traditions, favoured these views. … gave this great massif, this continuous plateau vaguely called the Plateau of Tartary, a moral interest and a special importance.

Original wording

Les hautes régions qui les premières avaient été abandonnées par les eaux, devaient aussi avoir reçu les premières des germes de civilisation. Des systèmes de géologie diluvienne, fondés sur des mythes anciens et sur des traditions locales, favorisaient ces aperçus. … donnait à ce grand massif, à ce plateau continu, vaguement appelé Plateau de la Tartarie, un intérêt moral et une importance particulière.

Who European scholars of the 18th century

Hulun Lake (Dalai Nor), Inner Mongoliareported 1675

A great stone ghost city near Lake Dalai

Describing Lake Dalai (Hulun Nur) from what the local peoples told him, Spafarii reports a great stone city a day's ride from the lake, 'with every kind of building', its stone halls still whole. Some said its people all died of plague and left their goods behind, so that no one dared enter and people only looked at it from afar; others said war had emptied it. On the same page he repeats that the lake is so deep it holds whales and seals.

A day's journey from the Dalai there is a great stone city with every kind of building, and the stone halls in it are whole; and they say that in that city the people all perished of plague, and all their goods remained there; and the natives dare not go into that city, but look at it all from afar; and others say that it was laid waste by war.

Original wording

Отъ Далая днище есть городъ великой каменной со всякимъ строеніемъ, и палаты въ немъ каменныя цѣлы; и говорятъ, что въ томъ городѣ отъ мору люди всѣ пропали, и осталось имѣніе ихъ все тамъ; и иноземцы въ тотъ городъ иттить не смѣютъ, а смотрятъ все издали; а иные говорятъ, что отъ войны запустѣло.

Who Mongols nomadising around the lake; the city's builders are not named

Carpathian Mountains1285 to 1286 (entered under 6790)

Telebuga lost in the mountains: a hundred thousand dead

Returning from a raid on Hungary, Nogai went by Brasov, while Telebuga tried to cross the mountains. A three-day crossing became fifty days of wandering. Famine set in, the soldiers began to eat people and then to die, and 'eyewitnesses said' a hundred thousand perished. Telebuga came out on foot with his wife and a single mare. The chronicler presents it as God's punishment.

Telebuga went across the Mountain; what should have been crossed in three days, he wandered for 50 days lost in the mountains, led by the wrath of God. And there was a great famine among them; and they began to eat people, and then began themselves to die, and a countless multitude of them died; eyewitnesses said this: the dead were a hundred thousand.

Original wording
